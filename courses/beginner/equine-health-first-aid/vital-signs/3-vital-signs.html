Equine Vital Signs

Each vital sign tells you something specific about how a horse's body is working. Use the tabs below to explore each one - what it measures, how to check it, and what normal looks like.

Six Signs, Six Stories

No single vital sign gives you the full picture on its own. Temperature tells you about infection or heat stress; heart rate reflects stress, pain, or cardiovascular status; gut sounds point to digestive activity. Together, these six measurements give a well-rounded snapshot of how a horse is doing. Use the tabs below to study each one.

Temperature is one of the most informative vital signs. A reading that is too high usually signals infection, heat stress, or inflammation. A reading that falls below the normal range is less common but equally important to catch.

TEMP
Normal Range - Adult Horse at Rest
99.5-101.5 degrees F  /  37.5-38.6 degrees C

How to Check Temperature

1
Use a digital rectal thermometer. Lubricate the tip with petroleum jelly or a water-based lubricant before inserting.
2
Stand to the side of the horse's hindquarters - never directly behind. Gently lift the tail and insert the thermometer into the rectum at a slight angle.
3
Hold the thermometer steady and wait for the beep. Most digital thermometers read within 30-60 seconds.
4
Remove, read, and clean the thermometer. Always wait at least 30 minutes after exercise before taking a resting temperature.

What Abnormal Readings Mean

Above 101.5 degrees FA temperature above 102 degrees F is generally considered a fever. Possible causes include infection, illness, or heat stress. Contact your veterinarian.
Below 99 degrees FA subnormal temperature can indicate shock, hypothermia, or serious systemic illness. This warrants immediate veterinary attention.

Heart rate reflects how hard the cardiovascular system is working. A resting heart rate that is elevated - especially when combined with other symptoms - is one of the most important indicators of pain, stress, or illness in a horse.

HR
Normal Range - Adult Horse at Rest
28-44 beats per minute

How to Check Heart Rate & Pulse

1
With a stethoscope: Place the chest piece just behind the horse's left elbow on the chest wall. Listen for the heartbeat. Each "lub-dub" counts as one beat.
2
By pulse: Find the facial artery by running your fingers along the inside of the lower jaw until you feel a firm, cord-like structure. Press gently to feel the pulse.
3
Count the beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by four to get beats per minute. Or count for 30 seconds and multiply by two.
4
Always take the measurement while the horse is calm and has been resting for several minutes. Fear, excitement, and recent exercise all raise heart rate temporarily.

What Abnormal Readings Mean

Above 44 bpm at restAn elevated resting heart rate often indicates pain, fever, stress, dehydration, or serious illness. A rate consistently above 60 bpm at rest warrants prompt veterinary attention.
Below 28 bpm at restA very low resting heart rate (bradycardia) is uncommon in healthy horses and can indicate a cardiac irregularity. Consult your veterinarian if consistently low.

Foals and young horses: Foals have much higher heart rates than adults - up to 80-120 bpm at birth, gradually slowing over the first months of life. Always compare readings against age-appropriate ranges.

Respiratory rate tells you how often a horse is breathing. At rest, a healthy horse breathes slowly and easily. Rapid, labored, or noisy breathing is a clear signal that something needs attention.

RESP
Normal Range - Adult Horse at Rest
8-16 breaths per minute

How to Check Respiratory Rate

1
Stand back and observe the horse from the side. Watch the flank (the area between the last rib and hindquarters) for movement.
2
Each rise and fall of the flank counts as one complete breath. Count the number of breaths over 30 seconds and multiply by two to get breaths per minute.
3
In cold weather, you can watch for puffs of air from the nostrils instead. Alternatively, hold your hand near the nostril to feel air movement.
4
Take the measurement when the horse is calm and standing still. Sniffing, whinnying, or moving will affect the count.

What Abnormal Readings Mean

Above 16 breaths/min at restElevated respiratory rate can signal respiratory infection, pain, fever, anxiety, or heat stress. Labored, noisy, or flared-nostril breathing requires veterinary evaluation.
Below 8 breaths/min at restA very slow respiratory rate at rest is uncommon. It can occur with certain sedatives or serious neurological conditions and should be reported to a vet.

A horse's digestive tract is almost always making noise - gurgling, bubbling, or rumbling sounds that confirm the gut is moving food through normally. Silence is one of the earliest and most important warning signs of colic.

GUT
Normal Finding
Sounds present in all four quadrants

How to Check Gut Sounds

1
You will need a stethoscope. Press the chest piece flat against the horse's abdomen - not the earpieces in your ears yet. Place them in gently before pressing the stethoscope to the horse.
2
Check four quadrants: upper left flank, lower left flank, upper right flank, lower right flank. This ensures you check both sides of the gut above and below.
3
Listen at each quadrant for at least 60 seconds before deciding sounds are absent. Some sounds are intermittent and you may need to wait a moment.
4
Normal sounds are described as gurgling, bubbling, or rumbling - similar to stomach sounds in people. Note whether sounds are present, faint, hyperactive, or absent in each quadrant.

What Abnormal Findings Mean

Absent or very quiet soundsSilence in one or more quadrants is a significant concern. It may indicate reduced gut motility, impaction, or the early stages of colic. Contact your veterinarian.
Excessively loud or rushing soundsVery loud, rapid gut sounds can indicate hypermotility - the gut moving faster than normal. This may accompany diarrhea, spasmodic colic, or early fermentation disturbances.

The skin tenting test is a simple, equipment-free way to get a quick read on a horse's hydration status. It takes only seconds but can tell you a lot - especially alongside other vital signs.

SKIN
Normal Result
Skin returns flat within 1-2 seconds

How to Perform the Skin Tenting Test

1
Find the skin on the side of the neck or the point of the shoulder - areas where the skin is loose enough to pinch easily.
2
Pinch a fold of skin firmly between your thumb and forefinger. Hold it for one to two seconds, then release cleanly.
3
Watch how quickly the skin returns to its flat, normal position. Start counting as soon as you release.
4
In a well-hydrated horse, the skin snaps back almost immediately - within one to two seconds. A tent that lingers longer than that suggests dehydration.

Interpreting the Results

2-4 seconds: Mild dehydrationThe skin is noticeably slower to return. The horse may need water and electrolytes. Monitor closely and consult your vet if other signs are present.
4+ seconds: Moderate to severe dehydrationA prolonged tent is a serious finding. At this level, veterinary care is needed. Do not rely on water alone to resolve significant dehydration.

Keep in mind: Older horses and horses with naturally loose skin may show a slightly slower return even when well-hydrated. Always use the skin tenting test as one piece of the picture, not on its own.

The gums - also called mucous membranes - are one of the quickest windows into a horse's circulatory and hydration status. Color and moisture together tell you a great deal, and the capillary refill time (CRT) test adds another layer of information.

GUMS
Normal Finding
Pink, moist & slippery - CRT under 2 seconds

How to Check Gum Color & Moisture

1
Gently lift the horse's upper lip to expose the gums above the front teeth. Note the color - it should be a salmon pink, similar in shade to bubble gum.
2
Run a finger along the gum surface. It should feel slick and moist. Tacky or dry gums suggest dehydration.
3
Capillary Refill Time (CRT): Press your thumb firmly against the upper gum for about two seconds, then release. The area you pressed will briefly turn white.
4
Count the seconds until the pink color fully returns to that spot. A healthy horse refills in one to two seconds.

Gum Color Guide

Pink - NormalHealthy circulation and hydration. The baseline you are aiming for.
Pale / WhiteMay indicate anemia, shock, or internal blood loss. Requires prompt veterinary attention.
Tacky or DryA sign of dehydration. Check skin tenting and heart rate alongside this finding.
Bright Red or Brick RedCan signal toxemia, systemic infection, or severe illness. Urgent veterinary care needed.
Blue or PurpleIndicates severe oxygen deprivation or circulatory failure. A true emergency - contact a vet immediately.
Yellow (Jaundiced)May point to liver disease or certain types of anemia. Not an emergency on its own, but warrants veterinary evaluation.

CRT over 2 seconds is considered prolonged and is a significant finding even if other signs seem normal. Report it to your veterinarian along with other vitals.
