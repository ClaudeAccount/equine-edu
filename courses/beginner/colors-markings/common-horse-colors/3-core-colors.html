Common Horse Colors

Core Colors

Chestnut, bay, and black form the genetic foundation of nearly every horse coat color. Learning these first makes every other color easier to understand and identify.

The Foundation Colors

Every horse coat color is built on a genetic base of either red pigment or black pigment - or a combination of both. The colors covered in this lesson - Chestnut, Sorrel, Liver Chestnut, Black, and Bay - are the starting point for understanding all other horse colors. Dilute genes, modifiers, and patterns always work on top of one of these base coats.

These colors are produced primarily by red pigment (phaeomelanin). They range from bright copper to deep reddish-brown - and the mane, tail, and legs stay within the same color family as the body.

Red Pigment

Chestnut

A warm reddish-brown body coat with a mane and tail that are the same shade or lighter - never black. One of the most common horse colors in the world.

Red Pigment

Sorrel

A lighter, brighter copper-red coat with a flaxen or matching mane and tail. Common in stock breeds. Often used interchangeably with chestnut, though sorrel typically refers to a lighter, more golden-red tone.

Red Pigment

Liver Chestnut

A very dark, deep reddish-brown coat - almost chocolatey in appearance. The mane and tail remain within the same dark brown family, without any black. The darkest variation of the chestnut color.

Telling Them Apart: All three are chestnut-based - the key is shade. Sorrel is the lightest and most copper-toned. Standard chestnut sits in the middle. Liver chestnut is the darkest, appearing nearly chocolate. In all three, the mane and tail never contain black hairs.

These colors are produced by black pigment (eumelanin) - either alone or in combination with red. The defining characteristic is the presence of true black points (mane, tail, lower legs).

Black Pigment

Black

A true black horse has a solid black body coat, black mane, black tail, and black legs with no reddish or brown tones. True black horses are less common than they appear - many horses described as black are actually very dark bay or brown.

Red + Black Pigment

Bay

A reddish-brown body coat with a black mane, tail, and lower legs - called black points. The body can range from light tan to dark reddish-brown, but the black points are always present and are the defining feature of bay.

Black vs. Bay: The key difference is the points. A black horse has a black body and black points. A bay horse has a reddish-brown body with black points. If the mane and tail are black but the body has any brown or reddish tones, it is bay - not black.
