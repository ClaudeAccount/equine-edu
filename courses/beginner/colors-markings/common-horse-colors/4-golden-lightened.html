Common Horse Colors

Golden & Lightened Colors

Palomino, buckskin, dun, and flaxen are four of the most visually striking — and most commonly confused — horse coat colors. Each is produced by a dilute gene acting on a base color.

Dilute Colors & What Creates Them

The colors in this lesson are produced when specific dilute genes act on a chestnut or bay base coat, lightening the pigment. The most important dilute gene in this group is the cream gene, which lightens red pigment to gold or yellow — and has little to no effect on black pigment.

Understanding the base color a dilute is working on is the key to correctly identifying palomino, buckskin, and dun. Flaxen is a slightly different modifier that affects only the mane and tail of chestnut horses.

💡 Key Concept

The cream gene lightens red pigment to gold — but leaves black pigment mostly unchanged. This is why palomino (chestnut + cream) has a golden body with a white mane, while buckskin (bay + cream) has a golden body but keeps its black mane, tail, and legs.

Cream-Diluted Colors

Both palomino and buckskin result from one copy of the cream gene — but they start from different base colors.

Chestnut + Cream Gene

Palomino

A golden body coat — ranging from pale cream-gold to rich honey gold — with a white or very light mane and tail. Produced when one cream gene acts on a chestnut base. The skin is typically pale and the eyes are dark.

Bay + Cream Gene

Buckskin

A golden or tan body coat with a black mane, tail, and lower legs (black points). Produced when one cream gene acts on a bay base. The black points are the key feature that separates buckskin from palomino.

Palomino vs. Buckskin: Both are golden — but look at the mane, tail, and legs. White or cream mane and tail = palomino. Black mane, tail, and legs = buckskin. The presence of black points is the single clearest distinction.

Dun & Flaxen

These two colors each involve a different genetic mechanism — and are easy to confuse with other colors if you don't know what to look for.

Dun Gene

Dun

A diluted body coat — tan, sandy gold, or mouse-grey depending on the base color — with a distinctive dorsal stripe (a dark line running from mane to tail along the spine). May also show leg barring (zebra stripes on legs). The dorsal stripe is the defining feature of a dun.

Flaxen Modifier

Flaxen

A chestnut horse with a noticeably lighter — flaxen, cream, or white — mane and tail while the body coat stays chestnut. Flaxen is not a separate color but a modifier that affects only mane and tail pigment on chestnut horses.

Dun vs. Palomino / Buckskin: Dun horses show primitive markings — most importantly a dorsal stripe down the back. Palomino and buckskin do not have dorsal stripes. If you see a golden horse with a dark line along the spine, it is dun — not palomino or buckskin.
