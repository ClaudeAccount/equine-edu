Common Horse Colors

Understanding Horse Color

Before you can confidently identify colors, it helps to understand what produces them. Every horse coat color comes from just two pigments — and a handful of genes that modify, dilute, or pattern them.

The Building Blocks of Horse Color

Horse coat color can seem endlessly varied — but it all starts with base coat colors. Every color is produced by the presence, absence, or modification of just two pigments, controlled by genetics. Understanding these basics won't just help you identify colors — it will help you understand why two horses can look so different, and predict what to look for when making an identification.

The Two Pigments

All horse coat color begins here. Every shade, dilution, and pattern you'll encounter is built on these two pigments.

Red Pigment
Phaeomelanin

Produces all red, orange, and yellow tones in the horse's coat. When present alone with no black, the horse is a shade of chestnut.

Produces → Chestnut · Sorrel · Liver Chestnut · (base for Palomino & Dun)
Black Pigment
Eumelanin

Produces all black, brown, and dark tones. Can appear alone (black horse) or combined with red (bay), and is affected by dilute and modifier genes.

Produces → Black · Bay · (base for Buckskin · Blue Roan)

💡 Key Takeaway

If a horse has red pigment only — it is some shade of chestnut. If it has black pigment only — it is black. If it has both — the base is bay. Every other color you'll learn is a modification of one of these three starting points.

How Genes Modify Color

Once you know the two pigments, the next step is understanding the three main types of genes that modify them.

Dilute Genes

Lighten one or both pigments, producing paler versions of the base color. The most important dilute gene is the cream gene, which lightens red pigment to gold — but leaves black mostly unchanged.

Cream gene on chestnut = Palomino  |  Cream on bay = Buckskin

Modifier Genes

Alter the expression of a base color in specific ways. The dun gene dilutes body color while leaving a distinctive dark dorsal stripe. The flaxen gene lightens only the mane and tail on chestnut horses.

Dun gene on bay = Dun  |  Flaxen on chestnut = Flaxen Chestnut

Progressive Genes

Change the coat over the horse's lifetime rather than all at once. The gray gene causes progressive depigmentation — the horse is born with a base color and lightens with each coat cycle.

Gray gene on black = Gray horse (born dark, lightens over years)

Pattern Genes

Create large-scale distribution patterns of color rather than altering pigment directly. The roan gene, pinto genes, and leopard complex all work this way — the base color is preserved but distributed differently.

Roan on chestnut = Strawberry Roan  |  LP gene = Appaloosa

Key Color Vocabulary

Base Color

The underlying genetic color before any modifying genes are applied. Every horse is genetically chestnut, bay, or black at its base.

Points

The mane, tail, and lower legs. In bay horses, the points are black — this is a key identification feature used throughout color recognition.

Dorsal Stripe

A dark line of hair running along the spine from mane to tail. The defining feature of dun horses — not present in palomino or buckskin.

Depigmentation

Loss of pigment from the hair. Occurs progressively in gray horses and evenly in roan horses, producing their distinctive mixed-color coats.
