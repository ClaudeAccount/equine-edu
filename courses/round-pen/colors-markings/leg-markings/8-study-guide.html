Marking Names by Height

What is the single most important factor in naming a leg marking?

Height — how far up the leg the white extends. The name changes as white rises: coronet (at the coronet band only), pastern (to the pastern), fetlock (to the fetlock), sock (to the cannon), stocking (above the cannon toward the knee or hock).

Name the leg markings in order from lowest to highest.

Coronet → Pastern → Fetlock → Sock → Stocking.

What is an ermine mark?

A dark spot found inside a white leg marking, most often near the coronet band. Ermine marks are specifically noted when present because they contribute to unique identification.

What is a partial marking, and when is that term used?

A partial marking is one where white does not wrap all the way around the leg — it covers only part of the circumference. ‘Partial coronet’ or ‘partial sock’ indicates white is present but incomplete around the leg.

Leg Identification and Anatomy Reference

Which leg reference joint is used for front legs? Which for hind legs?

Front legs use the knee as the upper reference joint. Hind legs use the hock.

Why does leg identity matter when describing markings?

Because the same marking height can appear on different legs, and accurate description always specifies which leg carries the marking: left front, right hind, etc.

What shared lower-leg areas are relevant to marking descriptions on both front and hind legs?

The coronet, pastern, and fetlock are the lower-leg areas most commonly used in marking names, and they appear on all four legs.

Scenario Spot-Checks

A horse has white that covers the lower front half of the pastern on the right front leg but does not wrap all the way around. What is this called?

Partial pastern, right front. The white does not wrap fully, so ‘partial’ is added. The specific leg is always named.

White extends from the hoof up to just below the right hind hock. What marking is this?

Stocking, right hind. White reaching that high on a hind leg qualifies as a stocking.
