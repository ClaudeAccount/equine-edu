Introduction to Tack & Equipment

The Saddle Saddle

Parts, types, and fit principles for the most important piece of riding tack.

The saddle is the primary interface between rider and horse. Its central purpose is to distribute the rider's weight evenly across a broad area of the horse's back, protecting the spine while providing the rider with a stable, balanced seat. Understanding saddle parts begins with understanding that every component serves a function — and that function has consequences for the horse's comfort and movement.

The two most important saddle fit decisions are tree width (which must match the horse's shoulder and back width) and tree angle (which must match the angle of the horse's shoulder). Get these two dimensions wrong, and no amount of padding or adjustment will produce a correct fit.

Saddle Anatomy
Part 1

The Tree

The tree is the saddle's internal frame, made of wood, fiberglass, or synthetic material. The tree determines the saddle's shape and how weight is distributed across the horse's back. A broken or ill-fitting tree cannot be corrected with padding; the saddle must be repaired or replaced.

Part 2

The Seat

The seat is where the rider sits. Depth, width, and twist affect rider position and balance. A seat that is too large allows the rider to slide backward; one too small pushes the rider forward and restricts movement. Correct seat sizing is as important for the rider as tree width is for the horse.

Part 3

Western vs. English Design

Western saddles are built for extended work — their larger surface area distributes weight more broadly, and the horn provides a grip point for roping. English saddles are lighter and closer-contact, designed to maximize the rider's ability to feel the horse's movement. Both designs require proper fit to the individual horse.

Part 4

Saddle Fit Indicators

A correctly fitting saddle sits level on the horse's back without pressing on the spine. Two fingers should fit between the pommel and the withers. The panels should make even contact along the horse's back, and the sweat pattern after a ride should be even — not absent at the front or back, which indicates bridging.

Saddle fit must be evaluated for the horse — not just the rider. A saddle that fits a rider perfectly but bridges on the horse causes localized pressure that leads to muscle atrophy, back pain, and behavior problems under saddle. Dry spots in the sweat pattern after a ride are a reliable sign that something is wrong.

Most saddle-related behavior problems — tail wringing when saddled, reluctance to go forward, bucking, or sensitivity when the girth is tightened — have saddle fit as a contributing or primary cause. Before assuming a behavioral cause, saddle fit should be evaluated. A qualified saddle fitter can assess fit systematically using pressure mapping and visual inspection.

Things to Remember

  • The tree is the internal frame of the saddle — it determines shape, size, and fit. Everything else attaches to the tree.
  • The pommel is the front raised arch; the cantle is the rear raised arch. The seat sits between them.
  • Western saddles have a deeper seat and a horn; English saddles have a flatter seat with no horn.
  • Saddle fit is not just a comfort issue — a poorly fitted saddle causes back pain, muscle damage, and behavioral resistance.
