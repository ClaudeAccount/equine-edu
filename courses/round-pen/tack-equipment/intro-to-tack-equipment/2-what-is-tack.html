Introduction to Tack & Equipment

What Is Tack?

An introduction to the collective term for horse equipment: its definition, purpose, and main categories.

Defining Tack

Tack is the collective term for all equipment placed on a horse to enable riding, driving, or handling. The word originates from an old English term for equipment and has described horse gear since the era of working horses. Understanding tack begins not with the names of individual parts, but with purpose: tack creates the physical connection between horse and handler, allowing communication, control, and shared work.

Every piece of tack exists to solve a specific problem — how to sit on a horse's back without causing injury, how to communicate direction without speaking, how to move a horse safely from one place to another. Understanding that purpose makes identifying and fitting equipment far more intuitive.

💡 Did You Know

The word “tack” is short for “tackle,” a general term for equipment associated with a particular activity. Its use in equestrian contexts dates to at least the 17th century. A well-maintained saddle can last 20–30 years or more — some antique saddles in use today are over a century old.

Core Categories

Four Categories of Horse Equipment

Category 1

Riding Tack

The saddle and bridle are the two foundational pieces of riding tack. The saddle distributes the rider's weight across the horse's back; the bridle provides directional communication through the horse's mouth or nose. All other riding equipment supports or refines these two functions.

Category 2

Handling Equipment

Halters and lead ropes form the foundation of daily horse handling. A halter fits over the horse's head and provides a safe attachment point; a lead rope connects to the halter's ring for leading, tying, and ground work. No barn interaction is possible without basic handling equipment.

Category 3

Driving Tack

Horses driven rather than ridden wear a harness in place of a saddle and bridle. Harness systems distribute pulling power across the horse's chest and shoulders and transmit directional cues through long lines to the driver. Driving tack is distinct from riding tack in design and function.

Category 4

Protective Equipment

Boots, bandages, and blankets protect the horse before, during, and after work. These items are often grouped with tack in a tack room, though they serve a protective rather than communicative function. Proper use of protective equipment reduces the risk of injury during training and turnout.

The most important principle in tack: fit matters as much as function. Equipment that does not fit correctly causes pain, behavioral resistance, and injury — even if every part is correctly identified and placed.

Why Tack Fit Is a Safety Issue

Tack communicates through pressure — pressure on the back from a saddle, pressure on the mouth or nose from a bridle. Poorly fitted tack applies that pressure in unintended places, creating chronic discomfort that affects the horse's behavior, willingness, and long-term soundness.

A saddle that bridges — meaning it contacts only at the front and back instead of distributing weight evenly — concentrates pressure on two small areas. A bridle adjusted too tightly causes constant mouth irritation. Recognizing correct fit is one of the most practical skills in basic horse care, and it begins with knowing what each piece is designed to do.

Things to Remember

  • Tack is any equipment placed on a horse to enable riding, driving, or safe handling — the saddle, bridle, halter, and all accessories count.
  • Riding tack (saddle, bridle) is designed for mounted work; handling tack (halter, lead rope) is for ground control.
  • Protective equipment like boots and wraps is still tack — it's worn by the horse to prevent injury during work.
  • Western and English tack serve the same functions but differ in design, weight, and style based on their riding disciplines.
