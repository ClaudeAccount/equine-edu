The Horse's Gaits

Canter, Lope, and Gallop

The canter and gallop are the two faster natural gaits. The canter is a controlled three-beat gait used across disciplines; the gallop is the horse's fastest natural movement, with four beats and a full moment of suspension.

The Faster Gaits

As speed increases beyond the trot, the horse shifts into the canter. The canter is used in almost every riding discipline for arena work, transitions, and conditioning. The gallop is reserved for situations requiring maximum speed — racing, cross-country jumping, and open-field riding. Understanding how each gait is structured helps riders and handlers recognize correct movement and potential issues.

💡 Did You Know

The fastest recorded gallop speed for a horse is approximately 88 km/h (55 mph), achieved by a Thoroughbred named Winning Brew over a short distance in 2008. At top speed, a galloping horse’s stride can cover over 8 meters per step.

The canter is a three-beat gait with a rocking, rolling feel. It is the most commonly ridden of the faster gaits and appears across English, Western, and western performance disciplines.

The Canter: Three-Beat Gait

At the canter, the horse moves in a three-beat sequence followed by a brief moment of suspension when all four feet are off the ground. On a right lead canter — the footfall pattern used when tracking right or circling right — the sequence is: left hind, then right hind and left fore together as one beat, then right fore (the leading foreleg), followed by suspension.

The leading leg, or lead, is the foreleg that reaches furthest forward and appears to "lead" the stride. On a right lead, the right foreleg leads. On a left lead, the left foreleg leads. Using the correct lead helps the horse stay balanced through turns and circles — a horse cantering a right circle on the left lead is said to be on the wrong lead and will feel unbalanced and stiff through turns.

The lope is the Western term for the canter. The footfall pattern is identical, but the lope is ridden with less collection and impulsion, producing a slower, quieter stride that suits Western style and longer rides. Both canter and lope average roughly 10 to 17 miles per hour in a working frame, though speed varies by training level and rider intent.

Key Fact: The canter is a three-beat gait followed by suspension. The lead identifies which foreleg reaches furthest forward — and the correct lead matters for balance on curved lines.

The gallop is the horse's fastest natural gait and differs from the canter in both footfall pattern and speed. It is rarely used in arena work but is fundamental to racing, cross-country, and open riding.

The Gallop: Four-Beat Gait

At the gallop, the diagonal pair that moves together at the canter splits apart, creating a four-beat pattern instead of three. The sequence on a right lead gallop is: left hind, right hind, left fore, right fore — followed by a longer and more pronounced moment of suspension than at the canter. This extended suspension is what gives the gallop its ground-covering appearance.

The gallop is the fastest of the four natural gaits. A fit riding horse can gallop at 25 to 30 miles per hour in sustained work. Thoroughbreds bred for racing have reached speeds exceeding 40 miles per hour over short distances, though this is exceptional rather than typical. The average trail or competition horse works well below those speeds in everyday settings.

At the gallop, the rider moves into a two-point or forward seat — rising out of the saddle and shifting weight into the stirrups — to reduce weight on the horse's back and allow freer movement. This position is familiar in racing, cross-country, and any situation where sustained speed or forward impulsion is needed.

Key Fact: The gallop is a four-beat gait, not three. The diagonal pair that acts as a single beat in the canter splits into two separate beats at the gallop.

At both the canter and the gallop, the horse takes a left lead or a right lead. Leads affect balance, comfort, and correctness — especially on curved lines and through turns.

Understanding Leads

A lead refers to which foreleg reaches furthest forward during the stride. On a left lead, the left foreleg leads. On a right lead, the right foreleg leads. When a horse is circling or tracking in one direction, the inside lead — the lead matching the direction of travel — helps the horse stay balanced through the turn. A horse on the outside or wrong lead will feel stiff, unbalanced, or resistant through curved lines.

Leads are checked by glancing down at the horse's shoulder. The leading shoulder swings forward further than the other. Riders working at the canter develop the feel for leads over time, but visual checks at the shoulder remain a reliable method for new riders and handlers working from the ground.

A flying lead change occurs when a horse switches leads mid-stride during the suspension phase, without breaking back to the trot or walk. Simple lead changes, where the horse is brought back to the trot or walk before picking up the new lead, are more commonly taught to beginning and intermediate riders before flying changes are introduced.

Key Fact: The correct lead matches the direction of travel — right lead when turning right, left lead when turning left. An incorrect lead is called a wrong lead or counter-canter.
Barn Connection: When watching horses in a paddock or during free exercise, the gallop is easy to recognize by its speed and the longer, reaching stride. Watching the suspension phase — that split second when all four feet are off the ground — is one of the most striking aspects of equine movement at speed.

Things to Remember

  • The canter is a three-beat gait followed by suspension. The lope is the Western term for the canter.
  • The gallop is a four-beat gait — the diagonal pair splits apart — with a longer suspension phase.
  • A lead identifies which foreleg reaches furthest forward. The correct lead matches the direction of travel.
  • A flying lead change happens mid-stride during suspension; a simple change goes through trot or walk first.
