The Horse's Gaits

Walk and Trot

The walk and trot are the two slowest natural gaits. Each has a distinct footfall pattern, number of beats, and feel — and each plays an essential role in training, warm-up, and daily riding.

The Two Foundation Gaits

Most riding sessions begin and end at the walk. The trot follows as the primary working gait in many disciplines, forming the basis for athletic conditioning, balance work, and early rider development. Understanding these two gaits in detail provides the framework for reading everything that comes next.

💡 Did You Know

The trot is described as a two-beat diagonal gait, but the brief moment when all four feet are off the ground — called the moment of suspension — is what makes a trot feel smooth or rough. Horses with a longer moment of suspension are described as having more “cadence,” and this quality is one of the things judges assess in dressage.

The walk is the slowest of the four natural gaits and the only one in which at least two feet remain on the ground at all times. Its steady, four-beat rhythm makes it the foundation of every training session.

The Walk: Four-Beat Gait

In a correct walk, the horse moves each leg individually in a specific order, creating four distinct hoof beats per stride cycle. The standard sequence is left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore — often written as LH, LF, RH, RF. This creates an even, rhythmic four-beat pattern with no moment when all four feet are off the ground.

Because at least two feet are always in contact with the ground, the walk is considered a gait without suspension. This makes it the most stable gait for the horse and the easiest for a new rider to sit. An average pleasure walk covers roughly 4 miles per hour, though collected and extended versions vary noticeably from that baseline.

The walk also reveals a great deal about a horse's health and balance. A clear, even four-beat rhythm with good overtrack — where the hind hoof lands in front of or on the print left by the fore hoof — indicates an active, forward walk. A pace-like or lateral walk, where same-side legs move in pairs, suggests tension, training issues, or physical discomfort.

Key Fact: The walk is a four-beat gait with no moment of suspension. At least two hooves remain on the ground throughout the stride cycle.

The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait and the primary working gait for many English and Western disciplines. Its consistent rhythm makes it ideal for conditioning, balance development, and transitions.

The Trot: Two-Beat Diagonal Gait

At the trot, the horse moves diagonal pairs of legs simultaneously — the left fore and right hind move together, then the right fore and left hind. Between these two beats is a moment of suspension, where all four feet are briefly off the ground. This suspension gives the trot its characteristic springy, bouncing quality.

The trot averages roughly 8 to 12 miles per hour in a working pace, though speed varies widely by breed, training, and whether the horse is collected, working, or extended. A slower, relaxed version of the trot often used in Western disciplines is called the jog. The jog has the same two-beat diagonal footfall pattern as the trot but is ridden with less impulsion and a quieter rhythm.

Riders absorb the trot's movement in two ways. In a sitting trot, the rider stays in the saddle and follows the motion through a supple lower back. In a posting trot (also called a rising trot), the rider rises and sits alternately with each diagonal pair, which reduces fatigue during longer rides and helps the horse move more freely. The posting diagonal matters in lessons and shows — riders are conventionally asked to post on the outside diagonal.

Key Fact: The trot is a two-beat gait where diagonal leg pairs move together. The brief suspension between each beat creates the characteristic bounce the rider feels.
Barn Connection: When a horse is observed trotting on a longe line, handlers watch for even rhythm, clear two-beat footfall, and consistent suspension. Any unevenness — shortened stride on one diagonal or reduced suspension — may indicate soreness and warrants a closer look.

Things to Remember

  • The walk is a four-beat gait — LH, LF, RH, RF — with no suspension.
  • The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait with a moment of suspension between each beat.
  • The jog is the Western version of the trot, ridden with less impulsion and a quieter rhythm.
  • Posting trot alternates rising and sitting with each diagonal to ease the bounce.
