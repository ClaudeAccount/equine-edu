Arena Figures & School Movements

Basic Figures

Circles, serpentines, and loops are the foundational figures of arena riding. Each one has a specific shape, starting point, and training purpose — and each develops something the horse needs to progress.

The Building Blocks of Arena Work

Every riding discipline that uses an arena builds on the same foundational figures. The names and sizes are consistent across English, Western, and driving disciplines, allowing riders, trainers, and judges to communicate with a shared vocabulary. Learning these figures and their purposes is the foundation of all structured riding work.

💡 Did You Know

Riding a correct 20-meter circle requires the rider to look ahead about one quarter of the circle at all times — not down at the horse, and not at the part of the circle just ahead of the horse’s nose. This forward visual focus is one of the first habits trained riding instructors develop in their students.

Circles are the most fundamental arena figure. A correctly ridden circle maintains consistent bend, size, and rhythm throughout — and exposes any imbalance in the horse or inconsistency in the rider's aids.

Circles: 20-Meter and 10-Meter

The 20-meter circle is the standard introductory circle size. In a 20x40 arena, a 20-meter circle ridden at A or C touches both long sides and the short end at the starting letter, with the far point of the circle at X. This circle size is large enough for most horses to maintain rhythm and balance while still requiring consistent bend and rider coordination to ride correctly.

The 10-meter circle is a more demanding figure that requires greater collection, sharper bend, and more precise aids. A 10-meter circle ridden at E or B fits between the center line and the long side of the arena without touching either wall. This circle is commonly used in training once the horse can maintain a relaxed, consistent 20-meter circle without drifting or falling out through the shoulder.

Common faults on circles include: the circle becoming egg-shaped (the horse drifts on one rein and falls in on the other), the size increasing gradually as the horse escapes to the outside, and the rhythm changing through the curved parts compared to the straight approach. All of these point to specific aid imbalances that targeted practice can correct.

Key Fact: A 20-meter circle at A or C in a small arena touches both long sides and reaches X at its far point. A 10-meter circle at E or B fits between the center line and the rail without touching either.

Serpentines and loops introduce the requirement to change bend while maintaining rhythm — a more complex demand than a single circle, and an excellent test of the horse's suppleness and the rider's coordination.

Serpentines

A serpentine is a series of equal-sized half-circles connected across the center line of the arena, producing an S-shaped track. The most common versions are the three-loop and four-loop serpentine. In a three-loop serpentine in a 20x40 arena, each loop is approximately 13 meters wide and crosses the center line at two points. In a 20x60 arena, a three-loop serpentine produces larger loops with each loop measuring 20 meters in width.

The key technical requirement of a serpentine is the change of bend at the center line — the horse must change from left bend to right bend (or vice versa) at the moment it crosses the center line. This change is prepared by straightening the horse for one stride, then applying the new aids for the bend in the opposite direction. A horse that cannot change bend at the center line without tension or resistance is showing a training gap the serpentine has revealed.

A single loop is a simpler version of this concept — a shallow curving line from the rail to a specified point (often the quarter line or center line) and back to the rail on the same side. Loops require less dramatic bend change than full serpentines and are a useful introduction to the concept of changing rein within a movement.

Key Fact: A serpentine requires a bend change at each center-line crossing — left bend becomes right bend. The horse is briefly straight for one stride at each crossing before the new bend begins.

Straight lines — the center line, quarter lines, and diagonals — are demanding in their own way. Maintaining a perfectly straight track tests the horse's straightness and the balance of the rider's aids from side to side.

Diagonals and the Center Line

A diagonal is a straight line crossing the arena from one long side to the other. Common diagonals in the small arena connect H to F and K to M, crossing through X. Diagonals are used for changing rein at the trot or canter, and are the track ridden in many transitions between movements. Riding a true diagonal requires the horse to travel in a perfectly straight line between two defined points — without drifting left or right — which requires equal contact and equal leg pressure from the rider.

The center line runs from A to C through the middle of the arena. It is the most demanding straight track because it is the furthest from the rail on both sides, removing the wall as a steering guide. Riding a straight center line — especially the entry at A at the start of a test — requires the horse to be truly straight, with the hind feet tracking directly behind the forefeet, not drifting to either side.

The half-circle and return to the track is a combined figure that begins at one point on the long side, rides a half-circle to a specified size, and then returns to the track on a straight diagonal line. This figure requires both curved and straight work and tests the horse's ability to adjust bend quickly while maintaining rhythm and energy.

Key Fact: Straight lines test true straightness — the horse's hind feet should follow directly behind the forefeet with no drift to either side. The center line is the most demanding straight track because the rail cannot help with steering.
Barn Connection: The quality of all arena figures depends on the quality of transitions into and out of them. A circle ridden with a rushed, unbalanced beginning and an abrupt, falling-apart exit is less useful than a circle that begins and ends on the same precise point with consistent rhythm throughout. Teaching the setup and exit of each figure is part of teaching the figure itself.

Things to Remember

  • A 20-meter circle at A or C touches both long sides and reaches X at the far point.
  • A 10-meter circle at E or B fits between the center line and the rail.
  • A serpentine requires a bend change at each center-line crossing — one stride straight, then new bend.
  • The center line is the most demanding straight track because the wall cannot help with steering.
