Natural & Artificial Aids

Natural Aids

The four natural aids are the rider's own tools of communication: leg, seat, hands, and voice. Every effective rider learns to apply these aids clearly, lightly, and in coordination with each other.

Communication Through the Body

Natural aids require no added equipment. They are always present whenever a rider is mounted. The challenge is not in having them — it is in learning to apply each one clearly and in the right combination. Most riding problems can be traced back to one natural aid being applied incorrectly, too strongly, or in conflict with another.

💡 Did You Know

An experienced rider’s aids can be so subtle that observers watching from the ground cannot detect them at all. At the highest levels of dressage, judges specifically look for this — movements that appear to occur with no visible rider input are scored higher than the same movement performed with obvious aids.

The leg is the primary forward-driving aid. It generates the energy and impulsion that powers all movement, and it also steers, supports, and bends the horse through turns and lateral work.

The Leg Aid

The rider's leg communicates through pressure against the horse's side. A closing of the leg at or near the girth asks the horse to move forward or increase energy. The same pressure applied slightly behind the girth can ask the hindquarters to move sideways — a fundamental request in lateral movements such as the leg yield or turn on the forehand.

Leg aids should be applied with the calf and lower leg, not by gripping with the knee or thigh. Gripping high creates stiffness and restricts the horse's movement. A soft, following leg contact allows the rider to feel the horse's sides moving and respond with precision.

The principle of light aids first applies strongly to the leg: a light squeeze precedes a firmer nudge, which precedes a kick. The horse is rewarded by the release of pressure — not by praise alone — and escalation should only happen when the lighter version produced no response.

Key Fact: The leg is the primary forward aid. It drives impulsion, steers the hindquarters, and supports lateral movement. Applied at the girth: forward. Applied behind the girth: sideways.

The seat is perhaps the most subtle natural aid and often the last one new riders learn to use consciously. Weight distribution and hip movement communicate energy, transitions, and direction without any pressure from leg or hand.

The Seat Aid

The rider's seat communicates through weight and movement. Sitting deeper into the saddle — engaging the core and allowing the seat bones to press down and forward — encourages the horse to slow or collect. Allowing the hips to follow freely and swinging with the horse's movement encourages forward energy. Blocking the hip swing — bracing through the lower back — can interrupt forward movement and signal a downward transition.

Weight shifts also communicate direction. Shifting weight slightly to the inside seat bone in a turn helps the horse understand the direction being requested. Combined with the leg and hand, a clear weight shift confirms the steering signal without requiring more rein pressure.

The seat is developed over time and with miles in the saddle. New riders often cannot influence the horse through seat alone because they are still developing balance and security. As balance improves, the seat becomes a powerful, quiet tool that reduces reliance on strong leg and hand aids.

Key Fact: The seat communicates through weight and hip movement. Sitting deeper encourages slowing or collection; following freely encourages forward energy. Weight shifts help signal direction.

The hands communicate through the reins and ultimately through the bit or noseband. They are used to steer, slow, soften, and shape the horse — but they are the last aid to escalate, not the first.

The Hand Aid

Rein contact connects the rider's hand to the horse's mouth through the bit. A soft, following contact — where the hand allows and absorbs the natural movement of the horse's head and neck — is the baseline. From this contact, the hand can close fingers to slow the pace, open to steer toward a direction, or soften to reward a correct response.

Direct rein (also called a leading rein) pulls toward the direction of travel and is commonly used for turning. An indirect rein (also called a neck rein in Western riding) presses against the horse's neck without crossing the mane and steers the horse away from the pressure — the basis of one-handed Western riding. Horses must be trained to understand and respond to the specific rein type being used.

Strong or constant pulling on the reins creates a horse that leans into pressure, braces through the neck, and becomes dull to rein contact over time. The goal is to use the minimum rein pressure needed, release the moment the horse responds, and keep the hands soft and following rather than fixed and pulling.

Key Fact: Hands communicate through rein contact. They should be soft and following, used to guide and soften — not to pull or hold the horse in position. Release is the reward.

The voice is the fourth natural aid — and the only one that uses sound rather than physical pressure. It is widely used from the ground, especially in longe work, and supplements the other aids when mounted.

The Voice Aid

Voice aids communicate through tone, rhythm, and specific words or sounds the horse learns over time. A clucking sound is commonly used to encourage forward energy. A long, low "whoa" — spoken calmly and drawn out — signals a downward transition or halt. A sharp or quick tone can be used to alert or correct. The word itself matters less than the tone, rhythm, and consistency with which it is used.

From the ground, the voice is often the primary aid. Handlers use it during leading, grooming, feeding, and longe work to communicate calm, ask for transitions, or reinforce other signals. Horses learn voice aids quickly when they are applied consistently and paired with clear outcomes.

Under saddle, voice aids are commonly combined with other natural aids to support and reinforce them. In formal competition, voice aids are often penalized or restricted, so riders are trained to phase them out progressively as the horse learns to respond to leg, seat, and hand alone.

Key Fact: The voice communicates through tone and rhythm, not just words. It is the most commonly used aid from the ground and is a valuable early-training tool before leg and hand signals are fully established.
Barn Connection: Natural aids work together as a system — never in isolation. A well-timed leg aid with a soft hand and a following seat creates a clear, complete message. When one aid conflicts with another — such as a driving leg pushing against a pulling hand — the horse receives two messages at once and cannot respond correctly to either.

Things to Remember

  • The four natural aids are leg, seat, hands, and voice — always present, no equipment required.
  • Leg at the girth drives forward; leg behind the girth moves the hindquarters sideways.
  • The seat communicates through weight and hip movement — deeper seat slows, following seat encourages forward.
  • Hands should be soft and following; release of pressure is the reward the horse learns from.
  • Voice communicates through tone and rhythm; often phased out as leg, seat, and hand develop.
