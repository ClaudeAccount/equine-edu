Natural & Artificial Aids

Why Aids Matter

Aids are the signals a rider uses to communicate with a horse. Clear, consistent aids are the foundation of effective, respectful horsemanship at every level.

The Language of Riding

During a riding lesson, an instructor asks a student to slow the horse from a trot to a walk. The student pulls on the reins — and the horse braces against the contact and speeds up instead. A moment later, the instructor demonstrates: a soft closing of the fingers, a slight deepening of the seat, a gentle closing of the leg. The horse softens and steps quietly back to a walk. Same request, very different language. Understanding aids explains the difference.

What an Aid Is

An aid is any signal a rider uses to communicate a request to the horse. Aids are not commands that force a horse to move — they are cues the horse learns to respond to through training and repetition. A well-trained horse understands what each aid means in context; a well-educated rider knows how to apply aids clearly, consistently, and at the right moment.

Aids are divided into two categories: natural aids and artificial aids. Natural aids are parts of the rider's own body — the legs, seat, hands, and voice. Artificial aids are equipment and tools that extend or support the natural aids when needed. Both categories serve the same purpose: clear, respectful communication between horse and rider.

💡 Did You Know

The concept of using refined leg and rein aids to communicate with horses dates back to the ancient Greek commander Xenophon, who wrote “On Horsemanship” around 350 BCE. Many of his principles about lightness and willing cooperation are still taught today.

Why Clear Aids Matter

Safety

Reliable Responses

A horse that understands and responds consistently to aids is safer to handle and ride. Clear signals reduce confusion, which reduces the risk of unexpected reactions from the horse.

Communication

Reducing Confusion

Inconsistent or conflicting aids — asking the horse to go with the leg while pulling hard with the hand — create confusion and resistance. Clear aids give the horse one clear message at a time.

Welfare

Respectful Horsemanship

Aids should be applied with the minimum effective pressure and released the moment the horse responds correctly. This reward — the removal of pressure — is how horses learn what is being asked.

Progression

Building on a Foundation

Advanced riding skills — lateral movements, collection, jumping — all build directly on the rider's ability to apply and coordinate basic aids. Learning aids early creates the foundation for all future progress.

Things to Remember

  • Aids are signals a rider uses to communicate — not commands that force movement.
  • Natural aids use the rider\'s body: legs, seat, hands, and voice.
  • Artificial aids are equipment that supports the natural aids.
  • Aids should start light and release the moment the horse responds correctly.
