Natural & Artificial Aids

Artificial Aids

Artificial aids are tools and equipment that supplement the natural aids. Each one has a specific purpose, correct application, and important limitations. None replaces clear natural aids — they support them.

Tools That Support Communication

Artificial aids are not shortcuts around training — they are extensions of the natural aids used in specific situations and with specific training goals. Understanding what each artificial aid does, when it is appropriately used, and what it cannot replace is part of educated horsemanship. Misuse of artificial aids often creates the very problems they are meant to solve.

💡 Did You Know

Modern safety stirrups — which are designed to release if a rider falls — were developed after it became clear that a significant number of serious riding accidents involved a rider being dragged because their foot became caught in a traditional stirrup. The design has evolved significantly from early iron box stirrups to today’s breakaway and angled safety versions.

Spurs are worn on the rider's boot and extend the reach and precision of the leg aid. They are a tool for refinement, not for driving a horse that does not respond to leg — and they require a steady, established leg position before introduction.

Spurs

Spurs attach to the heel of the rider's boot and allow the rider to apply the leg aid with greater precision and lighter contact than the calf alone. A quiet, experienced rider with a stable leg position uses spurs to give a more refined signal — a subtle touch rather than a broad squeeze — particularly in disciplines requiring fine lateral movements or precise transitions.

Spurs come in a range of designs. Blunt or rounded spurs (often called "dummy spurs" or "training spurs") have no rowel and produce minimal pressure. Longer-shanked spurs with rowels are reserved for experienced riders and specific disciplines where the horse is already well-trained to respond to leg aids. The type, length, and sharpness of a spur must match the horse's training level and sensitivity.

The most common misuse of spurs is introducing them before the rider has a consistently still, independent leg. A leg that moves with every stride will create unintentional spur contact, which at best desensitizes the horse to leg aids and at worst creates pain, anxiety, and resistance. Instructors commonly recommend that beginning riders not wear spurs until consistent balance and leg stability are established.

Key Fact: Spurs extend the leg aid's precision, not its force. A rider must have a stable, established leg before wearing spurs — otherwise every unintended movement touches the horse's side.

Whips and crops are carried by the rider and used as an extension of the leg aid — providing a light tap as reinforcement when the horse does not respond to the natural leg. They are a communication tool, not a punishment instrument.

Whips and Crops

A crop (or bat) is a short whip typically 20 to 26 inches long, with a flat leather flap at the end that produces a sound and light sensation when tapped against the horse's side or neck. A dressage whip is longer — often 36 to 45 inches — allowing the rider to tap the horse's hindquarters without changing hand position. A longe whip is much longer still and is used from the ground to direct the horse's energy on the longe line without touching the horse in most cases.

The sequence of aids matters: leg first, then if no response, a light tap with the whip as reinforcement. The tap should be applied immediately after the leg aid, while the horse is still processing the request — not after a delay, which creates confusion about what is being reinforced. After the horse responds, the whip is not used again for the same request unless the leg aid is again ignored.

Whips used as punishment — striking repeatedly, with force, or after the moment of the behavior — do not teach the horse what is wanted and often create fear or defensive reactions. Like all aids, the whip should produce the lightest effective signal and be used progressively, with release serving as the primary reward.

Key Fact: Whips and crops reinforce the leg aid — they are not a substitute for it. Leg first, then tap if no response. Timing is essential; a delayed tap reinforces nothing useful.

Martingales are pieces of tack that limit how high the horse can carry its head, helping maintain a position that allows effective rein contact. They are common in jumping sports and some Western disciplines.

Martingales

A standing martingale attaches from the girth to the noseband, setting a fixed limit on how high the horse can raise its head. It does not move with the horse's head — it simply creates a ceiling. Standing martingales are commonly seen in hunter and jumper divisions and in polo. They prevent the horse from flipping the head up into the rider's face but do not influence rein contact.

A running martingale (also called a running or German martingale) attaches from the girth through a loop at the chest and splits into two rings, each sitting on one rein. It only creates downward pressure on the rein when the horse raises its head above the point of effectiveness, adding a brief downward signal through the rein. When the horse carries its head in the correct position, the running martingale hangs slack and has no effect. This makes it a more active training tool than the standing martingale.

Martingales address the symptom — a high head carriage — not always the cause. A horse evading contact by raising the head is often responding to bit discomfort, inconsistent hands, or pain elsewhere. Martingales work best as a safety and training supplement alongside addressing the underlying issue, rather than as a permanent solution.

Key Fact: A standing martingale sets a fixed head position limit. A running martingale acts only when the head rises above the correct position. Neither replaces correct training or diagnoses the cause of high head carriage.
Barn Connection: Artificial aids are regulated or restricted in many competitive disciplines. Before using any artificial aid in training or competition, checking the rules of the relevant organization is part of responsible horsemanship. Equipment that is common in one discipline may be prohibited in another.

Things to Remember

  • Spurs extend the precision of the leg aid — not its force. They require a stable, established leg position.
  • Whips and crops reinforce the leg: leg first, tap if no response, release when the horse responds.
  • A standing martingale fixes a head-position ceiling. A running martingale acts only when the head rises too high.
  • Artificial aids are regulated in many disciplines — equipment legal in one class may be banned in another.
