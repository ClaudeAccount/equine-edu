The Head

Head — External Structures

Top of Skull

Poll

The bony crest at the very top of the skull, between the ears. Sensitive to pressure from ill-fitting headstalls. Stiffness here can limit flexion at the poll under saddle.

Top of Head

Ears

Large, mobile ears that rotate independently. Ear position is a primary indicator of attention, mood, and pain. Both ears consistently pinned back or held low may signal discomfort.

Between Ears

Forelock

The tuft of mane hair falling from the poll onto the forehead. Primarily a fly deterrent. Thin or missing forelock can suggest nutritional issues or skin conditions.

Upper Face

Forehead

The broad, flat area of the skull above the eyes. The width of the forehead is part of overall head conformation. Look for symmetry between the left and right sides.

Above Eye

Supraorbital Fossa

The indentation above each eye where fat is stored. Depth of this hollow is a reliable body-condition indicator — deep hollows suggest poor condition even when the rest of the horse looks acceptable.

Side of Face

Eye & Orbit

The eye sits within the bony orbit on the side of the skull. Assess for cloudiness, discharge, swelling of the lids, or asymmetry between eyes. A dull or sunken eye can indicate illness or pain.

Mid Face

Facial Crest

The prominent bony ridge running horizontally along the cheek below the eye. A visible external landmark used to assess head conformation and to position equipment such as flash nosebands.

Side of Jaw

Cheek

The soft tissue region over the lower jaw and cheek muscles. Obvious asymmetry — one cheek appearing swollen or sunken — may indicate dental issues on that side.

Lower Face

Nostril

The two external openings of the nasal passages. At rest, should be soft and slightly open. Note any discharge, flaring at rest, or asymmetry between nostrils, which can indicate respiratory issues.

Lower Face

Muzzle

The soft lower portion of the face including lips, chin, and the area around the nostrils. The vibrissae (whisker hairs) provide tactile sensitivity for close-range assessment of objects.

Underside of Jaw

Chin Groove

The groove on the underside of the lower jaw where a curb chain or strap sits. Sensitivity or skin damage here is often related to bit and curb-chain fit. Palpate for any swelling or scarring.

Lower Jaw

Mandible

The lower jawbone, palpable along its entire length. Should be uniform in width on both sides. Asymmetry or swelling may indicate dental issues, infection, or cyst formation.

Head-Neck Junction

Throatlatch

Where the underside of the jaw meets the top of the neck. Must have adequate clearance for the horse to flex at the poll. A thick or heavy throatlatch limits flexion and can affect airway function during collection.

Neck & Shoulder — External Structures

Top of Neck

Crest

The topline of the neck formed by the nuchal ligament and overlying muscles. A healthy, well-muscled crest is firm but supple. An excessively cresty or hard crest can indicate metabolic issues, particularly in easy-keeper breeds.

Side of Neck

Jugular Groove

The channel running along the lower side of the neck where the jugular vein lies. The jugular vein is visible and palpable here — useful to know its location for veterinary assessments and blood draws.

Neck Musculature

Neck Muscling

The muscle mass and shape of the neck in profile. A ewe neck (concave topline) affects balance, head carriage, and bit contact. Asymmetrical muscling between left and right sides may reflect training or a compensatory pattern.

Front of Shoulder

Point of Shoulder

The bony prominence at the front of the shoulder joint. A critical saddle-fit landmark — the front of any saddle must clear the point of shoulder to allow full stride without pinching.

Shoulder Blade

Scapula

The large, flat shoulder blade under the skin and muscle of the shoulder region. The scapula's length and angle directly affect stride length. A well-laid-back scapula is associated with a longer, smoother stride.

Shoulder Junction

Shoulder Angle

The angle of the shoulder, measured between the scapula and the humerus. An ideal shoulder angle of 45–55 degrees produces a long, ground-covering stride. An upright shoulder shortens stride and increases concussion to the front limbs.

Neck Meets Back

Withers

The highest point of the back, between the shoulder blades. Standard measurement point for horse height in hands. The withers anchor major back and neck muscles. Prominent withers can make saddle fit challenging; flat withers allow saddles to slip.

Upper Foreleg

Point of Elbow

The bony prominence at the back of the elbow joint (olecranon process). A hygroma (fluid-filled swelling) here is called a capped elbow and often results from lying on hard surfaces. Usually cosmetic if cool and pain-free.

Chest

Pectoral Muscles

The chest muscles between and behind the front legs. Well-developed pectorals reflect good overall conditioning. The groove between the pectoral muscles is where body-condition scoring can reveal early changes in condition.

Body — External Structures

Topline

Back

The topline from behind the withers to the loin. Should be straight or very slightly concave. A hollow or sway back reduces the ability to carry a rider comfortably and may indicate weakness, poor condition, or age-related change.

Topline

Loin

The area between the last rib and the croup. Short-coupled horses have a short loin, generally considered strong. A long loin is weaker and less able to transfer hindquarter power forward efficiently.

Hindquarters

Croup

The top of the hindquarters from the highest point of the pelvis to the tail. Croup slope varies by breed. Assess from the side and directly behind — asymmetry from behind may indicate a pelvic imbalance.

Side of Pelvis

Point of Hip

The bony prominence on the side of the pelvis (tuber coxae). Clearly visible from the side and above. Asymmetry between the left and right points of hip may indicate a pelvic injury or chronic muscle imbalance.

Rear of Pelvis

Point of Buttock

The most rearward bony prominence of the pelvis (tuber ischium), visible as the rounded rear point of the hindquarters. Used as a reference point for hindquarter muscling and for assessing pelvic symmetry from behind.

Tail Root

Dock

The bony, muscular upper portion of the tail. Tail carriage in motion is a meaningful soundness cue. A clamped tail held tightly against the body often signals pain or tension. Bandaging always starts at the dock.

Rib Cage

Barrel

The rounded rib cage between the forelegs and the flank. Well-sprung ribs create more thoracic volume and more saddle-panel contact area. Flat ribs are associated with a narrower chest and potentially less athletic capacity.

Behind Ribs

Flank

The soft area just behind the last rib and in front of the hindquarters. Rapidly heaving flanks at rest (double-lift) can indicate equine asthma. A tucked-up flank may suggest recent hard work, pain, or poor condition.

Girth Area

Heart Girth

The circumference measured just behind the elbow at the deepest point of the chest. Horses with good heart girth have a deep chest and more room for the heart and lungs. Girth placement is based on this area.

Belly

Underline

The profile of the abdomen from the girth area to the flank. A gradually rising underline front to back is typical of a fit horse. A pot-bellied or pendulous underline may suggest parasitism, poor feed quality, or poor muscular condition.

Front Legs — External Structures

Upper Front Leg

Forearm

The muscular region of the front leg between elbow and knee. A long forearm relative to cannon bone is a desirable conformation trait, associated with more efficient movement and less concussion.

Inside of Forearm

Chestnut — Front

The oval, horn-like vestigial structure on the inside of the foreleg above the knee. Shape and size are unique to each horse and have historically been used for identification. No function in the modern horse.

Middle Joint

Knee (Carpus)

The complex joint in the middle of the front leg, equivalent to the human wrist. Comprised of two rows of small carpal bones. Palpate for heat, swelling, or joint effusion. Any acute swelling here warrants veterinary attention.

Side of Cannon

Splint Area

The medial and lateral surfaces of the cannon where the small splint bones attach. Bony enlargements (“splints”) here are common. Active splints are warm and sore; mature splints are cool and hard. Location near the knee or suspensory matters.

Main Bone

Cannon — Front

The large metacarpal bone between the knee and fetlock. Should feel hard, flat, and cool with a well-defined back edge. Any thickening, heat, or loss of definition at the back of the cannon may indicate tendon or ligament involvement.

Back of Cannon

Flexor Tendons

The SDFT and DDFT run down the back of the cannon as distinct, firm, parallel structures. They should feel smooth, cool, and clearly defined. Filling, heat, or thickening (“bowed tendon”) indicates injury requiring veterinary assessment.

Lower Joint

Fetlock

The prominent joint between the cannon bone and the pastern. Palpate for joint effusion, heat, or pain on the sides. A hot, acutely filled fetlock is a soundness concern requiring prompt evaluation.

Back of Fetlock

Ergot

A small, horn-like vestigial structure at the back of the fetlock, hidden in the fetlock tuft. No current function. Overgrown ergots can be trimmed during grooming without discomfort to the horse.

Below Fetlock

Pastern

The sloping section between fetlock and hoof. Ideal front pastern angle mirrors the shoulder angle (around 47–55°). Short, upright pasterns reduce shock absorption; long, sloping pasterns increase strain on the flexor tendons.

Top of Hoof

Coronary Band

The soft, vascular band at the top of the hoof wall where hoof horn grows from. Should be smooth, supple, and consistent in height around the entire hoof. Any cuts or notches here produce corresponding defects in the growing hoof wall below.

Hoof

Hoof — Front

Front hooves are rounder and slightly larger than hind hooves to support the greater weight-bearing load. Paired front hooves should match each other in size and shape — significant differences can indicate chronic lameness on the smaller side.

Back Legs — External Structures

Upper Hind Leg

Thigh

The large muscular mass of the upper hind leg from the hip to the stifle. Assess for symmetry between left and right — one side being noticeably more developed may reflect a compensatory pattern.

Upper Joint

Stifle

The large joint at the top of the hind leg, equivalent to the human knee. Palpate for effusion on the sides of the joint. The stifle is the most complex joint in the horse and a common site of lameness in athletic horses.

Mid Hind Leg

Gaskin

The muscular region of the lower thigh between the stifle and the hock, equivalent to the human calf. Well-developed gaskins are an indicator of conditioning and hind-end strength.

Main Hind Joint

Hock (Tarsus)

The large, angular joint in the middle of the hind leg, equivalent to the human ankle. Palpate for heat and effusion on the inside of the joint (“bog spavin”) or bony enlargement on the lower inside surfaces (“bone spavin”). Clean, flat hocks with no swelling are ideal.

Back of Hock

Point of Hock

The bony, rearward prominence of the hock (calcaneus), equivalent to the human heel. A fluid-filled swelling here is called a “capped hock,” typically from trauma. Usually cosmetic if cool and pain-free.

Inside Below Hock

Chestnut — Hind

The vestigial horn-like structure on the inside of the hind leg, located below the hock. Hind chestnuts are typically smaller than front chestnuts. Shape and size are unique to each horse.

Lower Hind Leg

Cannon — Hind

The large metatarsal bone between the hock and fetlock. Palpate the back of the cannon for the flexor tendons and suspensory ligament, assessed the same way as the front leg. The hind cannon is typically slightly longer than the front.

Lower Joint

Fetlock — Hind

The fetlock joint of the hind limb, assessed the same way as the front. A modest amount of filling in the hind fetlocks after hard work is more common than in the fronts, but persistent filling still warrants monitoring.

Below Fetlock

Pastern — Hind

The pastern of the hind leg. Hind pasterns are typically more upright than front pasterns, which is normal. Compare left to right rather than front to hind when evaluating hind-limb pastern angles.

Top of Hoof

Coronary Band — Hind

The hoof-growth zone at the top of the hind hoof, assessed the same way as the front. Injuries here will grow out in the hoof wall over several months. Monitor for any lacerations during routine hoof care.

Hoof

Hoof — Hind

Hind hooves are typically more oval and slightly narrower than front hooves. They provide the primary propulsive force. Compare paired hinds for differences in size, shape, or hoof-wall angle that could indicate chronic unevenness of weight-bearing.
