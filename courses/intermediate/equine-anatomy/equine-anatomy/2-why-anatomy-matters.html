External Equine Anatomy

Why External Anatomy

Everything you need to know about a horse's health, soundness, and way of going is visible on the outside — if you know what to look for.

Developing Your Eye

The beginner course taught you to locate and name the major parts of the horse. That is the foundation. This course goes to the next level: learning to actually read what you see.

Every time you approach a horse, you are already doing a visual assessment — you just may not have a framework for it yet. This course gives you that framework. You will learn what each external structure looks like when it is healthy, how to evaluate it with your eyes and hands, and what it can tell you about the horse's soundness, condition, and conformation.

The Intermediate Step

This course does not go into surgical anatomy, internal organs, or veterinary procedures. The focus is entirely on the exterior — what experienced horse people see and feel every day at the barn. That is a wide and genuinely useful body of knowledge.

Why External Observation Matters

Knowing what to look for changes everything about how you interact with horses.

Daily Observation

Noticing Changes Early

Most health issues show up externally before they become serious — swelling at a joint, a change in hoof quality, a shift in muscling, or unusual skin and coat condition. Knowing what "normal" looks like for each part of the horse is the only way to catch something when it first appears.

Hands-On Assessment

Knowing What You Are Feeling

Running your hands down a horse's legs, checking the temperature of a joint, palpating the back — these are skills every handler should have. This course teaches you what each surface structure feels like so that palpation becomes an actual tool, not just a habit.

Conformation

Reading How a Horse is Built

Conformation is entirely an external science. Shoulder angle, pastern angle, the slope of the croup, the spring of the rib, the length of the back — all of it is evaluated from the outside. Understanding these features helps you assess a horse's suitability for a job and anticipate where it may be prone to stress or injury.

Communicating with Professionals

Describing What You See

Vets and farriers use precise anatomical language. When you can say "there is heat and a small amount of filling at the medial fetlock" rather than "something looks off on the lower leg," you communicate faster and more effectively. This course gives you that vocabulary.

Tack and Equipment Fit

Fit Starts with Knowing the Body

A saddle that presses on the withers, pinches the point of shoulder, or bridges the back causes pain and resistance. A poorly fitted halter can put pressure on the poll. Understanding external landmarks makes it much easier to assess whether equipment is fitting correctly.

Ground and Ride Work

Connecting Observation to Feel

Trainers and instructors give corrections and cues based on external body parts: "push from the hindquarters," "free up the shoulder," "soften through the poll." Understanding these structures on the outside of the horse makes it easier to understand what is being asked and why it matters.

How This Course is Organized

The horse's exterior is covered in four regions. Each region has its own tab in the main lesson so you can work through one section at a time before moving on.

The Head

From the poll between the ears to the muzzle and jaw — every external feature of the face, including sensory organs, facial landmarks, and the head-neck junction.

Neck & Shoulder

The topline of the neck, the jugular groove, the scapula and shoulder angle, the withers, and the pectoral muscles — and what each tells you about balance and stride.

The Body

The back, loin, croup, hindquarter landmarks, rib cage, barrel, flank, and underline — the load-bearing core of the horse, assessed from the outside.

The Legs

Both front and hind legs from elbow and stifle to hoof — every external joint, bony landmark, palpable tendon, and the structures of the hoof itself.

A Note on Scope

This course covers external anatomy — what you can see, feel, and assess on the surface of the horse. Some structures covered here, like the tendons on the back of the cannon or the ligaments at the fetlock, are not visible but are routinely assessed by hand. These are included because palpating them is a standard part of any hands-on horse evaluation.

This is not a veterinary anatomy course. Internal organs, the detailed structure of joints, and surgical landmarks are outside the scope here. The goal is confident, competent external observation — the kind of knowledge that pays off every day.
