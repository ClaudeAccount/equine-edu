Base Coat Colors

What Is Pigment?

Every horse coat color — from bright chestnut to true black — is built from just two pigments. Understanding these pigments is the foundation for understanding all base coat colors.

The Building Blocks of Horse Color

When you look at a chestnut, a black, or a bay horse, you are seeing the result of pigment — a natural substance produced inside the horse’s body that gives hair its color. No matter how many different coat colors exist in the horse world, they all trace back to just two types of pigment working alone or together.

This lesson introduces those two pigments, explains where they come from, and covers the important concept that when pigment is absent entirely, hair appears white. White markings are not a color — they are the absence of color.

The Two Pigments in Horses

Black Pigment

Eumelanin

Eumelanin is the pigment responsible for black and dark brown coloring. When eumelanin is fully expressed, it produces true black hair. It is found in the body coat, mane, tail, and lower legs of horses that carry it.

Red Pigment

Phaeomelanin

Phaeomelanin is the pigment responsible for red, copper, orange, and yellow tones. It creates the warm body color seen on chestnut horses. The shade can vary widely — from pale gold to deep liver — but the underlying pigment is the same.

No Pigment

White (Absence of Pigment)

Where no pigment is deposited in the hair, the hair appears white. This is why white markings — such as a blaze or white socks — exist. They are simply areas where the cells that produce pigment were never active. No pigment means no color.

💡 Did You Know

Only two pigments create all recognized horse coat colors. Chestnut horses have phaeomelanin only. Black horses have eumelanin only. Bay horses have both — red on the body and black on the mane, tail, and legs.

Where Pigment Comes From

Pigment Producers

Melanocytes

Pigment is made by specialized cells called melanocytes. These cells live in the skin and in the hair follicles. As each hair grows, melanocytes deposit pigment into it. The type and amount of pigment deposited determines the final color of that hair.

Genetic Control

Genes Switch Pigments On and Off

A horse’s genes determine which pigments its melanocytes produce. Some genes tell melanocytes to make eumelanin. Others switch production to phaeomelanin. This genetic switching is why two horses with the same parents can end up with different coat colors.

The Full Picture

All Colors from Two Pigments

Every recognized base coat color — chestnut, black, and bay — is the result of eumelanin, phaeomelanin, or a combination of both being expressed in different amounts and locations. No other pigments are involved.

Reading the Three Base Colors Through Pigment

C

Chestnut — Red Pigment Only

Chestnut horses produce only phaeomelanin. No eumelanin is expressed anywhere on the body, mane, or tail. The result is a fully red or copper horse with no black points.

B

Black — Black Pigment Only

Black horses produce only eumelanin. No phaeomelanin means no red or warm tones anywhere. The body, mane, and tail are all uniformly black with no contrast.

A

Bay — Both Pigments

Bay horses express both pigments. Phaeomelanin produces the red-brown body, while eumelanin creates the black mane, tail, lower legs, and ear tips — known as black points.

Key Terms

Pigment

A natural substance that gives color to hair, skin, and other tissues. In horses, pigment is produced by melanocytes and deposited into hair follicles.

Eumelanin

The black pigment. Responsible for black and dark brown hair. Present in the body coat and points of black and bay horses.

Phaeomelanin

The red pigment. Responsible for red, copper, and yellow tones. Dominant in chestnut horses, and present on the body of bay horses.

Melanocyte

A specialized cell in the skin that produces pigment. Melanocytes deposit eumelanin or phaeomelanin into hair as it grows.

White Marking

An area of white hair caused by the absence of pigment. No pigment cells were active in that region, so no color was deposited.

Base Coat Color

The underlying coat color determined by pigment type and genetics, before any dilution or pattern modifiers are considered.
