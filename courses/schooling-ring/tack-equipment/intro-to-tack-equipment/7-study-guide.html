Key Concepts

What is tack, and what are its three main categories?

Tack is any equipment placed on a horse to allow riding, driving, or safe handling. The main categories are:

  • Riding tack — saddle and bridle
  • Handling tack — halter and lead rope
  • Protective equipment — boots, bandages, blankets

A fourth group, driving harness, is used for horses that pull carts or carriages instead of being ridden.

What is the tree of a saddle, and why is it so important?

The tree is the inside frame of the saddle. It is usually made of wood, fiberglass, or synthetic materials. It controls:

  • the shape of the saddle
  • how weight is spread across the horse's back

Two key parts of fit are:

  • Tree width — matches the horse's shoulder and back width
  • Tree angle — matches the angle of the horse's shoulder

If the tree does not fit, padding cannot fix it — the saddle must be replaced or repaired.

What are the main parts of a saddle?
  • Pommel — front raised area; clears the withers
  • Cantle — back raised area; supports the rider
  • Seat — where the rider sits
  • Tree — internal frame
  • Panels — padded underside that touches the horse
  • Stirrups & leathers — support the rider's legs and feet
  • Girth/Cinch — holds the saddle in place
  • Skirt/Flap — outer covering that protects and supports structure
What is bridging in saddle fit?

Bridging happens when the saddle touches only at the front and back, leaving a gap in the middle. This is a problem because:

  • pressure is not spread evenly
  • two small areas carry too much weight
  • it can cause pain, muscle loss, and white hairs

Padding does not fix bridging and may make it worse.

What are the main parts of a bridle?
  • Crownpiece — rests behind the ears
  • Cheekpieces — connect crownpiece to bit
  • Browband — keeps bridle from sliding back
  • Throatlatch — prevents bridle from slipping off
  • Noseband — helps stabilize the head and jaw
  • Bit — sits in the mouth and carries rein pressure
  • Reins — connect rider's hands to the bit
What is the difference between a snaffle and a curb bit?
  • Snaffle bit — direct pressure; rein pulls directly affect the same side of the mouth
  • Curb bit — uses leverage; small rein movement creates more pressure through shanks and curb chain

Snaffles are usually used for basic training. Curbs are used when horses are more advanced.

How is bit height checked?

A correctly fitted snaffle bit creates one to two wrinkles at the corners of the mouth. If it is:

  • too low → may hit teeth or hang too loose
  • too high → creates constant pressure and discomfort

Bit height is adjusted using the cheekpieces, not the reins.

What are signs of poor tack fit?

Behavioral signs:

  • Ear pinning when saddled
  • Tail swishing during work
  • Bucking or resistance
  • Reluctance to move forward
  • Sensitivity when girth is tightened

Physical signs:

  • White hairs under saddle area
  • Hair loss or rubbed spots
  • Uneven muscle development
  • Uneven sweat patterns
What is correct halter fit?

A halter should:

  • allow two fingers under the noseband
  • allow four fingers under the crownpiece
  • be snug enough not to slip off, but not tight

Too loose is a safety risk; too tight causes discomfort and rubbing. Fit should be checked regularly as horses change shape through the year.

Common Mistakes

Using padding to fix a bad saddle

Padding cannot fix a tree that is too narrow or mismatched. It may actually increase pressure in the wrong places.

Ignoring behavior as a fit signal

Ear pinning, bucking, and resistance can be signs of discomfort — not just training issues.

Only checking fit for the rider

A saddle must fit the horse and the rider separately. Both matter.

Tightening the girth too quickly

Girths should be tightened gradually to avoid discomfort or resistance.

Ignoring white hairs

White hairs are permanent evidence of past pressure damage and should always be taken seriously.
