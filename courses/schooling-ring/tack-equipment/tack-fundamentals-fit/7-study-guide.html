Tack Basics

What does the word tack mean?

Tack is the equipment used to ride, drive, or handle horses, such as saddles, bridles, reins, halters, and harness.

What are the three main kinds of tack?

Riding tack (used under saddle), driving tack (used to pull a cart or carriage), and handling tack (used to lead or hold a horse from the ground).

What are the two main things tack does?

It helps a person stay safe and balanced, and it helps a person and a horse share clear signals.

How does tack help communication?

It carries small signals from the person to the horse, such as a rein movement through the bridle or a weight shift through the saddle, and it lets the person feel the horse in return.

Main Parts and Fit

What are the basic jobs of the saddle, the bridle, and the harness?

The saddle carries the rider's weight and protects the back, the bridle guides the head and carries rein signals, and the harness lets a horse pull a load.

What does fit mean, and why does it matter?

Fit is how well a piece of tack matches the shape of the horse. Good fit keeps the horse comfortable and signals clear. Poor fit can cause pain, stiff movement, and confusion.

Name some simple signs that tack may not fit well.

Pinned ears, a swishing tail, rubbed or white hairs, dry spots in a sweaty coat, short or stiff steps, and moving away from the tack.

Why should fit be checked often instead of only once?

A horse's shape changes with age, season, and fitness, so tack that fit before may not fit now.

Tack Types, Safety, and Scenarios

How do Western, English, and Driving tack differ in purpose?

Western tack is built for ranch work with a larger, supportive saddle. English tack is built for sport with a lighter, close-contact saddle. Driving tack is built for pulling and uses a harness instead of a saddle.

What basic principles do all tack types share?

They all spread pressure, allow free movement, carry clear signals, and depend on good fit.

What does the pre-use tack check cover?

The leather and straps, the stitching and buckles, the fit and placement, and the cleanliness of the tack, with a final recheck just before use.

A handler notices dry, cracked spots on a girth strap. What should happen next, and why?

The tack should not be used until it is repaired or replaced. Cracked, worn leather is a weak point that can break under strain and cause a fall.

A horse pins its ears and steps away when the saddle is brought out. What might this mean?

It may be a sign that the horse links the tack with discomfort. The fit and the horse's back should be checked before continuing.
