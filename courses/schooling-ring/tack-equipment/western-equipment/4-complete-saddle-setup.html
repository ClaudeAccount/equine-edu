Western Tack & Equipment

The Complete Saddle Setup

A western saddle does not work alone. The cinch, breast collar, and saddle pad or blanket all help the saddle stay secure, protect the horse's back, and support the type of riding being done.

More Than the Saddle

The saddle is the largest piece of western tack, but it depends on the rest of the setup to function correctly. A well-chosen pad or blanket helps manage pressure and moisture. A properly fitted cinch keeps the saddle stable around the horse's barrel. A breast collar can help keep the saddle from sliding back during hills, speed work, ranch tasks, or active riding.

These pieces should work with the saddle, not compensate for a poor fit. If a saddle shifts badly, pinches, bridges, or needs an extreme pad to seem usable, the setup should be checked by a knowledgeable instructor, saddle fitter, or experienced tack professional.

The Three Supporting Pieces

Each item has a job. Learning what it does helps riders tack up safely and talk clearly about equipment choices.

Security

Cinches

The cinch fastens around the horse's barrel and holds the saddle in place. On the near (left) side, the latigo — a long leather or nylon strap — threads through the cinch ring and ties or buckles to tighten the saddle. On the off (right) side, the off billet is a shorter, fixed strap that attaches directly to the cinch ring with a buckle. Together, the latigo and off billet hold the cinch evenly under the horse. The cinch should lie flat and be snug enough for security without pinching skin or pulling the saddle out of balance.

Stability

Breast Collars

A breast collar attaches to the front of the saddle and helps limit backward saddle movement. It is especially useful for trail riding, hills, ranch work, roping, speed events, and horses whose build makes the saddle more likely to shift.

Protection

Saddle Pads & Blankets

The pad or blanket sits between the horse and saddle. It helps protect the back, absorb sweat, reduce friction, and support even pressure — but it should match the horse, saddle, and job rather than hide a bad saddle fit.

Saddle Pad vs. Saddle Blanket

A saddle pad and a saddle blanket are not the same thing and are not used interchangeably. Each has a specific role in the western setup, and knowing the difference helps riders tack up correctly and recognize what is used at shows and events.

Structured Protection

Saddle Pad

A saddle pad is a thicker, structured piece — often made with foam, felt, wool, or gel inserts — designed to absorb impact, manage pressure, and protect the horse's back during work. Pads are commonly used for trail riding, ranch work, and other active disciplines where cushioning matters most.

Decorative Layer

Saddle Blanket

A saddle blanket is a woven blanket — often featuring traditional or decorative patterns — that goes on top of the saddle pad, between the pad and the saddle. It is primarily used for dress purposes: showing, parades, and other events where a polished, traditional look matters. It adds a finished western appearance without replacing the protection a pad provides underneath.

The saddle pad goes on first for protection and fit. The blanket goes on top of the pad when a dressed-up look is needed. A blanket alone is not used in place of a pad for a regular ride.

What Riders Should Check

1

Pad Placement

Place the pad or blanket smoothly on the horse's back with no folds, lumps, or trapped hair. It should extend beyond the saddle edges enough to protect the back and help prevent rubs from rigging or hardware.

2

Cinch Position

The cinch should line up naturally with the saddle's rigging and the horse's girth area. After tightening, check that the saddle is centered and that the cinch is not pulled too far forward or sitting unevenly.

3

Breast Collar Fit

A breast collar should be adjusted so it helps stabilize the saddle without restricting the horse's shoulder movement or pressing into the windpipe. It should sit cleanly across the chest and attach evenly on both sides.

4

Final Safety Check

Before mounting, check the cinch again, make sure straps are lying flat, and confirm that the pad, breast collar, stirrups, reins, and bridle are all secure. Tack can settle as the horse breathes, moves, or relaxes.

A good western setup should feel stable without looking forced. If the horse shows soreness, pinned ears, swelling, white hairs, girthiness, or sudden resistance, the tack setup deserves a closer look before continuing.
