Horse Teeth Basics

Teeth and Age

A horse's teeth change all through its life. Baby teeth are replaced by adult teeth on a steady schedule, and the teeth wear down in set patterns. People use these changes to estimate a horse's age. Knowing this timeline is a useful part of horse care.

A Lifetime of Change

Horses are born with baby teeth, also called milk teeth. These are replaced by adult teeth during the first five years of life. After that, the adult front teeth keep changing in ways that help people guess a horse's age.

This is called "aging a horse by its teeth." Horse people, buyers, and vets have used it for a long time. It gives a good estimate, not an exact age, but it is still helpful.

💡 Did You Know

The saying "don't look a gift horse in the mouth" comes from aging horses by their teeth. Looking in a horse's mouth was a way to judge its worth. Doing this to a horse you were given was seen as rude. People have used the saying in English since at least the 1500s.

Foals are born with baby teeth. These are smaller, whiter, and more delicate than the adult teeth that will replace them. The baby teeth fall out as the adult teeth push up from below, much like what happens with children.

Deciduous Teeth (Milk Teeth)

The baby front teeth are also called milk teeth. They are smaller and whiter than adult teeth, and they are more pinched in just above the gumline. These features help tell a young horse's teeth from an adult's.

Many foals are born with the baby central teeth already in, or they come in within the first few days. The baby middle teeth follow at about four to six weeks. The baby corner teeth come in at about six to nine months. By about nine months old, all the baby front teeth are in.

Caps

As adult teeth push up, the baby teeth above them, now called caps, slowly fall off. Caps are the leftover shells of the baby teeth. Most caps fall off on their own as the adult tooth comes in. Sometimes a cap stays on too long and sits loosely over the new tooth. This can be uncomfortable and may need a vet to remove it.

Baby front teeth are whiter and smaller than adult teeth, with a clearly pinched neck. When you lift the lips, a foal with baby teeth looks quite different from an adult horse.

Adult teeth come in on a fairly steady schedule. This helps estimate the age of horses under five. After age five, tooth wear matters more than new teeth coming in.

The Eruption Timeline

The adult central teeth usually come in at about two and a half years. They replace the baby central teeth. The adult middle teeth follow at about three and a half years. The adult corner teeth finish the set at about four and a half years. By age five, the top and bottom front teeth usually touch and wear together.

The adult cheek teeth replace the baby premolars on a similar schedule, between about two and a half and four years. The molars do not replace baby teeth. They come in between one and four years of age, from front to back.

A horse has a "full mouth" of adult teeth at about five years of age. After that, age guesses rely more on how the teeth wear.

The main ages to know: 2½ years = adult centrals; 3½ years = adult middle teeth; 4½ years = adult corners; 5 years = full mouth of adult teeth.

Once a horse has a full set of adult teeth, age is estimated from how the teeth wear and change over the years. These clues work best in horses between about five and twenty years old. They are less exact in older horses.

The Cup (Infundibulum)

Each adult front tooth has a cup, a dip in the middle of the biting surface. As the tooth wears, the cup gets shallower. The cup is "present" when you can see it clearly and "gone" (or "smooth") when it wears flat. The cup wears away in the bottom teeth first, then the top. The order it disappears — from centrals to middle teeth to corners — helps estimate age up to about twelve years.

The Dental Star

As the cup wears away, a dental star appears. It is a yellowish mark just in front of where the cup was, and it shows the inner part of the tooth. It starts as a thin yellow line, then becomes a rounder mark as the tooth wears more. The dental star is another clue to age, used along with the cup.

Galvayne's Groove

Galvayne's groove is a line that runs down the outside of the upper corner tooth. It shows up at the gumline around age ten. By about fifteen it reaches halfway down the tooth. By about twenty it runs the full length. Then it starts to fade from the top: gone from the top half by about twenty-five, and gone completely by about thirty. Because it follows a steady timeline, it is one of the most used age clues in older horses.

At the Barn

Aging a horse by its teeth is a good estimate, not an exact number. Several clues are checked together: cup wear, the dental star, the angle where the teeth meet, and Galvayne's groove. Even experts can be off by one to three years in older horses. When an exact age really matters — for registration, buying, or competing — pair the tooth check with registration papers, microchip records, or a vet's opinion.

Things to Remember

  • Horses have baby (milk) teeth that are replaced by adult teeth during the first five years of life.
  • Adult centrals come in at 2½ years, middle teeth at 3½ years, corners at 4½ years. Full mouth = about 5 years.
  • Retained temporary teeth are called caps; they usually fall off naturally but can occasionally require removal.
  • The cup is a dip in each front tooth's biting surface that wears away as the horse ages.
  • Galvayne's groove appears at the gumline of the upper corner incisor at about age 10 and reaches the full tooth by about age 20.
