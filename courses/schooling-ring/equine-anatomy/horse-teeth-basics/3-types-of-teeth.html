Horse Teeth Basics

Types of Teeth

A horse has a few different kinds of teeth. Each kind sits in a certain spot and does a certain job. Knowing what each one does, and where it sits, helps you understand dental care, age, and bit fit.

A Mouth Built for Grazing

A horse's mouth is built for grazing most of the day. The front teeth bite off grass. The back teeth grind it into a soft pulp before the horse swallows. Each kind of tooth has its own job. Knowing the difference helps explain why a problem in one part of the mouth looks different from a problem in another.

💡 Did You Know

A grown male horse usually has about 40 teeth: 12 front teeth (incisors), 4 canines, up to 4 wolf teeth, 12 premolars, and 12 molars. Most mares have about 36 teeth, since they often have no canines or only tiny ones, and wolf teeth are less common in mares.

The incisors are the front teeth. You can see them when a horse lifts its lip. They bite off grass and hay, and they are the teeth most often checked to estimate a horse's age.

The Incisors

An adult horse has 12 incisors: six on top and six on the bottom. They sit in a curved row at the front of the mouth. Each one has a name based on where it sits. The two in the center of each jaw are the centrals, the two next to them are the middle incisors, and the two on the outside are the corners.

Incisors grab and cut grass and hay. When a horse grazes, the top and bottom incisors meet and bite off the food. The angle where they meet changes as the horse gets older, and it is one clue used to estimate age.

Each incisor has a cup, a small dip in the middle of the biting surface. The cup slowly wears away as the teeth grind together over the years. Whether the cup is there, and its size and shape, is a strong clue to a horse's age.

The three pairs of incisors — centrals, middle incisors, and corners — come in on a known timeline. Centrals come first, then middle incisors, then corners. This order helps estimate the age of young horses.

The cheek teeth are the big grinding teeth at the back of the mouth. They do most of the chewing, breaking hay, grass, and grain into small bits the horse can digest. Most tooth problems that affect eating happen in the cheek teeth.

Premolars and Molars

The cheek teeth come in two groups: premolars and molars. Premolars sit closer to the front, just behind the gap where the bit rests. Molars sit farther back. Together they make one long grinding surface on each side of the mouth. There are three premolars and three molars per side of each jaw, for 24 cheek teeth in all.

The cheek teeth have wide, ridged tops that crush tough forage into small bits, like grinding stones. Horses chew side to side. Because of this, the outer edges of the top cheek teeth and the inner edges of the bottom cheek teeth wear down more slowly and can form sharp points over time. These points can press on the cheek or tongue and hurt, which is why regular floating is helpful.

You can see the incisors by lifting the lip, but the cheek teeth are harder to check. They need a full mouth exam with a speculum, a tool that holds the mouth open. A handler can only see so much from outside, so a vet or equine dentist is needed to check the cheek teeth.

The cheek teeth do most of the grinding. Sharp points on the outer edges of the top cheek teeth are the most common thing found during dental checkups, and they are the reason floating is done regularly.

Besides incisors and cheek teeth, horses may have canine teeth and wolf teeth. Not every horse has them. They do different things, and wolf teeth are often removed because of where they sit.

Canine Teeth (Tushes)

Canine teeth, also called tushes, are small, pointed teeth. They sit in the gap between the incisors and the premolars. They are most common in male horses. Most mares either have no canines or only very small ones.

Canine teeth do not help a horse chew. They are likely left over from long-ago horses that used them to fight or defend themselves. In males, they usually come in around age four or five. They can build up tartar, so they should be checked at dental visits.

Wolf Teeth

Wolf teeth are small teeth that sit just in front of the first cheek tooth, in or near the gap where the bit rests. Over time they have become very small. Not every horse has them. A horse may have two, one, or none. Both mares and males can have them.

Wolf teeth are often removed in riding horses because they sit where the bit presses. If the bit touches a wolf tooth, it can hurt and make the horse fight the bit. Taking them out is a simple job, usually done by a vet during a dental visit while the horse is sedated.

Wolf teeth are not the same as canine teeth. Wolf teeth are small teeth near where the bit rests, and they are often removed. Canine teeth sit in the gap between the front and cheek teeth and are most common in male horses. Knowing the difference helps avoid mix-ups.

At the Barn

A horse that suddenly fights the bit — tossing its head, refusing contact, or tensing its jaw — may have a tooth problem, not a training problem. Wolf teeth near the bit, sharp points on the cheek teeth, or canine teeth with tartar can all be the cause. The first step is a dental check by a vet or equine dentist, before changing how the horse is trained.

Things to Remember

  • Incisors (front teeth) grab and cut forage. Each has a cup in the biting surface that wears away with age.
  • Cheek teeth (premolars and molars) are the large grinding teeth that process hay and grain.
  • Sharp enamel points develop on the edges of cheek teeth and are smoothed by floating during routine dental care.
  • Canine teeth (tushes) sit in the interdental space and are most common in male horses.
  • Wolf teeth are small teeth near where the bit rests, and they are often removed in riding horses.
