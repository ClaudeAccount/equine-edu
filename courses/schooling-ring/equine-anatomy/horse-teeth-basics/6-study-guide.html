Key Concepts

What are the two main functional groups of equine teeth, and what does each group do?

Incisors (the six upper and six lower front teeth) grasp and cut forage. Cheek teeth (premolars and molars) grind food into fine particles the digestive system can process. Incisors are used to bite off grass; cheek teeth do most of the actual chewing work.

What is the interdental space, and why is it important in ridden horses?

The interdental space is the gap between the incisors and cheek teeth where no teeth are present. In ridden horses, the bit rests in this space. Wolf teeth or dental problems in the surrounding area can cause pain when the bit applies pressure, leading to bit resistance or head tossing.

What is floating, and why is it performed regularly?

Floating is the process of using a rasp to smooth sharp enamel points on the cheek teeth. Because horses chew with a side-to-side motion, the outer edges of the upper cheek teeth and inner edges of the lower cheek teeth wear more slowly, forming sharp points over time. These points can press painfully against the cheek or tongue. Routine floating (typically annually or biannually) prevents this from affecting eating comfort and behavior.

What is quidding, and what does it indicate?

Quidding is the dropping of partially chewed feed — the horse forms a ball of hay or grain, chews incompletely, and lets it fall from the mouth rather than swallowing. It is a common behavioral sign of dental discomfort, indicating that chewing is painful or mechanically inefficient. A veterinarian or equine dental provider should evaluate the mouth to identify the cause.

At what age does a horse have its full permanent mouth of incisors?

About five years of age. The permanent centrals come in at 2½ years, the permanent middle incisors at 3½ years, and the permanent corners at 4½ years. By age five, all six permanent incisors have erupted and are in full occlusion — the upper and lower teeth are meeting and wearing together.

What are caps, and when are they a concern?

Caps are retained shells of the temporary (deciduous) teeth that sit loosely over the erupting permanent teeth. In most cases, caps fall off naturally as the permanent tooth pushes them free. Occasionally a cap is retained longer than normal, causing discomfort and potentially interfering with the eruption of the permanent tooth. A veterinarian may remove a retained cap if it is causing a problem.

What is the cup (infundibulum), and how is it used in age estimation?

The cup — formally called the infundibulum — is a depression in the center of each incisor's biting surface, present when the tooth first erupts. As the tooth wears down from use, the cup becomes shallower and eventually disappears. Because the cup wears away from the lower centrals first and progresses through the middle incisors and corners in a predictable sequence (on both upper and lower jaws), its presence or absence in each position provides an age indicator from approximately five to twelve years of age.

Where does Galvayne's groove appear, and what is its age significance?

Galvayne's groove is a longitudinal groove on the outer surface of the upper corner incisor. It appears at the gumline at approximately ten years of age, extends halfway down the tooth by approximately fifteen years, reaches the full length by approximately twenty years, then disappears progressively from the top — half gone by approximately twenty-five years, and fully gone by approximately thirty years.

How do canine teeth differ from wolf teeth in horses?

Canine teeth (tushes) are pointed teeth in the interdental space, most common in male horses, and serve no practical chewing function. Wolf teeth are small vestigial premolars that sit just in front of the first functional premolar, in or near the bit contact area. Wolf teeth are commonly removed in ridden horses because bit pressure on them can cause pain. Canine teeth are generally left in place unless they accumulate excessive tartar. The two should not be confused.

Why is dental age estimation an approximation rather than a precise figure?

Multiple features — cup wear, dental star, angle of occlusion, Galvayne's groove — must be assessed together, and the rate of change varies between individuals due to diet, feed texture, environment, and genetics. Even experienced evaluators acknowledge a margin of error of one to three years in horses over fifteen. Dental assessment provides a useful estimate but should be combined with documentation when precise age is required.

Key Vocabulary

Incisors

The twelve front teeth — six upper and six lower — used to grasp and cut forage. Divided into pairs by position: centrals, middle incisors, and corners on each jaw. The incisors are the teeth most commonly examined when estimating a horse's age.

Cheek teeth (premolars and molars)

The large grinding teeth at the back of the mouth. Premolars sit closer to the front, immediately behind the interdental space; molars sit further back. Together they form a continuous grinding surface on each side of the mouth. Most dental problems affecting eating comfort occur in the cheek teeth.

Wolf teeth

Small, vestigial first premolars that sit in or near the interdental space where the bit rests. Not present in all horses. Commonly removed in horses used for riding because bit contact with wolf teeth can cause pain and bit resistance. Different from canine teeth.

Infundibulum (cup)

The depression in the center of each incisor's biting surface. Present when the tooth erupts and wears progressively with age. Its presence or absence in each incisor position is one of the primary features used in dental age estimation.

Galvayne's groove

A longitudinal groove on the outer surface of the upper corner incisor. Appears at the gumline at approximately ten years of age and progresses toward the bottom of the tooth over the following decades. One of the most recognized age indicators in horses over ten years old.

Deciduous teeth (milk teeth)

The temporary teeth present from birth that are progressively replaced by permanent teeth during the first five years. Deciduous incisors are smaller, whiter, and have a more constricted neck than permanent teeth. They are shed as permanent teeth push up from below.

Floating

The dental procedure of using a rasp to file down sharp enamel points on the cheek teeth. Performed by a veterinarian or qualified equine dental provider, typically annually or biannually. Prevents the pain and chewing inefficiency caused by sharp edges pressing against the cheek or tongue.

Dental star

A yellowish mark that appears on the incisor biting surface as the cup wears away, representing the exposed pulp cavity. It first appears as a thin line in front of the cup, then becomes a more rounded mark as wear continues. The dental star appearance and changing shape is a secondary age indicator used alongside cup assessment.

Common Mistakes

Confusing wolf teeth with canine teeth

Wolf teeth are small vestigial premolars near the bit contact area — they are commonly removed. Canine teeth (tushes) are pointed teeth in the interdental space most common in male horses — they are generally left in place. The two are different tooth types in different locations. Knowing which is which matters when interpreting dental exam findings.

Assuming bit resistance always means a training problem

Bit resistance — head tossing, jaw tension, reluctance to accept contact — has dental causes as well as training causes. Wolf teeth in the bit contact area, sharp cheek tooth points, and other dental issues can all produce bit-related behavior changes. A dental evaluation should be completed before concluding a behavior issue is purely training-related.

Thinking incisors do the grinding

Incisors grasp and cut forage at the front of the mouth; the grinding is done by the cheek teeth (premolars and molars) at the back of the mouth. A horse that is not chewing efficiently is likely having a problem with the cheek teeth, not the incisors — which is part of why cheek tooth problems are harder to detect from simple visual observation outside the stall.

Treating dental age estimation as a precise measurement

Aging a horse by its teeth is an estimate with a margin of error that increases with age. Dental features provide a useful approximation, but individual variation in wear rate, feed type, and dental structure means two horses of the same age can look different and two horses with similar dental appearance can be different ages. Documentation (papers, microchip) is the accurate source; dental assessment complements it.

Assuming a horse with dental problems will show obvious pain

Horses often continue to eat and behave relatively normally even with significant dental problems. Dental pain is frequently "silent" — the horse adapts by changing how it chews, drops feed gradually, or loses weight slowly. Routine dental exams catch problems that behavioral observation alone would miss.

Horse Bowl Focus

How many incisors does an adult horse have?

12 — six on the upper jaw and six on the lower jaw, arranged in pairs: centrals, middle incisors, and corners on each jaw.

What is the term for temporary (baby) teeth in horses?

Deciduous teeth, also called milk teeth or temporary teeth. They are replaced by permanent teeth during the horse's first five years of life.

At what age do the permanent central incisors typically erupt?

About 2½ years of age. Permanent middle incisors follow at 3½ years; permanent corners at 4½ years.

What is the infundibulum?

The cup — a depression in the center of the biting surface of each incisor, present when the tooth erupts and wearing progressively with age. Also called the cup. Its presence or absence in each incisor position is used in dental age estimation.

At approximately what age does Galvayne's groove first appear?

Approximately ten years of age, at the gumline of the upper corner incisor.

What is the interdental space?

The gap between the incisors and cheek teeth where no teeth are present. The bit rests in this space in a ridden horse. Wolf teeth can be found in or near this area.

What are wolf teeth, and why are they commonly removed?

Wolf teeth are small, vestigial first premolars that sit in or near the interdental space where the bit rests. They are removed in ridden horses because bit contact with wolf teeth can cause pain and bit resistance.

What is the term for retained temporary teeth that sit over the erupting permanent tooth?

Caps. They usually fall off naturally but can occasionally be retained and require removal by a veterinarian.
