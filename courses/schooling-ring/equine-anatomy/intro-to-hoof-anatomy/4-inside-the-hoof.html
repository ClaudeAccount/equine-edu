Hoof Anatomy

Inside the Hoof

The outside and underside are only part of the story. Inside the tough hoof capsule are bones and soft tissues that carry the horse's weight and soak up the shock of every step.

A Living Structure, Not a Shell

The hoof is much more than a hard outer shell. Inside it sit bones, a stretchy set of tissues, and a springy pad that all work together. They support the horse, keep the bones in place, and cushion the leg as the hoof hits the ground.

This lesson looks at the main parts a beginner should know by name. The goal is not to diagnose problems, but to picture what is happening inside the hoof and to describe it with the right words.

💡 Did You Know

A horse carries about 60% of its weight on its front feet. That is one reason front-foot soundness matters so much, and why the structures inside the hoof have to be so strong for their size.

The Bones Inside the Hoof

Two main bones sit inside the hoof capsule. They carry weight and help the lower leg move smoothly.

Coffin Bone

The Main Foot Bone

The coffin bone is the largest bone inside the hoof. It is shaped a little like a small hoof itself and carries most of the horse's weight down onto the ground.

Navicular Bone

A Small Helper Bone

The navicular bone is a small bone that sits behind the coffin bone, near the heel. It works like a pulley, giving a tendon a smooth surface to slide across.

Deep Digital Flexor Tendon

The Pulling Cord

This strong tendon runs down the back of the leg, slides over the navicular bone, and attaches to the coffin bone. It helps the hoof push off the ground with each stride.

The Soft Tissues Inside

Soft tissues hold the bones in place and cushion the hoof. They are just as important as the bones.

Laminae

The Velcro Layer

The laminae are thin, interlocking sheets of tissue that connect the coffin bone to the inside of the hoof wall. They grip like two sides of Velcro and hold the bone in place inside the capsule.

Digital Cushion

The Built-In Shock Pad

The digital cushion is a thick, springy pad in the heel area. It squashes a little with every step, soaking up shock before it can travel up the leg.

Sensitive Inner Layers

Where Feeling and Blood Are

Just inside the hard wall and sole are soft, sensitive layers full of blood vessels and nerves. They feed the hoof and grow new horn, which is why an injury that reaches them is serious.

The white line, seen on the underside, marks where the hard outer wall meets these sensitive inner structures. It is the thin barrier that keeps dirt and germs out of the living parts of the hoof.

How Everything Works Together

The hoof is not stiff. It flexes a little with every step, and that movement does important work.

1

The Hoof Spreads and Flexes

As the foot lands, the back of the hoof spreads slightly and the heels flex. This small movement is how the hoof begins to absorb the force of the step.

2

The Cushion Soaks Up Shock

As the hoof flexes, the digital cushion presses down and squashes. It works like a built-in shock pad, protecting the bones, joints, and tendons higher up the leg.

3

The Frog Pumps Blood

When the frog presses against the ground, it squeezes blood vessels inside the hoof. This helps push blood back up the leg, almost like a second heart for the lower leg.

Because the hoof works by flexing, a hoof that is dry, cracked, or poorly trimmed cannot absorb shock as well. Good shape and balanced moisture help the whole system work the way it should.

Growth and Function

The hoof is always growing, which is why it needs regular care.

New hoof wall grows downward from the coronary band, the soft ring at the top of the hoof where the hair meets the horn. It grows slowly, about one centimeter each month, so a whole new hoof wall takes the better part of a year to grow down. Because the wall keeps growing, the hoof needs trimming by a farrier every few weeks to stay balanced and the right shape.

Hoof horn is also about one-quarter water. In very dry weather it can become brittle and crack, and in very wet conditions it can grow soft. Horses kept on footing that is neither too wet nor too dry tend to grow stronger, healthier hooves.

In the Barn

Knowing the inside of the hoof makes daily care more meaningful. When picking out a foot, a handler who knows where the white line sits can notice early signs of trouble, such as crumbly horn along its edge. Feeling for unusual heat in the wall, noticing a softer or firmer heel, or spotting swelling at the coronary band are all observations that make more sense once the inside of the hoof is understood. Cleaning out a hoof is a quick health check, not just a chore.

Things to Remember

  • The coffin bone is the main weight-bearing bone inside the hoof.
  • The navicular bone is a small bone near the heel that works like a pulley.
  • The laminae hold the coffin bone to the hoof wall like interlocking Velcro.
  • The digital cushion is a springy pad in the heel that absorbs shock.
  • The hoof flexes with each step, and the frog helps pump blood up the leg.
  • The hoof wall grows down from the coronary band and needs regular trimming.
