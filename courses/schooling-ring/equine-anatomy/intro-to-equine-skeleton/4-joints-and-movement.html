Intro to the Equine Skeleton

Joints and Movement

The bones of a horse are connected by joints. These joints act like hinges and sliding surfaces that allow movement while also helping support weight. Most lameness problems in horses involve joints or the tissues around them.

Where Bones Meet

Joints are where bones meet and allow movement. Different joints allow different types of motion, including hinge, gliding, and limited rotational movement. Inside each joint:

  • Cartilage covers bone ends
  • Synovial fluid reduces friction
  • A joint capsule holds the structure together

Healthy joints allow smooth movement. When joints are inflamed or damaged, movement becomes stiff, uneven, or painful.

💡 Did You Know

Horses have a special system called the stay apparatus. This system of muscles, tendons, and ligaments allows a horse to "lock" its legs and stand while using very little energy. It even allows horses to sleep while standing. In the hindlimb, this involves a locking mechanism at the stifle joint.

The forelimb carries about 60% of a horse's weight, so its joints take considerable stress during movement and work.

Shoulder Joint

Connects scapula to humerus. Allows forward and backward motion.

Elbow Joint

Connects humerus to radius/ulna. Functions as a hinge joint.

Carpus

Known as the knee in horses. Acts like the wrist and absorbs shock.

Fetlock, Pastern, and Coffin Joints

  • Fetlock — cannon to P1
  • Pastern — P1 to P2
  • Coffin — P2 to P3

These joints absorb impact and are common sites of strain. The navicular bone sits behind the coffin joint and works closely with the deep digital flexor tendon.

The same joint pattern found in the forelimb below the knee is repeated in the hindlimb below the hock.

The hindlimbs are the main source of power and propulsion. The stifle and hock are the two hindlimb joints most often evaluated in performance horses.

Hip (Coxofemoral Joint)

Connects femur to pelvis. A deep ball-and-socket joint not visible from the outside.

Stifle

Equivalent to the human knee. A complex joint involved in movement and the stay apparatus. Includes the patella (kneecap), menisci, and stabilizing ligaments.

Hock (Tarsus)

Equivalent to the ankle. Critical for propulsion and power. Below the hock, the hindlimb follows the same structure as the forelimb: fetlock, pastern, and coffin joints.

The hock and stifle are the two hindlimb joints most often evaluated in athletic horses. One of the most common hindlimb issues is bone spavin — arthritis in the lower hock joints.

Every stride is a coordinated chain of joint movement. Understanding how joints work together — and what happens when they are stressed — is the practical goal of learning equine skeletal anatomy.

How Joints Work Together

During movement:

  • Shoulder and hip initiate stride
  • Elbow and stifle coordinate forward motion
  • Carpus and hock absorb impact and provide push-off
  • Fetlock, pastern, and coffin joints manage ground contact

Common Joint Conditions

Arthritis. Slow breakdown of cartilage inside a joint. Leads to stiffness and reduced motion.

Joint Effusion. Fluid buildup in or around a joint. Always indicates inflammation.

Examples:

  • Bog spavin — hock swelling
  • Windpuffs — fetlock swelling
  • Bone spavin — arthritis in lower hock joints

Any joint swelling should be noted and evaluated.

Fluid buildup (effusion) around a joint always warrants veterinary evaluation — it is a sign that the joint capsule is inflamed, even if the horse is not yet visibly lame.

At the Barn

During routine checks, handlers look for:

  • Heat
  • Swelling
  • Sensitivity to touch
  • Changes in movement

Noticing small changes early helps identify joint issues before they become severe.

Things to Remember

  • A joint is where two bones meet and move.
  • Cartilage, synovial fluid, and joint capsules support movement.
  • The fetlock is one of the most commonly injured joints.
  • The stifle and hock are key hindlimb joints.
  • Joint swelling always indicates inflammation.
