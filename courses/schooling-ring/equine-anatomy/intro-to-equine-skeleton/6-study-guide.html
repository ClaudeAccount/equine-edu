Key Concepts

What is the difference between the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton?

The axial skeleton is the horse's central framework. It includes the skull, spine (vertebral column), ribs, and sternum. Its job is to support the body and protect vital organs like the brain, spinal cord, heart, and lungs.

The appendicular skeleton includes the limbs, plus the shoulder blades and pelvis where the limbs attach to the body. This part is responsible for movement and carrying weight.

Most lameness problems happen in the appendicular skeleton, especially in the lower limbs.

Why does the horse have no collarbone, and what does that mean?

Horses do not have a collarbone (clavicle). Instead, the forelimb is attached to the body only by muscles, tendons, and ligaments. There is no bone connecting the scapula to the ribcage. This creates a flexible "muscle sling" that:

  • absorbs shock
  • allows long, free movement of the shoulder
  • supports forward motion
What forms the withers, and why are they important?

The withers are formed by the tall spines of the thoracic vertebrae (especially the middle ones). They are important because:

  • they are the highest point of the horse's body
  • they are used to measure a horse's height
  • they are important for saddle fit and balance
What is the bone sequence in the forelimb below the knee?

From top to bottom:

  • Cannon bone
  • Long pastern (P1)
  • Short pastern (P2)
  • Coffin bone (P3)

The navicular bone sits behind the coffin joint inside the hoof.

What are the major forelimb joints from top to bottom?
  • Shoulder joint
  • Elbow joint
  • Carpus (knee)
  • Fetlock joint
  • Pastern joint
  • Coffin joint

The fetlock is one of the most common sites of injury in horses.

What is the stifle joint, and what is its human equivalent?

The stifle is the large joint between the femur and tibia. It is equivalent to the human knee and includes:

  • the patella (kneecap)
  • cartilage structures (menisci)
  • strong stabilizing ligaments

It also helps lock the leg for standing as part of the stay apparatus.

What is the hock, and what is its human equivalent?

The hock (tarsus) is the major joint in the hindlimb between the tibia and cannon bone. It is equivalent to the human ankle and is made of several small bones. The point of the hock is the calcaneus, similar to the human heel bone.

What is joint effusion, and why does it matter?

Joint effusion is swelling caused by fluid inside or around a joint. It matters because it means:

  • the joint is inflamed
  • something is not normal
  • it may progress to pain or lameness

Even without obvious lameness, it should be checked by a veterinarian.

What is the stay apparatus, and why is it important?

The stay apparatus is a system of muscles and tendons that allows a horse to lock its legs and stand with very little effort. It allows horses to:

  • sleep while standing
  • conserve energy
  • react quickly to danger

In the hindlimb, the patella plays a key role in locking the stifle.

How many bones does the adult horse have?

About 205 bones, slightly fewer than humans. Some bones are fused together to create strength and support for speed and weight-bearing.

Key Vocabulary

Scapula

Shoulder blade. It is held in place by muscles, not a joint, and forms the shoulder with the humerus.

Carpus

The horse's "knee," but anatomically the same as the human wrist.

Cannon Bone

The large main bone in the lower limb (below knee or hock), flanked by splint bones.

Coffin Bone (P3)

The lowest bone inside the hoof capsule.

Fetlock Joint

Joint between the cannon bone and P1. A very common site of lameness.

Tarsus (Hock)

The hindlimb joint equivalent to the human ankle.

Navicular Bone

Small bone behind the coffin joint inside the hoof, important in forelimb soundness.

Patella

Kneecap at the front of the stifle. Helps with the stay apparatus.

Common Mistakes

Thinking the carpus is the same as the human knee

The carpus is actually the wrist, not the knee. The horse's knee is the stifle.

Confusing the hock and stifle
  • Hock = ankle
  • Stifle = knee

They are different joints at different heights.

Ignoring joint swelling if the horse isn't lame

Joint swelling (effusion) always signals inflammation and should be checked.

Confusing splint bones with the cannon bone

Splint bones are small side bones. The cannon bone is the large central bone.

Mixing up P1, P2, and P3
  • P1 = long pastern
  • P2 = short pastern
  • P3 = coffin bone

They are always numbered from top to bottom.
