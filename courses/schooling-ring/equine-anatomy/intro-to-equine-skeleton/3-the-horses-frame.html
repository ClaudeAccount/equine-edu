Intro to the Equine Skeleton

The Horse's Frame

The equine skeleton is built like a strong framework. It has a central core called the axial skeleton, and four limbs called the appendicular skeleton that support weight and create movement. Learning the major bones in each area helps with understanding anatomy, lameness, and conformation.

Organizing the Skeleton

The equine skeleton is divided into two main sections:

  • Axial skeleton — skull, spine, ribs, and sternum
  • Appendicular skeleton — limbs, shoulder, and pelvis

The axial skeleton forms the central support of the body and protects vital organs. The appendicular skeleton is responsible for movement and weight-bearing. Most soundness and lameness issues occur in the limbs, especially below the knee or hock.

💡 Did You Know

Horses do not have a collarbone. Instead, the forelimb is held to the body only by muscles, tendons, and ligaments. This creates a flexible "sling" that helps absorb shock and allows smooth forward motion.

The axial skeleton is the central frame — the skull, spine, ribs, and sternum. It houses and protects the brain, spinal cord, and major organs, and provides the anchor points for the neck, back, and limb muscles.

Skull and Mandible

The skull protects the brain and supports the teeth and nasal structures. The mandible (lower jaw) moves separately and allows chewing. The jaw meets the skull at the temporomandibular joint.

Vertebral Column (Spine)

The spine runs from head to tail and is divided into sections:

  • Cervical — 7 vertebrae (neck movement)
  • Thoracic — 18 vertebrae (ribcage and withers)
  • Lumbar — 6 vertebrae (loin area)
  • Sacrum — fused vertebrae (pelvic connection)
  • Coccygeal — tail vertebrae

Ribs and Sternum

Horses have 18 pairs of ribs:

  • First 8 pairs attach directly to the sternum
  • Remaining ribs attach through cartilage

The ribcage protects the heart and lungs and supports breathing muscles.

The withers are the raised area between the neck and back. They are formed by the long spines of the thoracic vertebrae. This is also the point used to measure a horse's height from the ground.

The forelimbs support a large portion of the horse's body weight and absorb impact during movement.

Shoulder to Knee

Scapula. The shoulder blade held by muscles, not a joint.

Humerus. The upper forelimb bone.

Radius and Ulna. The forearm; the ulna is fused to the radius.

Carpus. Commonly called the knee, but functions like the wrist.

Cannon to Hoof

Cannon bone. The main weight-bearing bone of the lower limb.

Splint bones. Support bones alongside the cannon.

From the cannon bone down, the limb follows a consistent bone pattern:

  • P1 (long pastern)
  • P2 (short pastern)
  • P3 (coffin bone)

Navicular bone. Sits behind the coffin bone inside the hoof area and plays an important role in movement and support.

From the knee to the hoof, the forelimb follows this order: cannon → P1 (long pastern) → P2 (short pastern) → P3 (coffin bone), with the navicular bone located behind the coffin joint. This same bone pattern is also found in the hindlimb from the hock downward.

The hindlimbs provide propulsion and power for forward movement. The lower hindlimb follows the same bone pattern as the lower forelimb.

Pelvis to Stifle

Pelvis. Fused ilium, ischium, and pubis. Connects the hindlimb to the spine at the sacrum.

Femur. The upper thigh bone connecting the pelvis to the stifle joint.

Stifle joint. Equivalent to the human knee. Complex joint involved in movement and the stay apparatus.

Tibia and fibula. Form the gaskin; the fibula is reduced and fused to the tibia.

Hock to Hoof

Hock (tarsus). Equivalent to the human ankle. Critical for propulsion and power.

Below the hock, the hindlimb follows the same bone pattern as the forelimb:

  • Cannon bone (metatarsal)
  • Splint bones
  • P1, P2, P3
  • Navicular bone

The fetlock, pastern, and coffin joints in the hindlimb match the same structure found in the forelimb.

Joint equivalents: the hock is similar to the human ankle, and the stifle is similar to the human knee.

At the Barn

When evaluating lameness, veterinarians often narrow the location to a specific structure:

  • Coffin bone or coffin joint — inside the hoof
  • Navicular region — back of the hoof area
  • Hock (tarsus) — hindlimb joint of propulsion

Recognizing these terms helps connect visible signs with internal structures.

Things to Remember

  • The skeleton is divided into axial and appendicular systems.
  • The forelimb is attached by soft tissue, not bone.
  • The carpus is the wrist, not the knee.
  • The stifle is the knee equivalent in the hindlimb.
  • The hock is the ankle equivalent.
  • Most lower limb structures follow the same bone pattern.
