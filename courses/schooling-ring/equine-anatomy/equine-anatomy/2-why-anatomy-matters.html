Parts of the Horse

Why Anatomy Matters

Knowing the names of a horse's body parts is one of the first and most useful skills in horsemanship. These terms come up every day around horses.

The Language of Horses

Learning the parts of the horse is one of the first useful things a beginner can do. Every horse has dozens of named parts, and each name points to an exact place on the body. These terms come up in barns, riding lessons, vet visits, and everyday care.

Knowing where these parts are, and what they are called, makes it possible to follow instructions, ask clearer questions, and understand what is happening with a horse. This course builds that working vocabulary one region at a time, starting with the basics and adding detail as it goes.

💡 Did You Know

Many horse terms are very old. Words like “withers,” “croup,” and “gaskin” appear in riding books from the 1500s with almost the same meaning they carry today. Clear language has always mattered because it affects a horse's care and safety.

Where These Terms Come Up

Knowing the parts of the horse makes each of these everyday situations easier to handle.

Horse Care

Grooming and Daily Handling

From brushing the withers to picking out the hooves, daily care involves specific parts of the body. Knowing the names helps a person follow instructions and notice anything out of the ordinary.

Health

Vet and Farrier Visits

Vets and farriers use exact terms when they examine a horse. Understanding words like hock, cannon bone, or coronet helps an owner stay informed about a horse's health.

Riding

Lessons and Training

Instructors give directions based on parts of the horse. Knowing where the poll, the shoulder, and the hindquarters are makes it easier to understand what a lesson is asking for.

Communication

Talking About Horses

Describing a horse, reporting an injury, or reading a breed standard all rely on correct terms. These are the shared words horse people everywhere use.

How the Horse Is Organized

The horse's body is divided into four main regions. This course covers each region in its own tab, so the vocabulary can be built one section at a time.

Region 1

The Head

From the poll between the ears down to the muzzle and jaw. This region holds terms such as the forelock, throatlatch, and chin groove — the features seen when looking a horse in the face.

Region 2

Neck and Shoulder

The crest, jugular groove, point of shoulder, and chest. The withers mark where the neck meets the back and are an important point for saddle fit and movement.

Region 3

The Body

The back, loin, croup, barrel, flank, and heart girth. The body region has more named structures than most people expect, and they come up often in descriptions of build and condition.

Region 4

The Legs

From the elbow and stifle down to the hoof. The legs carry a dense set of terms — every joint and bony landmark on both the front and hind legs.

There is no need to memorize everything at once. The goal is a strong, working vocabulary — the kind that comes naturally after these terms have been seen and used a few times.
A horse has no muscles below the knee or hock, only tendons, ligaments, and bone. This keeps the lower leg light for movement, but tendon and ligament injuries there can be slow to heal.
