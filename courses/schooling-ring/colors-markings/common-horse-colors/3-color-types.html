Common Horse Colors

Horse coat colors can seem confusing at first. They get easier once you know what to look for. This lesson covers five common color groups used to identify horses.

Common Coat Colors

Most coat colors share features that let us group them together. To identify a color, compare three things: the body color, the mane and tail color, and the leg color. Use the tabs below to explore the most common horse colors and their groups.

The Base Coat Colors

Chestnut, black, and bay are the three basic horse coat colors. Most other coat colors come from these three colors with changes in shade or pattern.

The term points refers to a horse’s mane, tail, and lower legs. The color of the points can help with identifying coat colors.

Reddish-Brown

Chestnut

Chestnut horses have a warm reddish-brown coat. Their mane and tail are usually the same color as the body or lighter shades of red. Chestnut horses do not have any black on their bodies, legs, manes, or tails (no black points).

Some chestnut horses may have a lighter, cream-colored mane and tail called flaxen. Even with flaxen coloring, the coat is still considered chestnut because the body stays in the red color range.

Brown Body, Black Points

Bay

Bay horses have a body color that ranges from reddish-brown to dark brown. These horses have black manes, tails, and lower legs (black points). The black points are what set bay apart from chestnut, since chestnut horses have no black on the body.

Solid Black

Black

Black horses have a solid black coat with a black mane, tail, and lower legs. The whole body is black, with no brown or red tones.

Some black horses look sun-bleached. The coat can fade to a rusty or brownish tone in spots that get a lot of sun, while shaded areas stay darker. This fading does not change the horse’s color. The horse is still black.

Golden & Lightened Colors

Palomino and buckskin horses can both look golden. The best way to tell them apart is to look at the mane, tail, and leg color.

Golden Colors

Golden Body, White Mane

Palomino

Palomino horses have a golden body that ranges from pale cream-gold to rich honey gold. The mane and tail are white or very light. The light mane and tail help identify a palomino.

Golden Body, Black Points

Buckskin

Buckskin horses have a golden or tan body with a black mane, tail, and lower legs (black points). The black points help tell a buckskin apart from a palomino, which has a light mane and tail.

Palomino vs. Buckskin: Both are golden, so look at the mane, tail, and legs. White or cream mane and tail means palomino. Black mane, tail, and legs means buckskin.

Gray Changes Over Time

Gray horses are not born gray. They are born another coat color and slowly lighten as they get older. The same horse may look nearly black as a foal, show dapples when young, and turn almost white as it ages.

This slow change is what makes gray different from other colors. Because a gray horse's look changes over time, it helps to learn its stages instead of one fixed look.

💡 Important Distinction

Gray horses are often mistaken for white, but there is an easy way to tell them apart. A gray horse is born another color and slowly lightens over time. A white horse is born white and stays white for life.

Skin color is a helpful clue. Gray horses have dark skin under the coat. True white horses have pink skin, which is often easy to see around the muzzle and other thin-haired areas.

How Gray Horses Change Over Time

Gray horses change in a steady pattern as they age. Learning the stages makes them easier to recognize.

Foal / Young Horse
Base Color Visible

Born with their base color, such as black, bay, or chestnut. Gray hairs show up around the eyes and muzzle first.

Young Adult
Dappling Stage

Dapples, which are rings of lighter and darker gray, often appear across the body as the coat lightens unevenly.

Mature / Older Horse
Light Grey or White

The coat keeps lightening until it looks mostly gray or nearly white. It may show flea-bitten speckles in later years.

Gray Coat Variations

Mixed White & Colored Hairs

Gray

A mix of white and colored hairs across the body gives an overall gray look. The shade can range from dark steel gray to near-white, depending on the horse's age.

Middle Stage

Dapple Gray

A gray horse in the dappling stage. It shows circular patterns of lighter gray ringed by slightly darker gray, often over the hindquarters and back. Dapples usually fade as the horse keeps lightening.

Later Stage

Flea-Bitten Gray

A mostly white or light gray coat flecked with small specks of colored hairs, usually chestnut, bay, or gray. These "flea bites" often show up in older gray horses after the coat has lightened almost completely.

Early Stage

Rose Gray

A gray horse in the early stages of lightening over a chestnut or reddish base coat. The result is a warm pinkish-gray tone that looks rosy before the coat lightens further with age.

What Is a Roan?

A roan horse has white hairs evenly mixed through the body coat. This gives a blended look over a solid base color. Unlike gray horses, roans do not keep lightening with age. The amount of white can shift a little with the season, coat length, and age.

Roan colors are named after their base coat, such as red roan, blue roan, and bay roan. The pattern is the same in each one. The body shows an even mix of white and colored hairs, while the head, mane, tail, and lower legs stay more solid in color.

💡 Roan vs. Gray

Roan and gray horses are easy to mix up. Both have white hairs mixed into a colored coat, but they change differently over time.

A roan horse keeps about the same mix of white and colored hairs for life. The head, mane, tail, and lower legs stay more solid in color.

A gray horse lightens as it ages. White hairs slowly spread across the whole body, including the head, until the first coat color often can no longer be seen.

Roan Coat Types

Roan colors are named for the horse’s body color underneath. This is the base coat that sits below the white hairs mixed through the coat.

Chestnut Base

Strawberry Roan

A roan pattern over a chestnut or sorrel base coat. White hairs are evenly mixed through the body, giving a pinkish-red look. The head, mane, tail, and lower legs stay solid chestnut.

Black Base

Blue Roan

A roan pattern over a black base coat. White hairs are evenly mixed through the body, giving a bluish or smoky look. The head, mane, tail, and lower legs stay solid black with little or no white.

Bay Base

Bay Roan

A roan pattern over a bay base coat. White hairs are evenly mixed through the body, giving a soft reddish-brown look. The head, mane, tail, and lower legs stay solid black, like a bay.

Telling Them Apart: The base color sets the roan type. Chestnut base = strawberry roan (no black points). Black base = blue roan (black head and points). Bay base = bay roan (black mane, tail, and legs with a roan body). The white-hair mixing looks the same in all three.

Key Features of Roan Coats

White Hair Mixing

White hairs are spread evenly through the body coat. They are not in patches. They are mixed steadily across the body over the base color.

Solid Head & Points

The head, mane, tail, and lower legs usually stay solid in the base color with little or no white. This helps tell roan apart from gray.

Stable Color

Unlike gray horses, roans do not keep lightening with age. The coat pattern stays about the same for the horse’s life.

Winter Darkening

Some roans look a little darker in winter and lighter in summer as the coat grows in and sheds out. This is a seasonal change, not a lasting change in coat color.

Patterned Coats

Pinto and Appaloosa are two coat patterns that stand out. Both are markings over a base coat, not colors on their own.

Pinto has large white patches over a colored coat. Appaloosa has spotting patterns, often grouped over the hindquarters or spread across the body.

Pinto horses have large, uneven white patches over a colored coat. The two most common patterns, tobiano and overo, differ in where the white sits on the body.

The easiest way to tell them apart is where the white patches are and whether they cross over the horse’s back.

Pinto Pattern

Tobiano

Tobiano is a pinto pattern. The white patches are usually smooth and rounded, and they often cross over the horse’s back. The legs are often white. The head is usually darker, with face markings like those on solid-colored horses.

Pinto Pattern

Overo

Overo is a pinto pattern. The white patches are uneven and usually do not cross the horse’s back. The white often starts on the sides and spreads outward. The legs are usually darker, and the face often has more white.

Tobiano vs. Overo: An easy rule: if white crosses the back (topline), it is likely tobiano. If white stays on the sides and belly without crossing the back, it is likely overo. When both patterns show up in one horse, it is called tovero.

Appaloosa horses have spotted coat patterns that are easy to recognize with practice. Besides the coat pattern, Appaloosas often show three features: speckled skin around the muzzle and eyes, white around the iris (sclera), and striped hooves.

Appaloosa Pattern

Leopard Appaloosa

A leopard Appaloosa has a white coat covered with large, dark spots all over the body. The spots can vary in size, and the coat is mostly white with dark spots that stand out.

Appaloosa Pattern

Blanket Appaloosa

A blanket Appaloosa has a dark base coat with a white "blanket" over the hips and hindquarters. The blanket may be solid white or have smaller spots. The rest of the body stays a darker base color.

Identifying Appaloosa Features: Besides the coat pattern, look for speckled skin (uneven dark and light patches) around the muzzle, lips, and eyes; white sclera around the iris; and striped hooves. These features often show up in Appaloosa horses no matter the coat pattern.
