Horse Behavior & Communication

Advanced Body Language Body Language

Reading combined behavioral signals — posture, facial expression, tail carriage, and movement patterns — to assess emotional state and predict behavior.

In the Introduction to Horse Behavior course, behavioral signals were introduced individually. Advanced body language reading integrates those individual signals into simultaneous assessment — reading multiple channels at once and building a composite behavioral profile. This composite profile is more reliable than any single signal and enables the kind of precise situational awareness that characterizes experienced handlers.

The additional complexity at this level involves subtle signals: the tension in the muzzle, the degree of muscle engagement in the neck, the quality of movement in the tail. These signals are harder to read than ears or eye whites but often appear earlier in the escalation sequence.

Advanced Signal Reading

The Muzzle and Jaw

A tense muzzle — muscles taut around the mouth and jaw — indicates tension regardless of whether the ears or eyes are showing overt distress. A horse grinding its teeth (bruxism) during work is expressing stress or discomfort. A horse that consistently stiffens the jaw under contact with the bit is communicating resistance to that contact, often related to pain, bit fit, or training tension.

Topline and Back

A horse that inverts — lifting the head and dropping the back when a rider sits or leg is applied — is bracing against pressure, likely from pain, fear, or anticipation of discomfort. A horse that swings through the back during movement, with a relaxed topline and rhythmic stride, is moving in physical and mental comfort. Back tension is one of the earliest indicators of saddle fit problems.

Tail Carriage and Movement

A tail carried high indicates excitement or arousal. A tail clamped tight to the hindquarters indicates fear or pain. A tail that swishes rhythmically during work indicates annoyance, often in response to leg aids, flies, or saddle pressure. A tail that is tucked when the horse is standing still, particularly combined with a hunched posture, warrants immediate veterinary attention for abdominal pain.

Displacement Behaviors

Displacement behaviors — yawning, scratching, licking and chewing, pawing, shaking the head — appear when a horse is in conflict between competing impulses or is processing a stressful situation. Licking and chewing, often seen at the release of pressure in a training session, is associated with a shift from sympathetic (fight-or-flight) to parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) nervous system activity.

The most informative time to read a horse's behavioral state is before any handling begins. A horse assessed at rest — before haltering, before the girth is tightened, before the first aid — tells the handler what baseline they are working from and what the session's likely trajectory will be. Skipping this assessment is the most common error in experienced handlers.

In showmanship, judges assess the horse's responsiveness and willingness — which are direct reflections of the horse's behavioral state at the time of the class. A handler who reads their horse's pre-class state and makes appropriate adjustments (additional warm-up, reduced stimulation, or a change in approach) produces a better presentation than one who enters the ring without that assessment. Behavioral reading is a scored skill in horsemanship, even when it is not explicitly listed as a judging criterion.

Things to Remember

  • The muzzle and jaw reveal subtle stress: grinding teeth, tight lips, or a wrinkled muzzle indicate discomfort or tension before larger signals appear.
  • The topline and back tell the story of tension: a horse that is hollow-backed and braced is not mentally or physically ready to work.
  • Tail carriage reflects the horse's overall state — high and flagging = excitement/tension; clamped = fear; relaxed and swinging with movement = comfortable.
  • Displacement behaviors (yawning, scratching, shaking) may indicate anxiety being redirected — watch for patterns, not single occurrences.
