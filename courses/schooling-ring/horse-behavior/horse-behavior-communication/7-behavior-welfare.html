Behavior & Welfare Assessment

Understanding Welfare Through Behavior

Behavior is one of the earliest and most reliable indicators of a horse's welfare. Horses that are in pain, under chronic stress, or living in environments that do not meet their behavioral needs will often show changes in behavior before physical signs become obvious.

Learning to observe these changes helps handlers, riders, and caretakers recognize problems early and respond appropriately. It also improves communication between horse owners and veterinary professionals.

The Five Domains model of animal welfare includes: nutrition, physical environment, health, behavioral interactions, and mental state. The mental state domain, how the horse feels, is assessed primarily through behavior.

Welfare Indicators

Welfare Indicators

Indicator 1

Stereotypies and Repetitive Behaviors

Stereotypies are repetitive behaviors that appear to serve no clear purpose. These include weaving, cribbing, stall walking, and wood chewing.

These behaviors often develop in response to long-term stress, limited forage access, lack of social contact, or confinement. Once established, they may continue even if conditions improve.

Stereotypies are not habits or quirks. They are indicators of past or present welfare compromise. Their presence should lead to a review of management practices, not punishment of the horse.

Indicator 2

Behavioral Signs of Pain

Pain often shows up through behavior before it becomes physically obvious.

Common signs include resistance or aggression during handling, reluctance to move forward, back inversion under saddle, facial tension, uneven or altered movement, teeth grinding, and pawing or flank watching.

Tools such as the Horse Grimace Scale and other equine pain assessment systems use facial expressions and body language to help identify and measure pain.

Indicator 3

Social and Environmental Needs

Horses have core behavioral needs that must be met for good welfare: forage access, movement, social contact, and predictability.

Forage access includes grazing or near-continuous feeding. Movement includes natural daily travel and exercise. Social contact includes visual, tactile, or herd interaction. Predictability means calm, consistent daily routines.

When these needs are restricted over time, horses may show stress-related behaviors, reduced responsiveness, or stereotypic behavior.

Indicator 4

Positive Welfare Indicators

Welfare assessment is not only about identifying problems. It also includes recognizing when a horse is doing well.

Positive welfare indicators include willingness to interact with familiar people, relaxed posture during rest, play behavior, mutual grooming with other horses, calm eating behavior, curiosity, and environmental interest.

A horse that consistently shows these behaviors is likely experiencing good welfare. A horse that rarely shows them may need a closer look at management and environment.

💡 Did You Know

The Horse Grimace Scale is a scientific tool used to identify pain through facial expression. Researchers found that horses show consistent changes in the eyes, ears, and muzzle when in pain. These changes can be scored from observation or photographs, allowing pain to be assessed even when a horse is not lame or vocalizing.

Stereotypic behaviors like cribbing and weaving are strongly linked to environmental stressors such as limited forage, confinement, and social isolation. Treating the behavior alone does not resolve the issue. The underlying cause must be addressed.

Putting It All Together

Behavioral welfare assessment connects directly to daily horse management. Feeding routines, turnout, housing, social contact, and training all influence how a horse behaves.

When a horse shows changes in behavior, especially dullness, tension, or repetitive actions, it is often communicating that something in its environment or care needs attention. Effective welfare assessment focuses on adjusting management, not correcting the horse.

In the Barn

Welfare in Daily Care

A horse's welfare status can often be seen in small daily details: posture, coat condition, interest in surroundings, social interaction, and willingness to engage with people.

Changes such as withdrawal, reduced curiosity, or repetitive behaviors are important signals. These often point to unmet behavioral or physical needs.

Improving welfare usually involves changes to environment, routine, turnout, feeding, or training approach rather than behavior correction alone.

Things to Remember

  • Behavior is often the first sign of welfare changes.
  • Stereotypies are indicators of stress or unmet needs, not habits or misbehavior.
  • Pain is a common hidden cause of behavioral change and should be ruled out early.
  • The Five Domains model includes nutrition, environment, health, behavior, and mental state.
  • Positive welfare includes engagement, curiosity, and social interaction, not just the absence of problems.
