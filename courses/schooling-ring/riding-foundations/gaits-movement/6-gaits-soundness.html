Understanding Horse Movement

Gaits & Soundness Assessment

Using gait observation as a lameness screening tool: what irregularities look like, what they indicate, and when veterinary evaluation is needed.

Observation Before Diagnosis

Lameness is the most common reason horses are removed from work and the most frequent reason for veterinary consultation in performance horses. Gait observation — specifically, the ability to detect and describe irregularity in the rhythm, weight-bearing, or footfall pattern of a horse's movement — is the primary tool for early lameness screening.

The goal of soundness observation for the horseperson is not diagnosis — that is the veterinarian's role — but early detection and accurate description. A handler who catches a subtle change from baseline and can describe it precisely enables the veterinarian to reach diagnosis faster and with better information.

💡 Did You Know

The AAEP Lameness Grading Scale was developed by the American Association of Equine Practitioners to give horse owners and veterinarians a shared language for describing lameness severity. When a handler calls a vet and says “I’d call it a Grade 3 at the trot,” the vet arrives with calibrated expectations and can move directly to evaluation. That shared reference significantly reduces time spent establishing baseline observations at the examination.

Soundness Observation Framework
Concept

The Sound Horse Baseline

Detecting lameness requires knowing what a sound version of that specific horse looks like. A horse with a chronic stride asymmetry present for two years is different from one that developed the same asymmetry last week. The baseline is established through systematic observation when the horse is known to be sound — watching at walk and trot on a straight line, on a circle on both reins, and on different surfaces. Without a known baseline, subtle changes are easy to miss.

Concept

Head Bob and Pelvic Drop

The two primary visual signs of lameness at the trot are the head bob and the pelvic drop. The head bob: the head drops when the sound foreleg bears weight and rises when the lame foreleg bears weight — identifying front limb lameness. The pelvic drop: the hip on the lame side rises when that hindleg bears weight, as the horse tries to unload the painful limb — viewed from behind, this identifies hindlimb lameness.

Concept

The AAEP Lameness Grading Scale

The AAEP Lameness Grading Scale classifies lameness from 0 (sound — no lameness detected) to 5 (non-weight-bearing — the horse refuses to use the affected limb). Grades 1 and 2 are subtle and may only appear under specific conditions such as a hard surface, tight circle, or after a flexion test. Grades 3 and 4 are consistently visible at the trot. Grade 5 is a veterinary emergency. Using this scale when reporting lameness provides a calibrated shared reference point.

Concept

Flexion Tests

A flexion test involves holding a joint in maximum flexion for 60 seconds, then immediately trotting the horse straight away while observing the first several strides for exaggerated lameness. A positive flexion test — increased lameness in those strides — localizes the problem to the structures held in flexion. Flexion tests are performed by the veterinarian during a lameness examination; the horseperson's role is to understand what they reveal and assist in trotting the horse straight away after the hold.

A horse completely unwilling to bear weight on one limb requires immediate veterinary contact. The most common cause is a hoof abscess — treatable and resolving quickly with drainage. However, a fractured bone or penetrating wound to the hoof presents identically to an abscess and is a veterinary emergency. Non-weight-bearing lameness is always treated as an emergency until structural causes are ruled out.

In horse judging, soundness is assessed on a trot-up on a straight, firm surface and on a circle. Judges look for the head bob and pelvic asymmetry described above. A horse placed lower than its conformation would otherwise warrant has been assessed as having a functionally significant movement irregularity. Understanding the evaluation criteria allows a student judge to explain placings with specific observations rather than simply ranking by appearance.

In the Barn

Watching horses move at liberty in a pasture or paddock is one of the best ways to develop a trained eye for soundness and gait quality. A horse moving freely shows its natural balance, rhythm, and willingness — without the influence of a rider or the context of a performance setting. Handlers who regularly observe horses in motion build pattern recognition that makes subtle gait changes and early lameness signs easier to detect when they occur. Early identification of a gait irregularity is one of the most practically valuable skills in horse care and management.

Things to Remember

  • Head bob at trot = front limb lameness — the head drops when the sound leg bears weight and rises when the lame leg bears weight.
  • Pelvic drop = hindlimb lameness — the hip on the lame side rises when that limb bears weight; viewed from behind at the trot.
  • AAEP scale 0–5: grades 1–2 subtle, grades 3–4 consistently visible at trot, grade 5 = non-weight-bearing = veterinary emergency.
  • Non-weight-bearing lameness is always treated as a veterinary emergency until fracture and penetrating wounds are ruled out.
