Gaits & Movement

Gaits & Soundness Assessment Soundness

Using gait observation as a lameness screening tool: what irregularities look like and what they indicate.

Lameness is the most common reason horses are removed from work and the most frequent reason for veterinary consultation in performance horses. Gait observation — specifically, the ability to detect and describe irregularity in the rhythm, weight-bearing, or footfall pattern of a horse's movement — is the primary tool for lameness screening.

The goal of soundness observation for the horseperson is not diagnosis — that is the veterinarian's role — but early detection and accurate description. A handler who catches a subtle change from the previous day's baseline and can describe it precisely (which leg, at which gait, worse on which surface, improving or worsening) provides the veterinarian with information that dramatically speeds diagnosis.

Soundness Observation Framework
Concept 1

The Sound Horse Baseline

Detecting lameness requires knowing what a sound version of that specific horse looks like. A horse with a chronic asymmetry of stride that has been present for two years is different from a horse that developed the same asymmetry last week. The baseline is established by systematic observation when the horse is known to be sound — watching at walk and trot on a straight line, on a circle on both reins, and on different surfaces.

Concept 2

Head Bob and Pelvic Drop

The two primary visual signs of lameness are the head bob (the head drops when the sound leg bears weight and rises when the lame leg bears weight at the trot) and the pelvic drop (the hip on the lame side rises when that limb bears weight, as the horse tries to unload the painful limb). The head bob identifies a front limb lameness; the pelvic drop — best seen from behind — identifies a hindlimb lameness.

Concept 3

The Lameness Grading Scale

The AAEP Lameness Grading Scale classifies lameness from 0 (sound, no lameness detected) to 5 (non-weight-bearing, will not use the affected limb). Grades 1 and 2 are subtle and may only be visible under certain conditions (hard surface, circle, flexion test). Grades 3 and 4 are consistently visible at trot. Grade 5 is a veterinary emergency. When reporting lameness to the veterinarian, using this scale provides a shared reference point.

Concept 4

Flexion Tests

A flexion test involves holding a joint in maximum flexion for 60 seconds, then immediately trotting the horse straight away while watching for exaggerated lameness in the first few strides. A positive flexion test — increased lameness after flexion — localizes the problem to the joints and soft tissues held in flexion. Flexion tests are performed by the veterinarian as part of a lameness exam; the horseperson's role is to understand what they reveal and to assist in the trot-out.

A horse that is three-legged lame — completely unwilling to bear weight on one limb — requires immediate veterinary contact. The most common cause is an abscess, which is treatable and resolves quickly with drainage. However, a fractured third phalanx, a sheared heel, or a penetrating wound to the hoof present identically and are emergencies. Weight-bearing lameness of grade 5 is always treated as an emergency until the veterinarian has ruled out structural causes.

In horse judging, soundness is assessed on a trot-up on a straight, hard surface and on a circle. Judges look for the head bob and pelvic asymmetry described above. A horse placed lower in a soundness evaluation than its conformation would otherwise warrant has detected an issue that the judge considers functionally significant. Understanding the evaluation criteria allows a student judge to explain their placings, not just rank them.

In the Barn

Watching horses move at liberty in a pasture or paddock is one of the best ways to develop a trained eye for soundness and gait quality. A horse moving freely on its own shows its natural balance, rhythm, and willingness — without the influence of a rider or the energy of an arena. Handlers who regularly observe horses in movement build pattern recognition that makes lameness and quality changes easier to spot when they occur. Early identification of gait abnormality is one of the most valuable skills in horse care.

Things to Remember

  • Head bob at trot = front limb lameness (head drops when sound leg bears weight)
  • Pelvic drop = hindlimb lameness (hip on lame side rises to unload the painful leg) — viewed from behind
  • AAEP scale 0–5: grades 1–2 subtle, grade 3–4 consistent at trot, grade 5 = non-weight-bearing = emergency
  • Non-weight-bearing lameness = always treat as emergency until vet rules out fracture or penetrating wound
