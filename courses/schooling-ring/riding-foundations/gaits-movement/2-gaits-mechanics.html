Gaits & Movement

Gaits Review & Mechanics Mechanics

The mechanics behind walk, trot, canter, and gallop: footfall sequences, beat counts, suspension, and the difference between speed and impulsion.

More Than Different Speeds

The four natural gaits — walk, trot, canter, and gallop — are not simply different speeds of the same movement. Each represents a distinct footfall pattern, a different rhythm, and a different mechanical relationship between the horse's hindquarters and its forward movement. A horseperson who can name and count the beats of each gait has the vocabulary to observe movement. A horseperson who understands the mechanics behind those beats can evaluate whether a horse is moving correctly, efficiently, and soundly.

This distinction matters in both training and evaluation contexts. A horse that moves faster is not necessarily moving better. Understanding gait mechanics is the foundation for assessing impulsion, rhythm, and soundness — three criteria central to both horsemanship scoring and veterinary movement evaluation.

Gait Mechanics

The Four Natural Gaits

The Walk: Four-Beat Foundation

The walk is a four-beat gait with no moment of suspension — at least one hoof remains on the ground at all times. The footfall sequence is: left hind → left fore → right hind → right fore. A correct walk has clear, even rhythm. The hind hoof should reach at least to the track of the forehoof (tracking up) or beyond it (overtracking), indicating engagement from the hindquarters. Loss of rhythm in the walk often indicates pain or tension.

The Trot: Two-Beat Diagonal

The trot is a two-beat gait in which diagonal pairs move together: left fore and right hind as one beat, right fore and left hind as the second. Between the two beats, a moment of suspension occurs in which all four feet leave the ground simultaneously. The western equivalent, the jog, is a slower, more collected version of the same diagonal gait. An irregular or unlevel trot — where one beat sounds different from the other — is one of the primary indicators of lameness during a veterinary or pre-purchase exam.

The Canter: Three Beats and a Lead

The canter is a three-beat gait with a moment of suspension after the third beat. The footfall order determines the lead: on the left lead, the right hind lands first, followed by the left hind and right fore together, then the left fore — the leading leg. On the right lead, the sequence reverses. The leading foreleg lands last and briefly supports the horse's full weight alone, making correct lead selection important for the horse's balance and the rider's security on turns.

The Gallop: Four Beats at Speed

The gallop is a four-beat gait derived from the canter by separating the diagonal pair into two distinct footfalls. It is the horse's fastest gait and features a longer moment of suspension than the canter. The gallop is used in racing, cross-country jumping, and fast field work but is rarely asked for in arena schooling. Its four-beat rhythm, distinct from the canter's three beats, can be heard clearly and is a useful tool for evaluating whether a canter has become a four-beat "gallop-canter" — a common collection fault.

Impulsion is not speed. Impulsion is energy generated from the hindquarters and directed forward into a controlled, elastic stride. A horse can trot very slowly with excellent impulsion, or canter very fast with none. The distinction is central to horsemanship evaluation and movement quality assessment.

Applying Gait Knowledge

Gait mechanics are applied in three practical contexts in horsemanship. In showmanship and horsemanship classes, gait transitions must be prompt, smooth, and executed at the designated marker — requiring the handler to understand what each gait feels and looks like from both the saddle and the ground. In horse judging, evaluating a horse's movement requires knowledge of what correct mechanics look like at each gait. In veterinary science, gait irregularities are the primary diagnostic tool for lameness — and recognizing what is normal is the prerequisite for recognizing what is not.

Horse Bowl questions on gaits test both factual knowledge (how many beats, which pairs are diagonal) and applied understanding (what an irregular trot indicates, what overtracking demonstrates). Both are covered in this course.

Things to Remember

  • Walk is a 4-beat gait with no moment of suspension; trot is a 2-beat diagonal gait with a moment of suspension; canter is a 3-beat gait with a moment of suspension; gallop is a 4-beat gait at speed.
  • At the walk, footfalls follow this sequence: left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore — creating a lateral rhythm.
  • At the trot, diagonal pairs move together: right fore + left hind rise and fall simultaneously, then left fore + right hind.
  • At the canter, the leading foreleg lands last in the sequence — the lead determines which foreleg appears to reach furthest forward.
