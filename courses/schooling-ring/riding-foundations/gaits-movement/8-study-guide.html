Key Concepts

How many beats does the walk have, and does it have a moment of suspension?

The walk is a four-beat gait with no moment of suspension. Each hoof strikes the ground separately, and at least one foot is always in contact with the ground. Footfall sequence: left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore.

Describe the trot's beat count, diagonal pairs, and suspension.

The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait. Diagonal pairs move together: left fore and right hind as one beat, right fore and left hind as the second. Between each pair there is a moment of suspension when all four feet leave the ground simultaneously.

What is the footfall sequence of the left-lead canter, and which leg is the leading leg?

Left lead canter: right hind (1), left hind and right fore together as the diagonal pair (2), left fore — the leading leg — (3), then a moment of suspension. The leading leg is the last foreleg to leave the ground before suspension; it extends farthest forward.

How does the gallop differ mechanically from the canter?

The gallop is a four-beat gait. In the canter, the diagonal pair (left hind + right fore, or vice versa) moves together as one beat. At the gallop, that diagonal pair separates into two independent footfalls, creating four distinct beats and a longer suspension phase. The gallop is a mechanically distinct gait — not simply a faster canter.

What is impulsion, and how does it differ from speed?

Impulsion is energy generated from the hindquarters and directed forward into a controlled, elastic stride. Speed is the rate of travel. A horse can have excellent impulsion at a slow pace or poor impulsion at high speed. Impulsion is visible as the degree to which the hindleg swings under the body and the spring in each stride.

What does "correct lead" mean, and how is it identified from the saddle?

On the correct lead, the inside foreleg is the leading leg — it extends farthest forward and lands last before suspension. On a left circle, the left lead is correct; on a right circle, the right lead is correct. From the saddle: glance at the inside shoulder — it swings farther forward on the correct lead.

What does "correct diagonal" mean at the posting trot, and how is it checked?

The correct posting diagonal means the rider rises as the outside fore and inside hind strike the ground (the outside diagonal pair). To check: glance at the inside shoulder. If it moves forward as the rider sits, the diagonal is incorrect — stand for one beat and recheck.

In what order should movement quality criteria be evaluated?

Rhythm first, then impulsion, then straightness, then collection. Rhythm is assessed before anything else because an irregular gait prevents meaningful evaluation of any other quality. A lame horse is always placed below all sound horses regardless of other movement qualities.

What are the two primary visual signs of lameness at the trot?

Head bob (front limb lameness): the head drops when the sound foreleg bears weight and rises when the lame foreleg bears weight. Pelvic drop (hindlimb lameness): the hip on the lame side rises when that hindleg bears weight — observed from behind at the trot.

What does the AAEP Lameness Grading Scale measure, and what is Grade 5?

The AAEP scale classifies lameness from 0 (sound — no lameness detected) to 5 (non-weight-bearing — horse refuses to use the limb). Grades 1–2 are subtle; grades 3–4 are consistently visible at the trot. Grade 5 is a veterinary emergency.

Key Vocabulary

Suspension

The moment in the trot, canter, or gallop when all four feet are simultaneously off the ground. The walk has no suspension.

Impulsion

Energy generated from the hindquarters and directed forward into a controlled, elastic stride. Not the same as speed.

Leading leg

The last foreleg to leave the ground before the moment of suspension in the canter. It extends farthest forward. On the left lead, the left foreleg is the leading leg.

Counter-canter

Intentionally cantering on the outside lead as a gymnastic suppling and straightening exercise. Distinct from an accidental wrong lead.

Half-halt

A brief, simultaneous application of seat, leg, and rein that momentarily collects the horse without producing a full gait transition. Used before corners, transitions, and lengthenings.

Overtracking

The hind foot landing in front of the print left by the same-side foreleg at the walk. Indicates good hindleg reach; a positive quality in movement evaluation.

Head bob

The characteristic up-and-down movement of the horse's head at the trot when front limb lameness is present. The head drops as the sound leg bears weight.

Pelvic drop

The characteristic asymmetric rise of the hip on the lame side when that hindleg bears weight. Identifies hindlimb lameness; observed from behind at the trot.

Common Mistakes

Confusing the gallop with a fast canter

The gallop is a mechanically distinct four-beat gait — the diagonal pair that moves together in the canter separates into two independent footfalls. A canter that develops four-beat rhythm is a fault (gallop-canter), not a gait upgrade. The distinguishing feature: beat count (3 vs. 4).

Confusing impulsion with speed

Speed is the rate of travel. Impulsion is hindquarter energy directed into elastic forward strides. A horse can travel slowly with excellent impulsion or quickly with none. The key question is whether the hindleg is swinging under the body and creating spring — not how fast the horse is moving.

Posting on the wrong diagonal

The correct diagonal is the outside pair (outside fore + inside hind). A common error is posting on the inside diagonal. The check: if the inside shoulder moves forward as the rider sits, the diagonal is incorrect. Stand for one beat, sit, and recheck.

Confusing counter-canter with a wrong lead

Counter-canter is intentional outside-lead work as a gymnastic exercise. A wrong lead is an accidental incorrect departure. Context determines which it is: if counter-canter was not asked for, the horse has taken the wrong lead.

Thinking collection is a head position

Collection is weight redistribution toward the hindquarters — produced when the hindleg comes under the body. A horse with a low head and trailing hindquarters is not collected. True collection requires active hindleg engagement, not rein pressure to lower the head.

Horse Bowl Focus

Beat counts for all four gaits

Walk: 4 beats, no suspension. Trot: 2 beats, diagonal pairs, suspension between. Canter: 3 beats + suspension. Gallop: 4 beats, diagonal pair separates, longer suspension than canter. Only the walk has no suspension.

How to identify the correct canter lead

The correct lead has the inside foreleg as the leading leg — extending farthest forward. Identified visually: the inside shoulder swings farther forward on the correct lead. On left circle = left lead; on right circle = right lead.

Head bob rule for front limb lameness

Head drops when the sound leg bears weight; head rises when the lame leg bears weight. The horse uses the head bob to reduce load on the painful front limb. Identified at the trot on a straight line.

Pelvic drop rule for hindlimb lameness

The hip on the lame side rises when that hindleg bears weight — the horse unloads the painful limb. Observed from behind the horse at the trot. Pelvic drop identifies hindlimb lameness; head bob identifies front limb.

AAEP Lameness Scale grades 0–5

0 = sound. 1–2 = subtle (may only appear on hard surface, circle, or after flexion test). 3–4 = consistently visible at trot. 5 = non-weight-bearing, will not use limb — veterinary emergency.

Movement evaluation order

Rhythm is evaluated first (irregular gait cannot be assessed for anything else). Then impulsion. Then straightness. Then collection. A lame horse is placed below all sound horses regardless of other movement qualities.

What a correct transition requires

Promptness (within one to two strides of the aid), balance (hindquarters prepared with half-halts beforehand), and straightness (no drifting or swinging hindquarters during the change). These three criteria are scored in horsemanship and equitation pattern work.

Posting diagonal rule

Rise with the outside shoulder = outside fore and inside hind = correct diagonal on a circle. Diagonal matters most on curved lines because it aligns the rider's weight with the diagonal that has the most ground support.
