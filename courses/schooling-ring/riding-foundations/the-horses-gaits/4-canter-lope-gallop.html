The Horse's Gaits

Canter, Lope, and Gallop

The canter and gallop are the two faster natural gaits. The canter is a steady three-beat gait used in many sports. The gallop is the horse's fastest movement, with four beats and a long moment of suspension.

The Faster Gaits

When a horse moves faster than the trot, it shifts into the canter. The canter is used in many riding sports for arena work, changes between gaits, and fitness. The gallop is used when top speed is needed, like racing, cross-country, and open-field riding. Knowing how each gait works helps riders and handlers spot good movement and any problems.

💡 Did You Know

The fastest gallop ever recorded was about 55 miles per hour. A Thoroughbred named Winning Brew set this record over a short distance in 2008. At top speed, a galloping horse can cover more than 8 meters in one stride.

The canter is a three-beat gait with a rocking, rolling feel. It is the most common of the faster gaits. You see it in English, Western, and performance riding.

The Canter: Three-Beat Gait

At the canter, the horse moves in a three-beat pattern. Then there is a short moment when all four feet are off the ground. One common order is: left hind first, then right hind and left fore together as one beat, then right fore. After that comes the moment of suspension.

The lope is the Western name for the canter. It uses the same footfall pattern, but it is slower and more relaxed. This suits Western riding and longer rides. A canter or lope moves at about 10 to 17 miles per hour.

Key Fact: The canter is a three-beat gait followed by a moment of suspension. The lope is the Western name for the same gait.

The gallop is the horse's fastest natural gait. It is different from the canter in both footfall pattern and speed. It is rarely used in the arena, but it is key for racing, cross-country, and open riding.

The Gallop: Four-Beat Gait

At the gallop, the diagonal pair that moved together at the canter splits apart. This makes a four-beat pattern instead of three. One common order is: left hind, right hind, left fore, right fore. Then comes a longer moment of suspension than at the canter. This long suspension is what makes the gallop cover so much ground.

The gallop is the fastest of the four natural gaits. A fit riding horse can gallop at about 25 to 30 miles per hour. Some racing Thoroughbreds have gone over 40 miles per hour for short distances, but that is rare. Most horses move much slower in everyday riding.

At the gallop, the rider often uses a two-point, or forward, seat. The rider lifts slightly out of the saddle and puts weight into the stirrups. This takes weight off the horse's back and lets it move more freely. Riders use this seat in racing, cross-country, and other fast riding.

Key Fact: The gallop is a four-beat gait, not three. The diagonal pair that makes one beat in the canter splits into two beats at the gallop.
Barn Connection: When watching horses in a field or at free exercise, the gallop is easy to spot. Look for its speed and long, reaching stride. The moment of suspension, when all four feet leave the ground, is one of the most amazing things to see in a moving horse.

Things to Remember

  • The canter is a three-beat gait followed by suspension. The lope is the Western term for the canter.
  • The gallop is a four-beat gait, with a longer moment of suspension than the canter.
