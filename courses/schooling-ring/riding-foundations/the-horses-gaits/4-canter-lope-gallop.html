The Horse's Gaits

Canter, Lope, and Gallop

The canter and gallop are the two fastest natural gaits. The canter is a controlled, three-beat gait used across most riding disciplines. The gallop is the horse’s fastest gait, used for speed, covering ground, and open movement.

The Faster Gaits

Once a horse moves beyond the trot, it transitions into the canter. The canter is commonly used in arena work, training progression, and many riding sports because it offers balance and control at a forward speed.

The gallop is used when maximum speed is required, such as racing, cross-country riding, or open-field movement.

Understanding both gaits helps riders recognize quality of movement, rhythm changes, and early signs of imbalance or fatigue.

💡 Did You Know

The fastest recorded gallop reached about 55 miles per hour over a short distance. At full speed, a galloping horse can cover more than 8 meters in a single stride.

The canter is a three-beat gait with a smooth, rocking rhythm. It is used in both English and Western riding and is one of the most important working gaits in performance horses.

The Canter: Three-Beat Gait

At the canter, the horse moves in a three-beat sequence followed by a moment of suspension:

Left hind → Right hind + Left fore (together) → Right fore → suspension

This creates a rolling rhythm that feels different depending on which lead the horse is on.

The lope is the Western term for the canter. It follows the same footfall pattern but is typically slower, lower, and more relaxed in presentation. It is commonly used in Western riding for extended movement and training.

A canter or lope typically ranges from 10 to 17 miles per hour, depending on the horse and context.

Key Fact: The canter is a three-beat gait followed by a suspension phase. The lope is the Western name for the same movement.

The gallop is the fastest natural gait of the horse. It differs from the canter in both speed and footfall pattern and is used primarily for forward travel, racing, and open terrain movement.

The Gallop: Four-Beat Gait

At the gallop, the diagonal pair seen in the canter separates, creating a four-beat sequence:

Left hind → Right hind → Left fore → Right fore → suspension

This results in a longer stride and a more extended suspension phase than the canter. The increased suspension is what allows the horse to cover maximum distance at speed.

A fit riding horse typically gallops between 25 and 30 miles per hour. Racing Thoroughbreds can exceed 40 miles per hour over short distances, though this is not typical for most horses.

Riders often use a forward, or two-point, seat at the gallop. This position lifts the rider out of the saddle and allows the horse to move more freely by reducing weight on the back.

Key Fact: The gallop is a four-beat gait. The diagonal pair from the canter separates into individual beats, creating a faster, more extended stride.
Barn Connection: In turnout or free exercise, the gallop is easily recognized by its speed and long reaching stride. The moment of suspension, when all four feet leave the ground, is most visible at full extension.

Things to Remember

  • The canter is a three-beat gait followed by a suspension phase.
  • The lope is the Western term for the canter.
  • The gallop is a four-beat gait with a longer suspension phase than the canter.
  • The gallop is the horse’s fastest natural gait.
