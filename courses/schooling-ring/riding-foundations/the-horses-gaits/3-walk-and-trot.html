The Horse's Gaits

Walk and Trot

The walk and trot are the two slowest natural gaits. Each has a distinct rhythm, footfall pattern, and feel. Both are used in everyday riding, training, and warm-up work, and they form the foundation for understanding all faster gaits.

The Two Foundation Gaits

Most rides begin and end at the walk. The trot is typically the next gait introduced in training and is one of the most important working gaits in equestrian disciplines. It builds balance, coordination, and fitness in both horse and rider.

A clear understanding of these two gaits makes it easier to recognize and learn the canter and gallop later.

💡 Did You Know

The trot is a two-beat gait, but there is a brief moment during each stride when all four hooves are off the ground. This is called the moment of suspension. It is what creates the springy or bouncy feel of the trot. The longer and more balanced the suspension, the smoother the trot feels to the rider.

The walk is the slowest of the four natural gaits. It is a steady, four-beat gait where at least two hooves remain on the ground at all times.

The Walk: Four-Beat Gait

The walk follows a specific sequence of footfalls:

Left hind → Left fore → Right hind → Right fore (LH, LF, RH, RF)

Each hoof hits the ground separately, creating four even beats. There is no suspension phase, meaning the horse is always supported by at least two legs.

This makes the walk the most stable gait and the easiest for riders to sit. A relaxed walk typically travels around 4 miles per hour, depending on the horse and environment.

The walk also provides important insight into soundness. A clean, even four-beat rhythm indicates relaxed, efficient movement. If the sequence becomes uneven or pairs begin to move together, it may indicate stiffness, tension, or discomfort.

Key Fact: The walk is a four-beat gait with no suspension phase.

The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait used in nearly every riding discipline. It plays a central role in developing balance, rhythm, and strength in both horse and rider.

The Trot: Two-Beat Diagonal Gait

At the trot, the horse moves in diagonal pairs:

Left fore + Right hind → Right fore + Left hind

Between each diagonal step, there is a brief suspension phase where all four feet leave the ground. This is what creates the trot’s characteristic bounce.

A working trot typically ranges from 8 to 12 miles per hour, depending on the horse. A slower, more relaxed version of the trot is called the jog, commonly used in Western riding. The jog follows the same footfall pattern but with reduced speed and less impulsion.

Riders manage the trot in two main ways:

  • Posting trot: rising and sitting in rhythm with the horse’s movement to reduce impact.
  • Sitting trot: staying in the saddle and following the motion with a relaxed seat and back.

Posting is often used for longer rides or when the horse needs more freedom of movement.

Key Fact: The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait with a moment of suspension between each stride.
Barn Connection: When observing a horse on a longe line, an even rhythm and consistent diagonal pairing indicate sound movement. Any irregularity in stride length or timing may be an early sign of discomfort and should be noted.

Things to Remember

  • The walk is a four-beat gait: LH, LF, RH, RF, with no suspension.
  • The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait with a suspension phase between beats.
  • The jog is the Western version of the trot, with slower speed and softer motion.
  • Posting helps riders follow the trot rhythm and reduce bounce.
