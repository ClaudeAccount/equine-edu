The Horse's Gaits

Walk and Trot

The walk and trot are the two slowest natural gaits. Each one has its own footfall pattern, number of beats, and feel. Both are used for training, warm-up, and everyday riding.

The Two Foundation Gaits

Most rides begin and end at the walk. The trot comes next. It is the main working gait in many riding sports. It builds fitness, balance, and early riding skills. Learning these two gaits well helps you understand the faster gaits later.

💡 Did You Know

The trot is a two-beat gait. But there is a short moment when all four feet are off the ground. This is called the moment of suspension. It is what makes a trot feel smooth or bouncy. A longer moment of suspension gives the trot more spring.

The walk is the slowest of the four natural gaits. At least two feet stay on the ground the whole time. Its steady four-beat rhythm makes it a great way to start any ride.

The Walk: Four-Beat Gait

In a walk, the horse moves one leg at a time in a set order. This makes four separate hoof beats. The order is left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore. It is often written as LH, LF, RH, RF. The four beats stay even. There is no moment when all four feet are off the ground.

At least two feet stay on the ground at all times. This is why the walk has no moment of suspension. It is the steadiest gait and the easiest one for a new rider to sit. A relaxed walk moves at about 4 miles per hour.

The walk can also show how a horse feels. A clear, even four-beat rhythm shows a healthy, forward walk. If the same-side legs start to move in pairs, the walk looks stiff. This can be a sign of tension or discomfort.

Key Fact: The walk is a four-beat gait with no moment of suspension. At least two hooves stay on the ground through the whole stride.

The trot is a two-beat gait. The legs move in diagonal pairs. It is a main working gait in both English and Western riding. Its steady rhythm is great for fitness, balance, and practicing changes between gaits.

The Trot: Two-Beat Diagonal Gait

At the trot, the horse moves its legs in diagonal pairs. The left fore and right hind move together. Then the right fore and left hind move together. Between these two beats, all four feet are off the ground for a moment. This is what gives the trot its springy, bouncy feel.

A working trot moves at about 8 to 12 miles per hour. The exact speed changes with the horse and its training. A slower, relaxed trot is called the jog. It is often used in Western riding. The jog has the same two-beat pattern as the trot, but it is slower and quieter.

Riders handle the trot's bounce in two ways. In a sitting trot, the rider stays in the saddle and follows the motion with a relaxed lower back. In a posting trot, the rider rises and sits in rhythm with the horse. Posting helps on longer rides and lets the horse move more freely.

Key Fact: The trot is a two-beat gait where diagonal leg pairs move together. The short suspension between each beat creates the bounce the rider feels.
Barn Connection: When a horse trots on a longe line, handlers watch for an even rhythm and a clear two-beat footfall. Any unevenness, like a short stride on one side, can be a sign of soreness. It is worth a closer look.

Things to Remember

  • The walk is a four-beat gait — LH, LF, RH, RF — with no suspension.
  • The trot is a two-beat diagonal gait with a moment of suspension between each beat.
  • The jog is the Western version of the trot, ridden with less impulsion and a quieter rhythm.
  • The posting trot rises and sits in rhythm with the horse to ease the bounce.
