Natural & Artificial Aids

Artificial Aids

Artificial aids are tools that support the natural aids. They do not replace training. They make communication clearer when used correctly.

Tools That Support Communication

Artificial aids are tools that support the natural aids. They do not replace training or create communication on their own. When used correctly, they refine signals. When used incorrectly, they can create confusion or resistance in the horse.

Natural aids (leg, seat, hands, voice) always come first. Artificial aids are added only when the horse already understands the base cue.

Modern safety stirrups were designed to reduce the risk of a rider being dragged if a foot gets caught during a fall.

Why Artificial Aids Are Used

Artificial aids exist to improve clarity, not to increase force. They help:

  • Refine signals the horse already understands.
  • Support communication in more advanced work.
  • Provide precision in trained movements.

They do not replace consistent training, timing, or release. Most issues with artificial aids come from using them before the natural aids are established.

Spurs refine the leg aid by increasing precision, not strength.

Spurs

They attach to the rider’s boot and allow a more exact point of contact than the leg alone. When used correctly, they help experienced riders give small, clear cues without increasing overall pressure.

Types include:

  • Rounded spurs: mild, general refinement.
  • Rowel spurs: more advanced, rotating contact for precision.

Spurs require a stable leg. A moving or unstable leg creates accidental pressure, which can confuse or desensitize the horse.

Key Fact: Spurs refine the leg aid. They do not increase force.

Whips and crops reinforce the leg aid when the horse does not respond.

Whips and Crops

Types include:

  • Crop: short riding whip.
  • Dressage whip: longer, used for reaching the hindquarters.
  • Longe whip: used from the ground during training.

Correct use follows a clear sequence:

  • Leg aid is applied.
  • If needed, a light whip cue follows immediately.
  • Pressure is released as soon as the horse responds.

Timing is critical. Delayed reinforcement weakens communication and can create confusion. Whips are not punishment tools.

Key Fact: Whips reinforce the leg aid. Timing determines effectiveness.

Martingales influence head position and rein control. They are commonly used in jumping disciplines and some Western classes.

Martingales

Types include:

  • Standing martingale: limits how high the horse can raise its head.
  • Running martingale: only activates when the head rises too high and releases when correct position is restored.

Martingales address position, not cause. A high head carriage may result from discomfort, imbalance, tack issues, or training gaps.

They are most effective when used alongside correct training, not as a replacement for it.

Key Fact: Martingales manage position, not the underlying cause.

Artificial aids vary by discipline, and rules differ between sports. Equipment allowed in one discipline may be restricted or penalized in another.

The most important question when using any artificial aid is: Does this improve communication, or replace it?

If natural aids are unclear, artificial aids will not solve the issue — they will amplify it.

Things to Remember

  • Artificial aids support natural aids, they do not replace them.
  • Spurs refine leg cues and require a stable leg.
  • Whips reinforce the leg aid with correct timing.
  • Martingales manage head position but do not fix underlying causes.
  • Clear communication always starts with natural aids.
