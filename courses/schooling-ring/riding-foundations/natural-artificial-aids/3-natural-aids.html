Natural & Artificial Aids

Natural Aids

Natural aids are the signals a rider gives using their own body. They do not require equipment. They are the foundation of all riding communication.

The Foundation of Communication

The four natural aids are: leg, seat, hands, and voice.

Every request made to a horse — to move forward, slow down, turn, or change balance — comes from some combination of these aids.

💡 Did You Know

In advanced dressage, skilled riders can guide horses using extremely subtle natural aids that are almost invisible from the ground. The goal is clarity without obvious movement.

Communication Through the Body

Natural aids work best when they are:

  • Clear
  • Independent
  • Coordinated

Each aid has a specific role, but they must work together. Many riding problems come from conflicting signals, such as a driving leg paired with a pulling hand.

Good riding is not about stronger aids — it is about clearer ones.

The leg is the primary driving aid. It controls forward energy, impulsion, and some lateral movement.

The Leg Aid

The leg communicates through pressure on the horse’s sides:

  • At the girth: asks for forward movement.
  • Behind the girth: asks the hindquarters to move sideways.

A correct leg aid uses the calf and lower leg, not gripping with the knee.

A common training approach is:

  • Light pressure.
  • Increased pressure if needed.
  • Release immediately when the horse responds.

Release is what teaches the horse.

Key Fact: The leg creates forward energy and helps guide direction.

The seat controls balance, rhythm, and weight distribution.

The Seat Aid

It communicates through how the rider sits and moves with the horse:

  • A following seat supports forward movement.
  • A deeper seat can encourage slowing or collection.
  • Subtle weight shifts can influence direction and balance.

The seat develops over time as the rider improves balance and stability. Early riders often rely more on leg and hand aids while the seat becomes more refined.

Key Fact: The seat influences rhythm and balance through weight and position.

The hands communicate through the reins and bit. They guide direction, regulate speed, and provide feedback.

The Hand Aid

Correct hand use starts with soft, steady contact. From there, the rider can:

  • Close the fingers to steady or slow.
  • Open the rein to guide direction.
  • Release contact to reward the horse.

There are two primary rein actions:

  • Direct rein: turns the horse toward the hand.
  • Neck rein (indirect rein): guides the horse away from pressure, common in Western riding.

Constant pulling creates resistance. Release creates understanding.

Key Fact: Hands should stay soft. Release is the reward.

The voice is the only natural aid that uses sound instead of physical pressure.

The Voice Aid

It is most commonly used in groundwork and early training, including:

  • Leading.
  • Lunging.
  • Basic handling.

Common voice cues include:

  • Cluck: encourages forward movement.
  • Calm “whoa”: signals slowing or stopping.

Tone and consistency matter more than the specific word.

In many disciplines, voice use is limited in competition, so it is gradually replaced by more refined body aids.

Key Fact: Voice aids rely on tone, timing, and consistency.

Natural aids must work together. The horse receives one combined message, not separate signals.

Clear communication looks like:

  • A supporting leg.
  • A steady, quiet seat.
  • Soft, following hands.
  • Calm, consistent timing.

Conflicting aids confuse the horse and delay response.

Things to Remember

  • Natural aids are the rider’s body: leg, seat, hands, and voice.
  • Each aid has a specific job, but they must work together.
  • The leg drives movement, the seat controls balance, the hands guide, and the voice supports.
  • Release is what teaches the horse.
  • Clear aids are more effective than strong aids.
