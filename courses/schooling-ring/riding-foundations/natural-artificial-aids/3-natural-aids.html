Natural & Artificial Aids

Natural Aids

The four natural aids are the rider's own tools: leg, seat, hands, and voice. Riders learn to use them clearly, lightly, and together.

Communication Through the Body

Natural aids need no equipment. The skill is learning to use them clearly, on their own, and together. Many riding problems happen when aids conflict, such as a driving leg with a pulling hand.

💡 Did You Know

At advanced levels of dressage, riders can guide the horse with almost no movement you can see. Judges often give higher scores to movements that look easy and invisible.

The leg is the main forward-driving aid. It controls energy, direction, and forward movement.

The Leg Aid

The leg talks to the horse through pressure on its side. At the girth, the leg asks the horse to move forward. Behind the girth, the leg asks the hindquarters to move sideways.

Use the calf and lower leg, not a grip with the knee. A steady, quiet leg makes the message clearer.

A good rule is light to strong. Start with a light squeeze. Add firmer pressure if needed. Add a tap if there is still no response. Release the pressure as soon as the horse responds.

Key Fact: The leg makes forward energy and helps with movement and direction. At the girth: forward. Behind the girth: sideways.

The seat talks to the horse through balance, weight, and movement in the saddle. It is often the last aid new riders learn to use on purpose.

The Seat Aid

The seat works through weight and movement. A deeper seat asks the horse to slow down. A following seat, where the hips move with the horse, asks for forward movement. A small weight shift helps show direction.

The seat takes time to build. New riders often cannot guide the horse with the seat alone because they are still learning balance. As balance improves, the seat becomes one of the quietest and most useful aids.

Key Fact: The seat controls rhythm and balance through weight and body position.

The hands talk to the horse through the reins and bit. They guide, soften, and steer. But they should be used after the leg and seat, not first.

The Hand Aid

Soft contact is the starting point. From there, the hand can close the fingers to slow or steady the horse. An opening rein guides direction. Releasing the contact rewards the horse for a correct response.

There are two main rein types. A direct rein turns the horse toward the hand. An indirect rein, also called a neck rein, presses on the neck and guides the horse away from the pressure. The neck rein is common in Western riding.

Constant pulling creates a horse that leans on the bit and stops listening. A soft release teaches the horse to respond.

Key Fact: Hands should stay soft and follow the horse. Release is the reward.

The voice is the one natural aid that uses sound instead of pressure. It is used a lot from the ground, and it supports the other aids when riding.

The Voice Aid

A cluck encourages forward movement. A calm, drawn-out "whoa" signals the horse to stop or slow down. The tone matters more than the word itself.

Voice aids are often used from the ground and in early training. Handlers use them while leading, grooming, and longeing. Horses learn voice aids quickly when they are used the same way each time.

In many competitions, voice use is limited or penalized. So riders slowly phase it out as the horse learns to respond to the leg, seat, and hand.

Key Fact: Voice aids work through tone and steady use, not just words.
Barn Connection: Natural aids must work together. A balanced leg, a soft hand, and a steady seat create a clear message. When aids conflict, the horse gets mixed signals and cannot respond clearly.

Things to Remember

  • The four natural aids are leg, seat, hands, and voice. No equipment is needed.
  • They must work together, not against each other.
  • The leg drives movement, the seat controls balance, the hands guide, and the voice supports.
  • Light pressure first, then release as soon as the horse responds.
