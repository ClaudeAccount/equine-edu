Introduction to Arena Figures

Arena Figures

Arena figures are set shapes and paths a horse and rider follow in the arena. This lesson gives a light look at the most common figures, from using the whole arena to circles, figure eights, and serpentines.

The Shapes Riders Follow

Arena figures are set shapes ridden in the arena, like circles and curves. The same figure names are used in English, Western, and driving. This shared set of names lets riders, trainers, and judges all picture the same path.

This lesson is a light introduction. Each figure below is explained in simple terms so the shape and its purpose are easy to picture.

💡 Did You Know

When riding a circle, the rider looks ahead along the curve, not down at the horse. Looking ahead helps keep the circle round and the right size. This is one of the first habits new riders learn.

Some figures simply use the space of the arena itself — the whole arena, one half of it, or a straight line down a long side.

Using the Arena Space

Full arena. Riding the full arena means using the whole space, all the way around the outside track. There is plenty of room for large figures like big circles.

Half arena. Riding the half arena means using only one half of the space, split across the middle. This helps when more than one rider shares the arena, or when a rider wants to work in a smaller area.

Riding along the length. Riding along the length means going straight down a long side of the arena. It sounds simple, but riding a truly straight line takes balance and even, steady aids from the rider.

Key Idea: The full arena uses the whole space. The half arena uses one half, split across the middle. Riding the length means traveling straight down a long side.

Circles are the most common arena figures. A good circle keeps the same size, shape, and rhythm the whole way around.

Circles, Voltes, and Figure Eights

Circle. A circle is a round figure of even size. The horse bends gently along the curve and keeps a steady rhythm. Common sizes are the 20-meter circle, which is large and easier, and the 10-meter circle, which is smaller and harder.

Volte. A volte is a small circle, usually about 6 meters across. Because it is so small, it asks the horse for more bend and balance than a large circle.

Figure eight. A figure eight is two circles of the same size joined in the middle, making the shape of the number 8. The horse changes its bend in the center, where the two circles meet, switching from curving one way to curving the other.

Key Idea: A volte is just a small circle, about 6 meters across. A figure eight is two equal circles joined in the middle, with a change of bend in the center.

A serpentine is a winding figure made of even loops. It is a great way to practice changing bend smoothly from one side to the other.

Serpentines and Simple Serpentines

Serpentine. A serpentine is a series of equal loops ridden across the arena, making an S-shaped path. Each time the horse crosses the center line, it straightens for one step and then bends the other way.

Simple serpentine. A simple serpentine uses gentle, shallow loops along one long side instead of full loops across the whole arena. The bend changes are smaller, so it is an easy first step before riding a full serpentine.

Key Idea: A serpentine is a row of even loops in an S shape, with a change of bend at the center line each time. A simple serpentine uses smaller, shallow loops.
Barn Connection: Figures are easier to ride well when the rider plans ahead. Looking up and picturing the shape before starting helps the horse follow a smooth, even path.

Things to Remember

  • The full arena uses the whole space; the half arena uses one half, split across the middle.
  • Riding along the length means traveling straight down a long side.
  • A circle is a round figure of even size; a volte is a small circle, about 6 meters across.
  • A figure eight is two equal circles joined in the middle, with a change of bend in the center.
  • A serpentine is a row of even loops in an S shape, changing bend at the center line.
  • A simple serpentine uses smaller, shallow loops and is an easy first step.
