Introduction to Arena Figures

The Arena

Standard riding arenas follow consistent dimensions and letter layouts. Knowing the arena's structure — where each letter sits, what each zone is called — allows riders and handlers to communicate clearly and plan figures accurately.

A Shared Map

Arena letters are not arbitrary — they are a standardized system used worldwide. When an instructor says "ride a 20-meter circle at C" or a test sheet reads "at A, working trot," everyone working from the same letter system knows exactly what and where is being asked. Learning the letters and dimensions gives access to a shared language for all structured riding.

💡 Did You Know

The 20m x 60m dressage arena size was standardized in the early 20th century largely for competitive practicality. Before standardization, arena sizes varied widely, making it impossible to compare movements executed at specific letter positions between different venues.

Riding arenas come in two standard sizes. Each size determines which letters are used, how large figures can be, and what figures are appropriate for the space available.

Standard Arena Sizes

The small standard arena measures 20 meters wide by 40 meters long (approximately 66 by 131 feet). This size is used for introductory through lower-level competitions and is the most common size at smaller riding facilities. The 20x40 arena fits a 20-meter circle from one end, with the circle touching both long sides and both short sides at that end.

The large standard arena measures 20 meters wide by 60 meters long, or about 66 by 197 feet. This size is used for upper-level competition and figures that need more length. The 20x60 arena allows two 20-meter circles to be ridden end to end without overlapping. It also gives more room for long straight lines.

Many everyday riding arenas do not precisely match these dimensions — barns use what space allows. However, knowing these standard sizes provides a reference for understanding how figures scale to the available space and why the standard letters are placed where they are.

Key Fact: The small arena is 20m x 40m; the large arena is 20m x 60m. All standard letter positions and figure sizes are based on these two dimensions.

Arena letters mark specific points along the walls and center line. Learning their placement is the first step to riding accurate figures and understanding test movements.

Arena Letter Placement

In a small (20x40) arena, the letters at the short ends are A (at the entry end, where riders traditionally enter) and C (at the far short end). Along the long sides, reading from A: K and H on the left side, F and M on the right side. The letters B and E mark the midpoints of each long side. The center of the arena, at the midpoint of both dimensions, is marked X — though X is not a physical letter on the wall, only a point on the center line.

In the large (20x60) arena, additional letters are added along the long sides between the short ends and the midpoints. Reading from A along the left long side: K, V, E, S, H. Along the right long side: F, P, B, R, M. The center points D, L, X, I, and G are marked on the center line running from A to C. G is directly in front of C.

A common memory aid for the letters around the outside of the small arena, starting at A and going left: All King's Horses Can Make Fine Hay (A, K, H, C, M, F, H — often adapted depending on the starting point). Memory aids vary — finding one that works is more useful than memorizing the phrase itself.

Key Fact: A and C are always at the short ends. E and B always mark the midpoints of the long sides. X is the center point of the arena — midway between A and C, and between E and B.

Arena terminology describes the zones, tracks, and directions within the space. These terms appear in lesson instructions, test sheets, and training discussions — understanding them makes all communication about arena work clearer.

Key Arena Terms

Track

The Rail Line

The track is the path along the inside edge of the arena fence or wall. Riding "on the track" means following this path closely. The quarter line is a parallel track approximately 5 meters in from the long side; the center line runs from A to C through the middle of the arena.

Direction

Tracking Left and Right

Tracking left means the horse moves counterclockwise, with the left side toward the inside of the arena. Tracking right means moving clockwise, with the right side toward the inside. Changing rein means changing direction across the arena, typically by riding a diagonal from one long side to the other.

Zones

Ends and Long Sides

The short sides are the 20-meter ends of the arena near A and C. The long sides run between the short ends. Corners are the transitions between long sides and short sides. Riding into the corners — rather than cutting them — is part of riding an accurate track and asking the horse to maintain bend through the turn.

Diagonal

Crossing the Arena

A diagonal is a straight line across the arena from one long side to the other, connecting two letters. Common diagonals include HXF and KXM. Diagonals are used for changing rein, for posting trot diagonal changes, and as the basis for some school figures.

Key Fact: The center line runs from A to C through the middle of the arena. The quarter line runs parallel to the long side, approximately 5 meters in from the rail. Both are used as tracks for figures and movements.
Barn Connection: Not all riding takes place in a standard-sized arena. When riding in a field, an open space, or a non-standard arena, the same principles apply — the rider uses visible landmarks or imagined points to plan figures and maintain accuracy. The habit of thinking in terms of center lines, quarter lines, and corner bend transfers to any space.

Things to Remember

  • The small arena is 20m x 40m; the large is 20m x 60m.
  • A and C are at the short ends; E and B are at the midpoints of the long sides; X is the center.
  • The track is the path along the rail; the center line runs from A to C through the middle.
  • Changing rein means changing direction — usually across a diagonal.
