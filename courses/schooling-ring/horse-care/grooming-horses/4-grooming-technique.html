Grooming Horses

Grooming Technique

Safe, effective grooming follows a consistent sequence that covers the whole horse — from curry to hooves — and adjusts technique for each area.

Work Through a Sequence

Grooming is most effective — and most safe — when it follows a consistent sequence. Starting at the neck and working systematically toward the hindquarters, then finishing with the legs, face, mane, tail, and hooves, ensures no area is missed and keeps the handler in predictable, safe positions relative to the horse throughout.

Grooming position matters. Handlers who drift into the horse's blind spots, stand directly behind the hindquarters, or reach awkwardly across the horse's body are in positions where a sudden movement creates a safety risk. Moving with intention and keeping the horse aware of the handler's location reduces that risk.

💡 Did You Know

Horses have two blind spots: directly in front of their nose and directly behind their hindquarters. A handler who approaches from either of these zones without warning may startle the horse. Grooming technique always begins with a hand placed on the horse's neck or shoulder to establish contact and communicate position before moving around the body.

Grooming Sequence

Step-by-Step Grooming Order

Step 1

Curry the body

Begin at the neck and work in circular motions across the shoulder, barrel, and hindquarters on one side. The curry comb loosens embedded dirt, dried sweat, and shedding hair from large muscle groups. Skip the legs, face, and bony areas — the curry is too aggressive for those zones.

Step 2

Dandy brush the body

Follow the curry with short, flicking strokes using the dandy brush — moving in the direction of hair growth. This removes the material the curry loosened. The dandy brush can also be used on the lower legs to remove dried mud, but with lighter pressure than on the barrel.

Step 3

Body brush the coat

Use the body brush in long, sweeping strokes following the direction of hair growth to remove fine dust and lay the coat flat. Clean the body brush against a metal curry comb every several strokes to keep it working effectively. The body brush can be used over the entire body including the face.

Step 4

Groom the face

Switch to the soft body brush or a dedicated face brush for the head. Work gently around the eyes, muzzle, and ears. Many horses are sensitive around the face — short, gentle strokes and patience build tolerance over time. A damp sponge or cloth is used to clean around the eyes, nostrils, and dock.

Step 5

Detangle mane and tail

Work the mane and tail in sections, starting from the bottom and working upward to remove tangles gradually without pulling out hair. A wide-toothed comb or soft brush minimizes breakage. The tail should never be grabbed and pulled from the top — this causes significant hair loss over time.

Step 6

Pick the hooves

Clean each hoof using the hoof pick, working from heel to toe to remove packed dirt, manure, and debris from the grooves beside the frog. The hoof pick should be angled away from the frog when possible. This step reveals hoof condition, thrush odor, and any embedded objects before they cause lameness.

Picking hooves is not optional. Packed debris creates moisture and anaerobic conditions in the hoof that lead to thrush — a bacterial infection of the frog. Embedded stones and debris can cause bruising and lameness. Hooves should be picked before and after every ride and as part of every grooming session, even when nothing appears visibly wrong.

Safety Positioning

Staying Safe Around the Horse

Safe grooming positioning follows a simple rule: stay close to the horse, not at arm's length. A handler who leans away from the horse while grooming has less control and less ability to move if the horse shifts. Staying in contact — one hand resting on the horse's side while the other brushes — keeps the handler aware of the horse's body tension and movement.

When moving to the opposite side, walk around the horse's head rather than ducking under the neck or passing behind the hindquarters. When picking up a hind leg, face toward the horse's tail rather than away from it, and stand close to the horse's hindquarter — not extended out to the side. These positions reduce kick risk and give the handler better leverage.

In the Barn

A consistent grooming sequence builds a predictable routine that horses come to recognize and tolerate. A horse that is groomed in the same order, at the same pace, every day becomes easier to manage during grooming — not because it is restrained, but because it has learned what to expect. Predictability reduces anxiety in horses; anxiety during grooming is often a sign that the process has been inconsistent or uncomfortable.

Things to Remember

  • Grooming sequence: curry → dandy brush → body brush → face → mane and tail → hooves — work systematically so no area is missed.
  • Always stay close to the horse during grooming; leaning away reduces control and increases risk if the horse shifts suddenly.
  • The tail is detangled from the bottom upward in sections — pulling from the top causes significant hair loss over time.
  • Hooves are picked heel to toe at every grooming session — packed debris leads to thrush and can hide embedded objects causing lameness.
  • Move around the horse's head when changing sides — never duck under the neck or walk behind the hindquarters without clear space.
