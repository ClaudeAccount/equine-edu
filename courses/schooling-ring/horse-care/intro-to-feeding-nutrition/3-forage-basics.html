Intro to Feeding & Nutrition

Forage Basics

Hay and pasture are not optional supplements — they are the foundation of the equine diet. Understanding forage types and quality is a core feeding skill.

Forage Is the Foundation

Forage — hay and pasture grass — is the most important component of a horse's diet. It provides the structural fiber the hindgut microbial population ferments for energy, keeps feed moving through the digestive tract, and satisfies the horse's behavioral drive to chew and graze. A feeding program that prioritizes grain over forage is built on the wrong foundation.

The general standard in equine nutrition is that horses should consume a minimum of 1 to 1.5 percent of their body weight in forage per day. For a 1,000-pound horse, that is 10 to 15 pounds of hay daily as a baseline — more for horses with higher workload or specific nutritional needs.

💡 Did You Know

The act of chewing hay produces saliva, which contains bicarbonate — a natural buffer for the stomach acid horses produce continuously. A horse that spends more time chewing hay produces more saliva and has a lower risk of gastric ulcers. This is one reason why providing free-choice hay or slow-feed hay nets is more than a convenience; it is a practical ulcer prevention strategy.

Types of Forage

Common Forage Types and Their Characteristics

Type 1

Grass Hay

Timothy, orchard grass, bermudagrass, and brome are common grass hay types. Grass hay is lower in protein and energy than legume hay and suitable for horses without high energy demands. It is appropriate for horses in light work, easy keepers, and as the primary hay for horses where legume content must be limited.

Type 2

Legume Hay (Alfalfa)

Alfalfa is the most commonly fed legume hay. It is higher in protein, calcium, and energy than grass hay. It is appropriate for horses with higher nutritional demands — young growing horses, lactating mares, and horses in hard work. Fed in excess to horses without these demands, it contributes to digestive upset and potentially to laminitis in metabolically sensitive horses.

Type 3

Mixed Hay

Many hay bales contain a blend of grass and legume species — often orchard grass mixed with alfalfa, or timothy mixed with clover. Mixed hay offers a balance between the higher energy of legume hay and the lower caloric content of pure grass hay. It is widely used and appropriate for many horses in moderate work.

Type 4

Pasture Grass

Fresh pasture provides forage that horses find highly palatable and motivating. Pasture quality varies significantly by season, species composition, and soil fertility. Lush spring and fall grass is high in non-structural carbohydrates (sugars and starches) and a trigger for laminitis in metabolically sensitive horses. Pasture access must be managed, not assumed to be safe at all times and in all amounts.

Hay quality matters as much as hay type. Dusty, moldy, or poor-quality hay is not just nutritionally inferior — it is a health risk. Mold in hay triggers respiratory problems and can cause colic. Dust and debris irritate the airway. Good hay is green to golden in color, free of mold and dust, pleasant-smelling, and leafy. Hay that smells musty or shows dark patches should not be fed.

Quality Indicators

How to Evaluate Hay Before Feeding

Hay quality assessment is a practical skill used every time hay is purchased or a new bale is opened. The key indicators are appearance, smell, texture, and stage of maturity at cutting. Hay cut early — before the seed heads fully develop — retains more leaves, which carry most of the nutritional value. Late-cut hay is stemmy, lower in nutrition, and higher in fiber that horses digest less efficiently.

Smell is one of the fastest quality checks. Good hay smells clean and slightly sweet. A musty, fermented, or unusual odor indicates mold or improper curing — conditions that disqualify the hay from use. Bales that feel hot inside may still be curing and should not be fed until they cool and stabilize, as heating forage can develop dangerous mold.

In the Barn

When a new hay delivery arrives, opening a bale and evaluating it before filling feeders is a standard practice — not optional. Feeding an entire delivery of poor-quality hay because it looked acceptable on the outside is a common cause of respiratory problems and feed-related colic in barns. A few minutes of inspection per bale prevents hours of veterinary management later.

Things to Remember

  • Forage is the foundation of the equine diet — horses should consume at least 1 to 1.5 percent of their body weight in forage daily.
  • Grass hay (timothy, orchard grass) is lower in energy and protein; legume hay (alfalfa) is higher — match hay type to the horse's workload and needs.
  • Lush spring and fall pasture is high in non-structural carbohydrates and a laminitis trigger for metabolically sensitive horses.
  • Good hay is green to golden, free of mold and dust, and pleasant-smelling — musty or dark hay should not be fed.
  • Chewing hay produces saliva that buffers stomach acid — more forage intake means more natural ulcer prevention.
