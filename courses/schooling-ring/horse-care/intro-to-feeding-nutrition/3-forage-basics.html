Intro to Feeding & Nutrition

Forage Basics

Hay and pasture are not optional extras. They are the foundation of a horse's diet. Understanding forage is one of the most important feeding skills in horse care.

Forage Is the Foundation

Forage means hay and pasture grass. It is the main food a horse should eat every day.

Horses are built to eat forage for most of the day. Their digestive system depends on fiber from hay and grass to keep everything working correctly. This fiber is broken down in the hindgut and turned into energy.

Forage also keeps food moving through the digestive system and helps prevent stomach problems. If a horse is fed mostly grain instead of forage, the diet is unbalanced and can lead to health issues.

A general guideline is that a horse should eat 1 to 1.5% of its body weight in forage every day. That means a 1,000-pound horse needs about 10 to 15 pounds of hay daily, and sometimes more depending on work level and needs.

💡 Did You Know

When a horse chews hay, it makes saliva. This saliva helps protect the stomach by reducing acid. Horses make stomach acid all the time, even when they are not eating. That is why eating forage often helps protect against stomach ulcers.

Types of Forage

Common Forage Types

Grass Hay

Grass hay includes types like timothy, orchard grass, bermuda grass, and brome.

  • Lower in protein and energy
  • Good for most adult horses in light work
  • Often used as the main everyday hay

Legume Hay (Alfalfa)

Alfalfa is the most common legume hay.

  • Higher in protein, calcium, and energy
  • Good for young horses, hard-working horses, and nursing mares
  • Can be too rich for horses that do not need extra energy

Mixed Hay

Mixed hay is a combination of grass and legume hay.

  • Balanced energy and nutrition
  • Common choice for many horses
  • Works well for horses in moderate work

Pasture Grass

Pasture is fresh grass horses graze on.

  • Very natural and enjoyable for horses
  • Changes with seasons and weather
  • Spring and fall grass can be very high in sugar

Some horses, especially those prone to weight gain or metabolic problems, need pasture time carefully managed because rich grass can cause health issues like laminitis.

Hay Quality Matters

Not all hay is safe or good quality. Good hay should be:

  • Green to golden in color
  • Free of mold
  • Free of dust
  • Smelling clean and slightly sweet
  • Leafy, not overly stemmy

Bad hay may smell musty, look dark, feel dusty, or have mold. This kind of hay can cause breathing problems, colic, or other serious health issues.

Quality Checks Before Feeding

  • Look at the color and texture.
  • Smell the hay for freshness.
  • Check for dust or mold inside the bale.
  • Make sure it feels dry and not hot.

Hay that feels warm inside or smells off should not be fed.

In the Barn

Every time new hay arrives, it should be checked before being fed to horses. Feeding bad hay without checking it first can lead to serious health problems, including breathing issues and digestive upset.

Taking a few minutes to inspect hay can prevent major problems later.

Things to Remember

  • Forage, such as hay and pasture, is the main part of a horse's diet.
  • Horses should eat about 1 to 1.5% of their body weight in forage daily.
  • Grass hay is lower energy, while alfalfa is higher energy.
  • Mixed hay is a balance of grass and legume hay.
  • Fresh pasture can be very high in sugar, especially in spring and fall.
  • Good hay is clean, dry, and free of mold or dust.
  • Chewing hay helps protect the stomach from ulcers.
  • Always check hay quality before feeding it.
