Key Concepts

Why do horses need constant access to forage?

Horses are designed to eat small amounts of forage throughout most of the day. In a natural setting, they may spend 16 to 18 hours grazing.

A horse's stomach produces acid all the time, even when the horse is not eating. If a horse goes too long without hay or grass, that acid can irritate the stomach lining and may lead to gastric ulcers.

Forage is also important because it feeds the helpful bacteria in the hindgut. These bacteria break down fiber and help provide energy for the horse. They work best when forage is available regularly.

What happens when feed is changed too quickly?

The hindgut contains millions of helpful bacteria that are used to digesting certain feeds.

When feed is changed suddenly, those bacteria do not have enough time to adjust. This can upset the digestive system and increase the risk of gas, colic, and laminitis.

Feed changes should be made slowly over 7 to 10 days.

Why are large grain meals risky?

Horses are not designed to eat large amounts of grain at one time.

When a horse eats too much grain in a single meal, the digestive system may not be able to process it properly. Extra starch can move into the hindgut, where it causes digestive problems.

Large grain meals increase the risk of colic and laminitis. Smaller meals spread throughout the day are much safer.

How much forage does a horse need each day?

A horse should eat at least 1% to 1.5% of its body weight in forage every day.

For a 1,000-pound horse, that equals about 10 to 15 pounds of hay daily as a minimum. Some horses need more depending on their workload, age, and overall health.

What is the difference between grass hay and alfalfa?

Grass hay, such as timothy or orchard grass, is lower in protein and energy. It is a good choice for many horses.

Alfalfa is a legume hay. It contains more protein, calcium, and energy than most grass hays. It is often fed to growing horses, nursing mares, and horses with higher nutritional needs.

Why can spring and fall grass be risky for some horses?

During spring and fall, grass often contains higher levels of natural sugars.

Some horses are sensitive to these sugars and have a greater risk of developing laminitis if they eat too much rich pasture. This is especially true for easy keepers and horses with metabolic conditions.

How does chewing hay help the stomach?

When horses chew hay, they produce saliva.

Saliva contains bicarbonate, which helps reduce the effects of stomach acid. The more a horse chews, the more saliva it produces.

This is one reason why regular access to hay is important for digestive health.

What should good hay look and smell like?

Good hay should be green to golden in color, leafy, and free of dust and mold.

It should smell fresh, clean, or slightly sweet.

Hay that smells musty, looks moldy, feels damp, or is unusually warm should not be fed.

How much water does a horse need each day?

Most horses need about 5 to 10 gallons of fresh, clean water each day.

Horses may need much more water during hot weather, after exercise, or when eating mostly dry hay. Some horses can drink more than 20 gallons a day in very hot conditions.

How should grain be fed compared to hay?

Hay should be available before grain is fed, or hay and grain should be fed at the same time.

Grain should not be fed on an empty stomach. Having forage available helps protect the stomach and supports healthy digestion.

Key Vocabulary

Hindgut

The back part of the digestive system where helpful bacteria break down fiber and turn it into energy.

Non-Structural Carbohydrates (NSC)

Sugars and starches found in grass and feed that provide energy but can cause problems when eaten in large amounts.

Legume Hay

A type of hay, such as alfalfa, that is higher in protein, calcium, and energy than grass hay.

Grass Hay

Hay made from grasses such as timothy or orchard grass. It is the main forage source for many horses.

Gastric Ulcer

A sore in the lining of the stomach caused by too much stomach acid and not enough forage.

Colic

A general term for abdominal pain or digestive discomfort in a horse.

Laminitis

A serious condition that causes inflammation and damage inside the hoof.

Free-Choice Hay

A feeding method where hay is available to the horse throughout the day instead of only at certain meal times.

Common Mistakes

Changing Feed Too Quickly

Sudden feed changes can upset the digestive system and increase the risk of colic and laminitis. Feed changes should be made over 7 to 10 days.

Not Providing Constant Access to Water

Horses should always have access to clean, fresh water. Limited water intake can lead to dehydration and digestive problems.

Feeding Too Much Grain at One Time

Large grain meals can overwhelm the digestive system and increase the risk of digestive upset.

Feeding Grain Before Hay

Grain should not be fed on an empty stomach. Providing hay first helps support healthy digestion.

Feeding Poor-Quality Hay

Dusty, moldy, damp, or spoiled hay can cause illness, breathing problems, and digestive issues.
