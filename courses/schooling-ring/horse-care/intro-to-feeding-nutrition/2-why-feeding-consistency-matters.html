Intro to Feeding & Nutrition

Why Feeding Consistency Matters

The horse's digestive system is designed for continuous, consistent intake. Disrupting that pattern is the most common cause of preventable digestive emergencies.

A Digestive System Built for Consistency

Horses are non-ruminant herbivores with a digestive tract designed for near-continuous intake of small amounts of forage. In the wild, horses graze for 16 to 18 hours per day, moving slowly across a landscape and consuming roughage almost without interruption. Domestic feeding schedules rarely replicate this pattern, but they must account for it.

The consequences of ignoring the horse's digestive design are concrete and well-documented. Sudden feed changes, missed meals, extended periods without forage, and overly large grain meals are direct causes of colic, laminitis, gastric ulcers, and other digestive emergencies that are both serious and preventable.

💡 Did You Know

A horse's stomach is small relative to its body size — it holds only about 2 to 4 gallons and empties into the small intestine within a few hours. Unlike a cow's rumen, the horse's stomach does not ferment feed. The bulk of fermentation happens in the hindgut (cecum and large colon), which is why changes in fiber intake destabilize the hindgut microbial population and trigger digestive upset.

The Core Principles

Four Rules That Govern Safe Horse Feeding

Rule 1

Feed on a Consistent Schedule

Horses anticipate feeding time and begin producing gastric acid in preparation. When feeding is delayed or irregular, that acid accumulates against an empty stomach, contributing to ulcer development. Feeding at the same times each day reduces gastric stress and supports digestive regularity.

Rule 2

Change Feed Gradually

The hindgut microbiome — the bacterial population that ferments fiber — adapts to the type of feed currently being provided. A sudden switch in hay type, grain, or forage source disrupts this population. Feed changes should be made over a minimum of seven to ten days, introducing the new feed in small amounts while reducing the old feed proportionally.

Rule 3

Keep Forage Available

Long gaps without forage — more than three to four hours — allow the stomach to accumulate acid without buffering food material. In horses without access to pasture, providing hay in nets or feeders that slow intake can bridge these gaps. Forage is the most important part of the diet; grain supplements it, not the reverse.

Rule 4

Feed Small Amounts Frequently

A single large grain meal overwhelms the small intestine's digestive capacity. Undigested starch that passes into the hindgut disrupts the microbial balance and can trigger laminitis and colic. If grain is part of the feeding program, dividing it across multiple smaller meals throughout the day is safer than one or two large ones.

The two most dangerous words in horse feeding are "sudden change." Whether it is a new hay batch, a switch in grain brand, the arrival of spring grass, or a late meal — abrupt changes to what the horse eats or when are the most preventable triggers of colic, laminitis, and gastric ulcers. Every feeding decision should be measured against whether it maintains consistency.

In the Barn

The first sign that something is wrong in a horse's feeding routine is often behavioral: a horse that paws, looks at its flank, stands unusually still, or shows no interest in food may be in digestive distress. A horse that is off feed — refusing hay it normally eats — is a horse that warrants immediate attention. Feeding consistency is partly about prevention; feeding observation is about catching problems early.

Things to Remember

  • Horses are designed for near-continuous forage intake — the domestic schedule must account for this design, not ignore it.
  • Sudden feed changes disrupt the hindgut microbial population and are a primary cause of colic and laminitis.
  • Feed changes should happen over a minimum of seven to ten days, not overnight.
  • Long gaps without forage allow gastric acid to accumulate against an empty stomach, contributing to ulcer development.
  • Large single grain meals are dangerous — undigested starch reaching the hindgut triggers serious digestive events.
