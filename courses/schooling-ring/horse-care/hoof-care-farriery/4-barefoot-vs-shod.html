Hoof Care & Farriery

Barefoot vs. Shod

When horses need shoes, when barefoot is appropriate, and how to evaluate the decision based on workload, hoof quality, and footing.

The barefoot-versus-shod decision is not ideological — it is practical, individual, and based on specific factors for a specific horse. Both approaches are viable when applied appropriately; both cause harm when applied inappropriately. Understanding the factors that favor each approach allows the horseperson to make an informed decision in consultation with their farrier and veterinarian.

The fundamental question is not 'shoes or no shoes?' but 'what does this horse's hoof need to function soundly under the demands placed on it?' The answer may change over a horse's life as workload, footing, and hoof quality evolve.

💡 Did You Know

Steel horseshoes as commonly used today are a relatively modern invention — widespread systematic horseshoeing developed during the Middle Ages as horses were put to work on paved roads and harder surfaces. Horses used primarily on soft ground historically went unshod. The same logic applies today: the decision follows the footing and workload, not tradition.

Decision Factors
Factor 1

Hoof Quality and Thickness

Horses with thin soles, brittle walls, or hooves that crack and chip rapidly may not be able to maintain adequate horn integrity under any but the most forgiving footing conditions. Shoeing protects these hooves from wear that exceeds their growth rate. Horses with thick soles, dense, flexible walls, and good horn quality often maintain sound hooves without shoes even in moderate work.

Factor 2

Work Type and Intensity

Hard pavement, deep arena footing, rocky trails, and performance work requiring traction (cutting, reining, jumping, eventing) may exceed the wear capacity of the barefoot hoof. Horses working exclusively on soft footing — grass, sand, or packed dirt — generate less wear and may maintain adequate hoof integrity without shoes. Road work or performance work on hard surfaces almost universally requires shoes.

Factor 3

Footing and Environment

Horses that live in consistently wet conditions develop softer, less wear-resistant hooves. Horses in very dry, rocky environments may wear hooves faster than the growth rate permits. The footing a horse lives and works on is as important as the work intensity in determining whether shoes are appropriate.

Factor 4

Therapeutic Shoeing

Some horses require shoes for therapeutic rather than protection reasons. Horses with laminitis, navicular syndrome, coffin joint arthritis, or white line disease may benefit from specific shoe types (bar shoes, heart bar shoes, wide-web shoes) that modify how forces are distributed through the compromised structure. Therapeutic shoeing is prescribed in consultation with the veterinarian, not selected by the horseperson independently.

The transition from shod to barefoot requires a transition period — typically 3 to 6 months — during which the hoof rebuilds structural integrity without the shoe's support. Removing shoes and immediately returning a horse to its previous work on the same footing is a common source of soreness and transitional lameness. A gradual reduction in work intensity on more forgiving footing during the transition period protects the horse while the hoof adapts.

The horse boot is a third option that combines elements of both approaches: a removable protective covering worn during work, removed during turnout. Boots allow the hoof to flex and receive natural ground stimulation during rest while providing protection during work periods that would otherwise require a shoe. Boot fit and durability under the specific conditions of use determine whether they are a practical solution for a given horse and management system.

In the Barn

When evaluating a horse for barefoot transition, the most useful indicators are: how quickly the hoof chips or cracks between farrier visits, whether the horse shows sensitivity on gravel or hard ground, and how the sole depth and concavity compare to the horse's workload demands. A horse that chips aggressively between trims, walks short on gravel, or has flat soles with minimal concavity is telling the evaluator something about its current footing demands relative to hoof quality. These observations, combined with the farrier's assessment of wall thickness and sole depth, inform the decision more reliably than either strong advocacy for barefoot management or reflexive preference for shoes.

Things to Remember

  • Barefoot viability depends on hoof quality, work type, footing, and the individual horse — not ideology.
  • Shod horses still need 6–8 week farriery cycles — shoes do not stop hoof growth.
  • Barefoot transition requires 3–6 months with reduced work on forgiving footing for the hoof to rebuild structural integrity.
  • Therapeutic shoes are prescribed in consultation with the veterinarian — not selected independently by the owner.
