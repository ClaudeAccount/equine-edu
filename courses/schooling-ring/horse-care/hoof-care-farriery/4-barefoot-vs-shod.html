Hoof Care & Farriery

Barefoot vs. Shod

Choosing the right hoof care for each horse.

Every Horse Is Different

Some horses do very well without shoes, while others need the protection or support that shoes provide.

There is no single answer that works for every horse. The best choice depends on the horse's hoof quality, workload, environment, and overall hoof health.

As a horse's needs change over time, its hoof care plan may change as well. A horse that goes barefoot for many years may eventually need shoes, while another horse may be able to transition from shoes to barefoot with the right management.

Regular hoof care and guidance from a farrier and veterinarian help determine what is best for each horse.

💡 Did You Know

Horseshoes have been used for centuries to protect hooves from excessive wear and provide additional support. Today, some horses wear shoes year-round, while others remain healthy and comfortable with regular trimming alone.

Choosing Between Barefoot and Shoes

Several factors help determine whether a horse should remain barefoot or wear shoes.

Factor 1

Hoof Quality

Healthy hooves are the foundation of every hoof care plan. Some horses naturally have strong hoof walls and thick soles that hold up well to regular work. These horses may remain comfortable without shoes. Other horses have thinner soles, weaker hoof walls, or hooves that wear faster than they grow. These horses may benefit from the added protection that shoes provide.

Factor 2

Type of Work

The work a horse performs has a major effect on its hooves. Horses that travel long distances, compete in athletic events, work on pavement, or regularly travel over rocky trails often place greater demands on their feet. Horses that spend most of their time on pasture or work on softer footing may be able to remain barefoot if their hooves stay healthy and balanced.

Factor 3

Ground Conditions

The surfaces where a horse lives and works also affect hoof health. Very rocky or abrasive ground may wear hooves faster than they can grow. Wet conditions can soften the hoof, while extremely dry conditions may increase the risk of cracks. Considering both the horse's environment and workload helps determine the most appropriate hoof care plan.

Factor 4

Medical or Corrective Needs

Some horses wear shoes because they need additional support rather than protection. Veterinarians and farriers sometimes recommend therapeutic shoes for horses recovering from injury or managing certain hoof conditions, such as laminitis or navicular disease. These specialized shoes are designed to support healing, improve comfort, or change how weight is distributed across the hoof. Therapeutic shoeing should always be planned by a veterinarian working together with a qualified farrier.

Transitioning Between Shoes and Barefoot

Moving from shoes to barefoot is a gradual process.

As the hoof adjusts, it may need time to strengthen and adapt to carrying more wear without the protection of a shoe.

During this transition, some horses benefit from lighter work, softer footing, or temporary use of hoof boots.

The length of the transition varies from horse to horse and depends on hoof quality, workload, and management.

Hoof Boots

Hoof boots provide another option for some horses.

Unlike metal shoes, hoof boots can be removed when they are no longer needed.

Some horses wear boots only while riding or working on rough terrain and remain barefoot the rest of the time.

For hoof boots to work well, they must fit correctly and be appropriate for the horse's activity.

Working With Your Hoof Care Team

Choosing whether a horse should go barefoot or wear shoes is not a one-time decision.

Farriers and veterinarians evaluate:

  • Hoof balance
  • Hoof quality
  • Wear patterns
  • Soundness
  • Workload
  • Living conditions

These factors help determine the hoof care plan that best supports the horse's long-term comfort and health.

In the Barn

Daily hoof care provides valuable clues about whether a horse's current hoof care plan is working. While cleaning the hooves, watch for excessive chipping, cracks, loose shoes, soreness on hard or rocky ground, or unusual wear. Changes like these may indicate that the horse's hoof care needs have changed and should be discussed with a farrier.

Things to Remember

  • Some horses do well barefoot, while others benefit from wearing shoes.
  • The best choice depends on hoof quality, workload, footing, and overall hoof health.
  • Hooves continue to grow whether a horse wears shoes or not, so regular farrier care is always needed.
  • Horses transitioning from shoes to barefoot may need time to adjust.
  • Hoof boots can provide temporary protection for some barefoot horses.
  • Farriers and veterinarians work together to recommend the hoof care plan that best supports each horse.
