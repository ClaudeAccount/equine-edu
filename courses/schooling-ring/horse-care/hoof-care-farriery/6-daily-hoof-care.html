Hoof Care & Farriery

Daily Hoof Care Hoof Care

Hoof picking technique, daily inspection checklist, and how routine care prevents the majority of hoof problems.

Daily hoof care — picking out the feet, inspecting each hoof, and noting any changes — is the most fundamental and most impactful routine in horse management. It takes approximately two minutes per horse and prevents the majority of common hoof problems from progressing beyond the earliest, most easily treated stage.

Daily hoof care is not a task that can be deferred when time is short. A horse whose feet are not picked regularly accumulates packed debris, moisture, and bacteria against the frog and sole. This is the environment in which thrush develops, foreign bodies are missed, and subtle lameness goes undetected.

Daily Hoof Care Protocol
Step 1

Asking for the Foot

Stand at the horse's shoulder facing the rear. Run a hand down the back of the leg from knee or hock to fetlock, then apply gentle rearward pressure at the fetlock to ask the horse to flex the leg. Hold the foot securely — not gripping tightly, which makes horses uncomfortable — with one hand supporting under the toe and the other near the coronary band. Never crouch beneath a horse; kneel or squat with the ability to step away quickly.

Step 2

The Pick-Out Technique

Use the hoof pick from heel to toe — moving debris out of the hoof, not further in. Pay particular attention to the sulci on either side of the frog and the central groove, where debris and manure accumulate most densely. Clear the white line area and the junction between the sole and the wall. The frog itself should not be aggressively scraped; it should be cleared of debris but its tissue should be left intact.

Step 3

What to Look For

While picking, inspect for: heat in the wall or sole (may indicate inflammation); foul smell or black discharge from the sulci (thrush); any embedded objects such as rocks, nails, or wire; cracks in the hoof wall (superficial or deep); changes in the shape of the frog (shrinkage may indicate lack of ground contact); any swelling at the coronary band. Changes from the previous day's baseline are more important than any single finding.

Step 4

When to Call the Farrier or Veterinarian

Farrier call indicators: a shoe that is loose, shifted, or missing; cracks that appear to be deepening or reaching the coronary band; flares or wall chips that extend beyond the white line. Veterinarian call indicators: any lameness of more than mild degree; heat and increased digital pulse in one or more feet; swelling at or above the coronary band; any penetrating wound to the sole; any suspicion of laminitis, abscess, or serious structural problem.

The difference between a hoof abscess found at day 3 and the same abscess found at day 1 is a horse that is comfortable by day 4 versus a horse that has been lame for a week. A subtly short stride on day 1, caught during daily hoof inspection, is the signal. Catching it on day 3 means the abscess has tracked and formed a pocket; catching it on day 1 means the farrier or veterinarian can often find and open it before the horse is significantly uncomfortable.

Hoof care is an opportunity for a brief but systematic health check beyond the hooves. While handling each foot, a handler is in close proximity to the entire lower leg. Any swelling, heat, or sensitivity in the tendons, fetlock, pastern, or coronary band that was not present the previous day should be noted and reported to the veterinarian if it persists or worsens. Many early lameness issues are first detected during routine hoof handling, not during ridden work.

In the Barn

Daily hoof picking takes less than five minutes per horse and is one of the highest-return preventive care practices available. It identifies thrush, stones, foreign bodies, and early signs of abscess before they develop into lameness. Horses that are picked out regularly tolerate the farrier easily because the handling is routine. Hooves left without regular maintenance accumulate material that creates the conditions for thrush and uneven wall growth. Five minutes daily is almost always less costly than the lameness treatment it prevents.

Things to Remember

  • Pick heel-to-toe to clear debris outward; pay special attention to sulci and white line area
  • Daily inspection checklist: heat, foul smell, foreign bodies, cracks, frog changes, coronary band swelling
  • Call vet if: any lameness >mild, heat + digital pulse, swelling at coronary band, penetrating wound to sole
  • Daily picking catches thrush, abscesses, and foreign bodies at earliest, most treatable stage
