Hoof Care & Farriery

Common Hoof Conditions

Thrush, white line disease, laminitis, navicular syndrome, and other common hoof health issues: recognition and management.

The most common hoof health conditions in domestic horses are largely preventable through consistent daily care and regular farriery. When they do occur, early recognition — enabled by the daily hoof-picking routine — allows intervention before damage becomes permanent. This module covers recognition, causes, and management of the conditions most frequently encountered in routine horse care.

Several of the most serious hoof conditions — laminitis in particular — require immediate veterinary involvement. This module provides the knowledge base for recognition, first response, and informed veterinary communication.

💡 Did You Know

Thrush is one of the most common and most preventable hoof conditions — yet it persists widely because many handlers do not pick feet daily. In horses whose feet are picked out every day, thrush is caught and treated immediately when it begins, before it reaches the sensitive frog. In horses whose feet are picked weekly or less, the same thrush often progresses to the sensitive tissue before being noticed.

Thrush is the most common hoof infection encountered in routine care. It is caused by management conditions, not by inherent hoof weakness — and it is almost entirely preventable through daily hoof picking and clean stabling.

Thrush is a bacterial infection of the frog, characterized by a black, malodorous discharge from the sulci — the grooves on either side of the frog and in the central groove. It results from prolonged exposure to wet, anaerobic conditions: wet stalls, muddy paddocks, or inconsistent hoof picking that allows manure and debris to pack against the frog tissue. The anaerobic bacteria responsible for thrush thrive wherever oxygen is excluded by packed material.

Early thrush — a slight darkening and odor at the sulci — is treated by cleaning the hoof thoroughly, trimming excess frog tissue that creates deep pockets, and applying an antiseptic treatment (dilute iodine, copper sulfate, or commercial thrush treatment) to the affected tissue. Management correction — clean stabling and daily picking — is as important as the topical treatment. Severe thrush that invades the sensitive frog causes lameness and tracks into the white line, requiring veterinary assessment and more aggressive debridement.

Thrush is a management condition, not a hoof condition. The bacteria are always present in the environment — thrush develops when management allows the conditions for those bacteria to thrive. A horse in a clean, dry environment with daily hoof picking rarely develops thrush; a horse in a consistently wet, dirty environment develops it regardless of topical treatment.

White line disease is a progressive infection of the non-pigmented horn at the hoof wall-sole junction. Unlike thrush, it is not always visually obvious from the outside — which is why daily hoof inspection of the sole is necessary to catch it early.

White line disease, also called seedy toe, damages the non-pigmented horn in the white line. Bacteria and fungi can enter through cracks, changes after laminitis, or breaks where the hoof wall meets the sole. The damaged horn may look powdery, crumbly, and gray-white. The infection can move upward between the hoof wall and the inner hoof structures. In serious cases, part of the hoof wall may separate from its laminar attachment.

Treatment requires removing — resecting — all infected horn, which often means cutting away the affected section of hoof wall. The exposed area is kept clean and dry with antiseptic dressings while new horn grows in from the coronary band. A farrier may apply a patch or support shoe to protect the affected area during regrowth. Horses with active white line disease should be managed on clean, dry footing. The condition recurs if the underlying entry point (a crack or wall defect) is not addressed.

White line disease is identified from the sole, not the wall. The outer hoof wall may appear normal while significant separation tracks upward on the inside. Daily hoof inspection that includes examining the sole — not just picking out debris — allows earlier detection. If the white line appears widened, crumbly, or discolored when the hoof is picked, a farrier should assess it promptly.

Laminitis is a medical emergency requiring immediate veterinary involvement. The window between early signs and irreversible structural damage can be as short as 24 to 48 hours. Early recognition is what determines whether a horse recovers fully or develops chronic founder.

Laminitis is inflammation of the laminar tissue connecting the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. When the laminae become compromised, the coffin bone loses its structural support and can rotate downward or sink within the hoof capsule — a sequela called founder that causes permanent, severe damage to the hoof's architecture. Feed-related causes include carbohydrate overload from excess grain or high-sugar pasture. Other causes include retained placenta in mares, systemic illness, and Cushing's disease (PPID).

Early signs of laminitis include: increased digital pulse (a bounding, easily felt pulse at the back of the fetlock), heat in the hooves, and a characteristic rocked-back stance in which the horse shifts weight onto the heels to relieve toe pressure. Affected horses may be reluctant to move, walk with a short choppy stride, or refuse to turn. Horses with EMS, PPID, or prior laminitis are at significantly elevated risk and require proactive dietary and management protocols to reduce recurrence risk.

Laminitis is a time-critical emergency. A horse showing early signs — heat, digital pulse, rocked-back stance — should be immediately removed from pasture, confined to a stall with deep bedding to support the heels, and have the veterinarian contacted at once. Observation to see if it improves on its own loses the treatment window in which most damage is preventable.

Navicular syndrome is a chronic condition of the palmar foot — the navicular bone, navicular bursa, or deep digital flexor tendon — that is manageable but not curable. Early recognition and consistent farriery management extend working soundness significantly.

Navicular syndrome (palmar foot pain) affects the structures at the back of the hoof: the navicular bone, navicular bursa, and the deep digital flexor tendon as it passes over the bone. It is most common in the front feet of working horses and is associated with chronic loading of these structures by long-toe-low-heel hoof conformation. The coffin bone angle, when reduced by a long toe, increases the load on the navicular bone with each step — over time causing degenerative changes that become painful.

Signs include a shortened, choppy stride on hard ground, toe-first landing (to avoid loading the painful heel), stumbling, and reluctance on circles. Diagnosis requires veterinary assessment including hoof testers, nerve blocks, and radiographs. Management centers on corrective shoeing to restore correct hoof angle and reduce navicular bone loading — typically a shoe with raised heels, a rolled toe, or a bar shoe. Exercise modification and in some cases medication (bisphosphonates, corticosteroid joint injections) are used alongside shoeing. Many horses remain in comfortable work for years with consistent management.

Long-toe-low-heel conformation is the single most modifiable risk factor for navicular syndrome. A horse consistently maintained at the correct hoof angle with timely farriery reduces loading on the navicular apparatus compared to a horse allowed to develop extended toes between irregular farriery visits. Prevention through consistent farriery is more effective than treatment after damage has occurred.

In the Barn

Daily hoof picking is the single most effective preventive measure for the most common hoof conditions. Thrush, white line disease, and foreign body injuries are all identified during the daily pick-out routine. A handler who picks feet daily catches these conditions at the earliest, most easily treated stage. A handler who picks feet occasionally discovers conditions that have been present — and progressing — for weeks. Dry, cracked hoof walls that develop horizontal or vertical cracks also require farrier assessment: deep vertical cracks extending to the coronary band or sole can cause lameness and may require stabilization with screws, staples, or fiberglass repair to prevent further propagation.

Things to Remember

  • Thrush: black malodorous discharge in frog sulci; treated with cleaning, debridement, and antiseptic; caused by wet, anaerobic conditions — a management problem, not a hoof disease.
  • White line disease: powdery, crumbly horn in the white line; resect all infected tissue; identified from the sole during daily inspection.
  • Laminitis: emergency — coffin bone rotation possible within 24–48 hours; remove from pasture, stall rest with deep bedding, immediate veterinary contact.
  • Navicular syndrome: long-toe-low-heel is primary risk factor; managed with corrective shoeing; not curable but often manageable for years.
