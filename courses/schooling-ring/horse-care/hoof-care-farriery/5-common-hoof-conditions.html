Hoof Care & Farriery

Common Hoof Conditions Conditions

Thrush, white line disease, laminitis, navicular syndrome, and other common hoof health issues: recognition and management.

The most common hoof health conditions in domestic horses are largely preventable through consistent daily care and regular farriery. When they do occur, early recognition — enabled by the daily hoof-picking routine — allows intervention before damage becomes permanent. This module covers recognition, causes, and management of the conditions most frequently encountered in routine horse care.

Several of the most serious hoof conditions — laminitis in particular — require immediate veterinary involvement. This module provides the knowledge base for recognition, first response, and informed veterinary communication.

Common Hoof Conditions

Thrush

Thrush is a bacterial infection of the frog, characterized by a black, malodorous discharge from the sulci (grooves on either side of the frog and in the central groove). It results from prolonged exposure to wet, anaerobic conditions — wet stalls, muddy paddocks, or inconsistent hoof picking that allows manure and debris to pack against the frog. Early thrush is treated by cleaning the hoof thoroughly, trimming excess frog tissue, and applying an antiseptic treatment. Severe thrush that invades the sensitive frog causes lameness and requires veterinary treatment.

White Line Disease

White line disease is a separation and destruction of the non-pigmented horn in the white line, typically caused by bacteria or fungi entering through cracks or after laminitis episodes. The affected horn is crumbly, gray-white, and has a characteristic powdery texture. Treatment involves removing (resecting) all infected horn, which may require a portion of the hoof wall to be cut away. The exposed area is kept clean and dry while new horn grows in. Horses with compromised white line integrity should be managed on clean, dry footing.

Laminitis

Laminitis is inflammation of the laminar tissue connecting the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. In the acute phase, affected horses show heat in the feet, increased digital pulse, and a characteristic rocked-back stance (resting weight on the heels to relieve toe pressure). Laminitis is an emergency — laminar destruction can progress to coffin bone rotation or sinking within 24 to 48 hours. Causes include carbohydrate overload, pasture consumption in susceptible horses, retained placenta, and systemic illness.

Navicular Syndrome

Navicular syndrome (palmar foot pain) is a chronic, degenerative condition affecting the navicular bone, navicular bursa, or deep digital flexor tendon, most commonly in the front feet of working horses. Signs include a shortened, choppy stride, toe-first landing (avoiding heel contact), and stumbling. The long-toe-low-heel conformation is a significant risk factor. Management includes corrective shoeing, targeted exercise modification, and in some cases medication. It is not curable but is often manageable for years with appropriate farriery and veterinary collaboration.

Daily hoof picking is the single most effective preventive measure for the most common hoof conditions. Thrush, white line disease, and foreign body injuries are all identified during the daily pick-out routine. A handler who picks feet daily catches these conditions at the earliest, most easily treated stage. A handler who picks feet occasionally discovers conditions that have been present — and progressing — for weeks.

Dry, cracked hooves that develop horizontal or vertical cracks require assessment by the farrier to determine whether the crack is superficial (cosmetic, growing out) or structural (penetrating the sensitive structures). Deep vertical cracks that extend to the coronary band or to the sole can cause lameness and may require stabilization with screws, staples, or fiberglass repair to prevent further propagation.

Things to Remember

  • Thrush: black malodorous discharge in frog sulci; treated with cleaning + antiseptic; caused by anaerobic wet conditions
  • White line disease: powdery crumbly horn in white line; resect all infected tissue; manage on dry footing
  • Laminitis: emergency — coffin bone rotation possible in 24–48 hrs; immediate vet contact required
  • Navicular syndrome: long-toe-low-heel is major risk factor; corrective shoeing is primary management
