Hoof Care & Farriery

Common Hoof Conditions

How to spot common hoof problems and know when to get help.

Healthy Hooves Start With Daily Care

Many hoof problems can be stopped before they start. Daily hoof care, clean living space, and regular farrier visits do most of the work.

Cleaning a horse's feet each day makes it easier to spot changes early. Small problems are usually much easier to treat than ones that have been growing for weeks.

This lesson covers a few common hoof problems. It explains what causes each one, the signs to watch for, and the first steps to take if you find a problem.

Some hoof problems need a vet right away. Learning the warning signs helps protect the horse's health and soundness over the long run.

💡 Did You Know

Daily hoof picking is one of the simplest ways to prevent hoof problems. It removes packed mud, manure, and stones, and it makes it easier to spot changes in the hoof before they get serious.

Thrush is the most common hoof infection. It comes from wet, dirty conditions, and daily care keeps most horses from getting it.

What Is Thrush?

Thrush is a germ infection of the frog, mostly in the grooves along each side. It grows best in wet, dirty spots where germs can spread.

Signs of Thrush

  • A strong, bad smell
  • Black or dark goo in the frog grooves
  • Soft or damaged frog
  • Soreness when the frog is touched
  • Limping in bad cases

What Causes Thrush?

Thrush is more likely when hooves stay wet or dirty for a long time. Risk goes up with:

  • Muddy paddocks
  • Dirty stalls
  • Feet that are not picked often
  • Deep frog grooves that trap dirt

What Should Be Done?

Mild thrush often gets better with:

  • Daily hoof cleaning
  • Cleaner, drier footing
  • Trimming by a farrier when needed
  • A thrush treatment your vet or farrier suggests

If the infection is bad, causes pain, or does not get better, call a vet.

Thrush comes from how a horse is kept, not from a weak hoof. Regular hoof picking, a clean living space, and routine farrier care greatly lower the risk.

White line disease eats away the hoof from the inside, so you often cannot see it from the outside. Checking the bottom of the foot helps you catch it early.

What Is White Line Disease?

White line disease is an infection where the hoof wall meets the sole. Germs or fungus get in through weak or split spots in the white line and slowly break down the hoof.

Signs of White Line Disease

  • A stretched or widened white line
  • Dry, crumbly hoof material
  • Hollow spots under the hoof wall
  • Cracks or a split in the hoof wall
  • Limping in worse cases

The damage often starts inside the hoof, so careful cleaning and checking matter.

What Causes White Line Disease?

It starts when germs or fungus get into damaged spots in the white line. Poor hoof balance, cracks, and a hoof that is splitting all raise the risk.

What Should Be Done?

  • Removing the damaged hoof material
  • Corrective trimming by a farrier
  • Keeping the hoof clean and dry
  • Following the vet's advice if the infection is large

Treating it early often prevents worse damage.

You find white line disease from the bottom of the foot, not the outside wall. When you pick the foot, look at the sole and white line. If the white line looks wide, crumbly, or off-color, have a farrier check it soon.

Laminitis is a medical emergency. Call the vet right away. Quick action is what decides whether a horse recovers or stays hurt.

What Is Laminitis?

Laminitis is a painful swelling of the laminae, the soft tissue that holds the coffin bone to the inside of the hoof wall.

If the laminae get badly hurt, the coffin bone can tip or drop inside the hoof. This serious problem is often called founder. Laminitis is a medical emergency.

Signs of Laminitis

  • Warm hooves
  • A strong pulse at the back of the ankle
  • Not wanting to move
  • Short, careful steps
  • Leaning back to take weight off the front feet
  • Trouble turning

What Causes Laminitis?

  • Eating too much grain
  • Sudden time on rich grass
  • Certain illnesses
  • Cushing's disease (also called PPID), a hormone problem

What Should Be Done?

  • Take the horse off grass if it makes sense.
  • Put the horse on soft footing or deep bedding.
  • Keep it from moving more than needed, unless the vet says otherwise.
  • Call a vet right away.

Quick treatment greatly improves the chance of a good recovery.

Laminitis is a race against time. If a horse shows early signs, like hot feet, a pounding pulse, or leaning back, act now. Do not wait overnight to see if it gets better.

Navicular syndrome is a long-term pain in the back of the foot. It usually cannot be cured, but many horses stay comfortable with good care.

What Is Navicular Syndrome?

Navicular syndrome is a long-term problem that causes pain in the back part of the hoof. Several parts can be involved, like the navicular bone, the nearby ligaments, and the deep tendon that runs over it.

Signs of Navicular Syndrome

  • Short, choppy steps
  • Landing toe-first
  • Not wanting to turn
  • Stumbling
  • Front-foot lameness that comes and goes

What Causes Navicular Syndrome?

  • Hoof imbalance
  • Long toes and low heels
  • Repeated stress on the back of the foot
  • The horse's own build

What Should Be Done?

A vet and farrier work together to manage it. Care may include:

  • Corrective trimming or shoeing
  • Changes in exercise
  • Pain relief
  • Ongoing hoof care

Navicular syndrome usually cannot be cured, but many horses stay comfortable and active with the right care.

A long toe and low heel make navicular worse. On-time trims that keep the hoof at the right angle take strain off the back of the foot and help more than any fix tried after damage is done.

In the Barn

Cleaning the feet each day gives you a chance to check each foot closely.

Watch for:

  • Cracks
  • Bad smells
  • Black goo
  • A loose or stretched white line
  • Warm hooves
  • Swelling around the coronary band
  • Sudden limping
  • Stones or other stuck objects

Telling someone about changes early lets many hoof problems be treated before they get worse.

Things to Remember

  • Daily hoof care helps prevent many common hoof problems.
  • Thrush is a germ infection of the frog, often from wet, dirty conditions.
  • White line disease affects where the hoof wall meets the sole and should be treated soon.
  • Laminitis is a medical emergency that needs a vet right away.
  • Navicular syndrome is long-term but can often be managed with good vet and farrier care.
  • Regular farrier visits and catching problems early are key to healthy hooves.
