Equine Nutrition

Feed-Related Health Problems

Colic, laminitis, choke, gastric ulcers, and other conditions with direct nutritional causes or contributors.

The majority of common equine health emergencies have nutritional or feeding management causes. Colic, laminitis, choke, and gastric ulcers are not random misfortunes — they are predictable consequences of specific feeding errors or management patterns. Understanding the mechanisms that produce each condition is the foundation for preventing them.

This module does not replace veterinary care — all of these conditions require veterinary diagnosis and treatment. It provides the knowledge base that prevents them from occurring and supports informed veterinary communication when they do.

💡 Did You Know

Colic is the leading cause of premature death in horses, accounting for an estimated 28–33% of equine deaths. Despite this, the vast majority of colic episodes are mild and resolve with veterinary treatment. Most are preventable: continuous forage access, gradual feed changes, and avoiding large grain meals address the primary risk factors for the most common types.

Colic is abdominal pain — a symptom with many causes. Feed-related causes account for a large proportion of cases and are almost entirely preventable through consistent management practices.

Colic is abdominal pain in the horse — a symptom, not a diagnosis. Feed-related causes include: large grain meals overwhelming the small intestine's starch capacity (leading to hindgut fermentation disturbance), sudden diet changes disrupting hindgut microbial balance, inadequate forage causing decreased gut motility, and dehydration thickening intestinal contents and reducing gut transit. Signs include pawing, looking at flanks, refusing feed, rolling, sweating, and elevated heart rate.

All colic warrants veterinary contact. Signs of severe pain (continuous, unrelenting rolling; inability to stand; sweating without cause) or systemic distress (elevated heart rate above 60 bpm, pale or white mucous membranes) are emergency indicators requiring immediate veterinary response. Walking a mildly colicky horse is appropriate while waiting for veterinary assessment; it should not replace or delay that assessment.

A handler cannot distinguish mild from surgical colic at the farm level. Gas colic can resolve with walking; a strangulation requires surgery within hours. Any colic that does not resolve promptly, that recurs, or that produces signs of systemic distress requires veterinary evaluation — not extended home management.

Laminitis is a medical emergency. Carbohydrate overload — from excess grain or lush pasture — is the primary feed-related trigger. Early recognition and intervention prevent the permanent structural damage that makes chronic laminitis a career-ending condition.

Laminitis is inflammation of the laminar tissue connecting the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. When the laminae become compromised, the coffin bone loses its support within the hoof capsule and can rotate downward or sink — a sequela that causes permanent and severe damage. Feed-related causes include carbohydrate overload (grain founder from unrestricted grain access), high-sugar pasture intake in susceptible horses, and rapid diet changes that flood the hindgut with fermentable carbohydrate before the microbial population can adapt.

Early signs include: increased digital pulse (palpable at the back of the fetlock), heat in the hooves, a characteristic rocked-back stance where the horse shifts weight off the toes, and reluctance to move or walk on hard surfaces. Horses with metabolic syndrome (EMS), Cushing's disease (PPID), or a prior laminitis episode are at significantly elevated risk and require dietary management to prevent recurrence. The coffin bone can rotate or sink within 24 to 48 hours of the inflammatory event — making early recognition and immediate veterinary contact essential.

Laminitis is a time-critical emergency — intervention in the first 24 hours makes the difference between recovery and permanent damage. A horse that shows early signs (heat, digital pulse, rocked-back stance) should be immediately removed from pasture and confined to a stall with deep bedding while the veterinarian is contacted — not observed overnight to see if it improves.

Choke in horses is esophageal obstruction, not airway obstruction — horses can still breathe. It requires veterinary attention and is most often preventable through feed management practices and routine dental care.

Choke in horses is esophageal obstruction — material stuck in the esophagus rather than the trachea (as in human choking). Because the esophagus is separate from the airway, horses in choke can still breathe, but they cannot swallow. Signs include feed material and saliva draining from both nostrils, repeated swallowing attempts, neck stretching and extending, coughing, and distress. The horse typically refuses to eat and may show signs of discomfort in the throat and neck area.

Choke most commonly occurs with pelleted or extruded feeds eaten too rapidly, sugar beet pulp fed dry rather than soaked, hay cubes, or coarse hay in horses with dental problems that prevent proper chewing. It resolves spontaneously in many cases as saliva softens the obstruction, but veterinary treatment (sedation and lavage to clear the obstruction) is required when it does not. The primary risk of unresolved choke is aspiration pneumonia — feed material and saliva that enter the airway during repeated swallowing attempts cause respiratory infection.

Choke prevention is primarily a management and dental care issue. Horses that bolt feed benefit from large, smooth rocks placed in the feed bucket (forcing slower eating), divided meals, or feeding hay before grain to reduce hunger-driven rapid eating. Horses with dental problems that impair chewing require soaked or complete feeds rather than hay cubes or dry pellets.

Gastric ulcers are largely a management disease — the horse's stomach produces acid continuously whether or not food is present. Near-continuous forage access is both the primary prevention strategy and the most effective management tool.

Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome (EGUS) affects a high proportion of performance horses and horses under management stress — studies have documented ulcer prevalence of 60–90% in racehorses and 37–50% in leisure horses. The primary cause is prolonged periods without forage: the horse's stomach produces acid continuously, and without forage to buffer it, the squamous mucosa of the upper stomach is exposed to acid damage. Signs include poor appetite, dull coat, weight loss, girthiness, low-grade colic symptoms, and poor performance — but many horses with significant ulceration show no obvious signs.

Prevention is straightforward: continuous or near-continuous forage access eliminates the periods of unprotected acid exposure that cause mucosal damage. Divided concentrate meals reduce post-feeding acid surges. Minimizing fasting periods during transport or competition reduces ulcer risk in performance horses. Medical treatment (omeprazole) is effective and frequently required in confirmed cases, but prevention through management is both more cost-effective and more sustainable than treatment cycles.

The most effective preventive measure for EGUS is ensuring continuous or near-continuous forage access. A hay net that empties at midnight and is not refilled until morning creates five or more hours of unprotected acid exposure. Small-hole hay nets, slow feeders, or access to hay around the clock reduce this exposure more reliably than any supplement.

In the Barn

The most common feeding errors behind feed-related health emergencies are deceptively simple: too much grain at once, too little forage, and dietary changes made too quickly. Horses that develop colic or laminitis often have a feeding change or overfeeding event in the preceding 24 to 48 hours. The safest general management practice is to treat continuous forage access as non-negotiable, make all feed changes gradually over 10 to 14 days minimum, and weigh feed rather than estimating by volume. Feed changes — from one hay source to another, from hay to pasture, from winter feeding to spring grazing — should always be transitioned slowly. The faster the dietary change, the higher the risk of hindgut disturbance and its downstream consequences.

Things to Remember

  • Colic is abdominal pain with many causes — any colic that does not resolve quickly or produces systemic signs (elevated heart rate, pale mucous membranes) requires veterinary evaluation.
  • Laminitis is a time-critical emergency — intervention within 24 hours prevents the coffin bone rotation that makes chronic laminitis career-ending.
  • Choke is esophageal obstruction, not airway — the horse can still breathe, but requires veterinary care and carries aspiration pneumonia risk if unresolved.
  • Gastric ulcers are caused by acid damage to the stomach lining; the primary prevention is continuous forage access to buffer stomach acid continuously.
