Equine Nutrition

Feed-Related Health Problems Health Problems

Colic, laminitis, choke, gastric ulcers, and other conditions with direct nutritional causes or contributors.

The majority of common equine health emergencies have nutritional or feeding management causes. Colic, laminitis, choke, and gastric ulcers are not random misfortunes — they are predictable consequences of specific feeding errors or management patterns. Understanding the mechanisms that produce each condition is the foundation for preventing them.

This module does not replace veterinary care — all of these conditions require veterinary diagnosis and treatment. It provides the knowledge base that prevents them from occurring and supports informed veterinary communication when they do.

Feed-Related Conditions

Colic

Colic is abdominal pain in the horse — a symptom, not a diagnosis. Feed-related causes include: large grain meals overwhelming the small intestine's starch capacity (hindgut fermentation disturbance), sudden diet changes disrupting hindgut microbial balance, inadequate forage causing decreased gut motility, and dehydration thickening intestinal contents. Signs include pawing, looking at flanks, refusing feed, rolling, sweating, and elevated heart rate. All colic warrants veterinary contact; signs of severe pain or systemic distress require emergency care.

Laminitis

Laminitis is inflammation of the laminar tissue connecting the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. Feed-related causes include carbohydrate overload (grain founder), high-sugar pasture intake, and rapid diet changes that flood the hindgut with fermentable carbohydrate. Signs include digital pulse in the feet, heat in the hooves, a characteristic rocked-back stance, and reluctance to move. Laminitis is an emergency — the coffin bone can rotate or sink within 24 to 48 hours without intervention.

Choke

Choke in horses is esophageal obstruction — material stuck in the esophagus rather than the airway (as in humans). It most commonly occurs with pelleted feed eaten too rapidly, sugar beet pulp fed dry, hay cubes, or coarse hay in horses with dental problems. Signs include feed material and saliva draining from the nostrils, repeated swallowing attempts, and neck stretching. Choke usually resolves with veterinary treatment; unresolved choke carries aspiration pneumonia risk.

Gastric Ulcers (EGUS)

Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome affects a high percentage of performance horses and horses under management stress. The primary cause is prolonged periods without forage — the horse's stomach produces acid continuously, and without forage to buffer it, the squamous mucosa of the upper stomach is exposed to acid damage. Prevention: continuous or near-continuous forage access, divided concentrate meals, and minimizing long periods of fasting during transport or competition.

The most effective preventive measure for feed-related health conditions is ensuring continuous or near-continuous forage access. Eliminating long fasting periods prevents gastric acid pooling (ulcers), maintains gut motility (colic), and reduces the likelihood of gorging on concentrate or pasture following a period of restriction (laminitis). Most feed-related emergencies are more expensive to treat than the forage they would have prevented.

Feed changes — from one hay source to another, from hay to pasture, from winter to spring grazing — should always be made gradually over a minimum of 10 to 14 days. This transition period allows the hindgut's microbial population to adjust to the new substrate. The faster the dietary change, the higher the risk of hindgut disturbance and its downstream consequences: gas colic, diarrhea, and laminitis.

In the Barn

The most common feeding errors are deceptively simple: too much grain, too little forage, and changes made too quickly. Horses that develop colic or laminitis often have a feeding change or overfeeding event in their recent history. The safest practice is to treat forage as the foundation of every diet, make all changes gradually over two to three weeks, and weigh feed rather than estimating by volume. A horse that always has access to hay has a digestive system that is working as designed.

Things to Remember

  • Colic is abdominal pain — it has many causes, from gas to impaction to displacement. Any colic sign (looking at flank, pawing, rolling) requires veterinary contact.
  • Laminitis (founder) is inflammation of the laminar tissue inside the hoof wall. Carbohydrate overload — grain or lush pasture — is a primary trigger.
  • Choke is esophageal obstruction, not tracheal. The horse can still breathe but cannot swallow — feed and saliva drool from the nose and mouth.
  • Gastric ulcers are caused by acid damage to the stomach lining. The primary prevention is continuous forage access to buffer stomach acid.
