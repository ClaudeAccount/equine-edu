Equine Nutrition

Forage: The Foundation

Hay and pasture types, quality assessment, and why forage forms the base of every sound equine feeding program.

Forage — hay and pasture — is not one component of an equine diet. It is the foundation around which every other feeding decision is built. The equine digestive system evolved to process fibrous plant material continuously over many hours. Forage provides this fiber, maintains healthy hindgut microbial populations, keeps the gastrointestinal tract motile, and buffers gastric acid. No concentrate, supplement, or feeding program is safe or effective if forage supply is inadequate.

The primary forage decision is not which type of hay to feed — it is whether the horse is receiving enough forage. The minimum forage recommendation is 1.5% of body weight per day in dry matter. For a 1,100-pound horse, that is approximately 16 pounds of hay per day.

💡 Did You Know

A 1,100-pound horse at maintenance requires approximately 1.5% of body weight in forage dry matter per day — around 16 pounds of hay. If that horse goes longer than four to six hours without forage, the stomach pools acid against an empty lining. Horses that spend long periods without continuous hay access are at significantly elevated risk for gastric ulcers.

Grass hay is the most common forage base for adult horses at maintenance. Understanding what differentiates grass species helps handlers match hay selection to the horse's individual needs and metabolic profile.

Grass hays — timothy, orchardgrass, bromegrass, bermudagrass — are the most common forage base for the majority of horses. They are lower in sugar and protein than legume hays, making them appropriate for horses with normal energy requirements and those at risk for metabolic conditions. Grass hay is also appropriate for horses with high laminitis risk when tested for non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) content — an NSC level below 10% is the target for horses with metabolic syndrome, Cushing's disease, or prior laminitis.

Different grass species vary in their protein, calorie, and sugar content. Timothy is widely used and palatable. Orchardgrass is slightly higher in protein and caloric density than timothy. Bermudagrass is common in warm climates and appropriate for most horses. Coastal bermudagrass has been associated with impaction colic in horses that bolt their feed, likely due to its coarser stem structure. Species selection depends on what is locally available and appropriate for the horse's profile.

Grass hay is the appropriate forage for most adult horses at maintenance or in light work. It should be fed at a minimum of 1.5% of body weight per day in dry matter. Horses with metabolic conditions should receive tested hay with NSC below 10% rather than untested grass hay, which can be significantly higher in sugar depending on cutting and conditions.

Legume hays provide significantly more protein and calories than grass hays — valuable for horses with high energy demands, but too rich for horses at maintenance or at metabolic risk.

Legume hays — primarily alfalfa (lucerne) and clover — are significantly higher in protein, calcium, and caloric density than grass hays. They are appropriate for horses with high energy or protein requirements: hard-working performance horses, broodmares in late gestation and lactation, young growing horses, and horses needing to gain condition. They are often too energy-dense for easy keepers, horses at risk for metabolic disorders, or horses with a history of laminitis.

Alfalfa provides roughly 14–18% crude protein compared to 8–12% in most grass hays, and higher digestible energy. This makes it extremely effective for horses that need to gain weight or support high workloads. The high calcium content in alfalfa is generally not a problem for performance horses or broodmares, but the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio in an all-alfalfa diet can be imbalanced for some horses. Mixing alfalfa with grass hay is a common management approach that captures alfalfa's nutritional density while moderating the caloric load.

Legume hay is appropriate for horses with high energy or protein demands — not as a default forage. Feeding alfalfa to a horse at maintenance or with metabolic risk can cause weight gain, increased starch and sugar intake, and elevated laminitis risk. Match forage type to the horse's actual requirements, not to preference or habit.

Mixed hays combine the nutritional benefits of grass and legume species, but their content varies significantly between cuttings, farms, and seasons. Laboratory testing is the only reliable way to know what a given load provides.

Mixed hays combine grass and legume species — often grass with a percentage of alfalfa — and represent a middle ground in nutritional value. The nutritional analysis of mixed hay depends entirely on the specific ratio of species present, the stage of growth at cutting, and environmental factors that affect sugar and protein content at harvest. A 30% alfalfa / 70% grass mix will have a very different analysis than a 60% alfalfa / 40% grass mix from the same farm.

Mixed hay is a practical choice for horses that need slightly more energy than pure grass hay provides but are not candidates for full alfalfa diets. It is commonly available in many regions and can be an economical middle option. Because its content varies significantly between loads, testing each new batch from a new source is particularly important for horses with metabolic conditions or precise nutritional needs.

Mixed hay's nutritional content cannot be estimated from visual inspection alone. A batch that looks similar to last month's hay may have significantly different NSC, protein, or energy content depending on species ratio and harvesting conditions. Laboratory forage analysis is the only reliable verification — and is essential when feeding horses with metabolic conditions.

Hay quality cannot be reliably determined by appearance alone. Visual and sensory assessment gives useful preliminary information; laboratory forage analysis provides the quantitative data required for accurate ration balancing.

Visual assessment of hay quality provides useful preliminary information: color (bright green indicates preserved nutrients; yellow or brown indicates oxidation and weathering), leaf content (leaves carry the majority of nutritional value — fine-stemmed, leafy hay is higher quality than coarse, stemmy hay), smell (fresh, slightly sweet, grassy = appropriate; musty or dusty = mold, do not feed), and moisture (hay baled too wet heats internally and molds; too dry loses leaves in handling). Maturity at harvest is significant — earlier-cut hay contains higher protein and digestibility.

Laboratory forage analysis is the standard for any horse with specific nutritional needs. A forage test — submitted to a certified laboratory — provides quantitative data on crude protein, digestible energy, non-structural carbohydrates (NSC), acid detergent fiber (ADF), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and key minerals including calcium, phosphorus, and selenium. This information makes accurate ration balancing possible. For horses with metabolic syndrome, Cushing's disease, or a history of laminitis, NSC levels are a management-critical value that cannot be estimated visually.

Feed hay by weight, not by flake. Flake weight varies significantly between bales and species — a flake of alfalfa may weigh twice what a flake of timothy weighs. A hay scale or weight tape is the most important tool for accurate feeding. A horse that appears to be "getting plenty" by flake count may be significantly under-supplied if flake weight has not been measured.

In the Barn

Hay is best purchased by the load from a trusted, consistent source — consistent in species, cutting, and growing region. Ask for a forage analysis on new hay sources before feeding to horses with metabolic risk. When testing is not possible, use visual quality assessment as a preliminary screen and buy small quantities of a new source before committing to a full load. Storing hay off the ground on pallets, protected from moisture and sunlight, preserves nutritional value and prevents mold. Most forage quality losses between cutting and feeding are attributable to moisture exposure and sun bleaching — proper storage is a feeding decision.

Pasture quality varies dramatically by season, grass species, soil fertility, and grazing pressure. Spring pasture in temperate climates can be extremely high in sugar and fructan — substances that trigger laminitis in susceptible horses. Horses at risk for metabolic syndrome, Cushing's disease (PPID), or laminitis should have pasture access managed based on forage analysis and veterinary guidance, not visual assessment alone.

Things to Remember

  • Forage — hay and pasture — forms the base of every horse's diet. No concentrate should compensate for a forage deficit.
  • Grass hays (timothy, orchard, bermuda) are lower in protein and calories — appropriate for most adult horses. Legume hays (alfalfa, clover) are higher in both — appropriate for high-demand horses.
  • Hay quality is assessed by smell, color, leafiness, and absence of dust or mold — but nutritional content requires laboratory analysis for precision.
  • Feed hay by weight, not by flake. Flake weight varies significantly; accurate feeding requires knowing how many pounds the horse is receiving.
