Equine Nutrition

Forage: The Foundation of Equine Nutrition Nutrition

Hay and pasture types, quality assessment, and why forage forms the base of every sound equine feeding program.

Forage — hay and pasture — is not one component of an equine diet. It is the foundation around which every other feeding decision is built. The equine digestive system evolved to process fibrous plant material continuously over many hours. Forage provides this fiber, maintains healthy hindgut microbial populations, keeps the gastrointestinal tract motile, and buffers gastric acid. No concentrate, supplement, or feeding program is safe or effective if forage supply is inadequate.

The primary forage decision is not which type of hay to feed — it is whether the horse is receiving enough forage. The minimum forage recommendation is 1.5% of body weight per day in dry matter. For a 1,100-pound horse, that is approximately 16 pounds of hay per day.

Forage Types & Quality

Grass Hay

Grass hays — timothy, orchardgrass, bromegrass, bermudagrass — are the most common forage base for the majority of horses. They are lower in sugar and protein than legume hays, making them appropriate for horses with normal energy requirements and those at risk for metabolic conditions. Grass hay is also appropriate for horses with high laminitis risk when tested for non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) content.

Legume Hay

Legume hays — primarily alfalfa (lucerne) and clover — are significantly higher in protein, calcium, and caloric density than grass hays. They are appropriate for horses with high energy or protein requirements: hard-working performance horses, broodmares in late gestation and lactation, young growing horses, and horses needing to gain condition. They are often too energy-dense for easy keepers or horses at risk for metabolic disorders.

Mixed Hay

Mixed hays combine grass and legume species — often grass with a percentage of alfalfa — and represent a middle ground in nutritional value. The nutritional analysis of mixed hay depends on the specific ratio of species present and varies significantly between cuttings and farms. Testing is the only reliable way to know what a specific load of hay contains.

Hay Quality Assessment

Visual assessment of hay quality includes color (bright green = preserved nutrients; yellow/brown = oxidized, weathered), leaf content (leaves carry the majority of nutritional value; stems carry fiber with less nutrition), smell (sweet and grassy = fresh; musty = mold, do not feed), and moisture (hay baled too wet heats and molds; too dry loses leaves in handling). Laboratory forage analysis provides reliable quantitative data on protein, NSC, and mineral content.

Hay is best purchased by the load, not by the flake. Flake weight varies significantly between bales and species. Feed hay by weight — a hay scale or weight tape and a conversion factor ensures the horse receives the correct amount. A horse that 'looks like it's getting plenty' may be significantly under-supplied if flake weight has not been measured.

Pasture quality varies dramatically by season, grass species, soil fertility, and grazing pressure. Spring pasture in temperate climates can be extremely high in sugar and fructan — substances that trigger laminitis in susceptible horses. Horses at risk for metabolic syndrome, Cushing's disease (PPID), or laminitis should have pasture access managed based on forage analysis and veterinary guidance, not visual assessment alone.

Things to Remember

  • Forage — hay and pasture — should form the base of every horse's diet. No concentrate should compensate for a forage deficit.
  • Grass hays (timothy, orchard, bermuda) are lower in protein and calories than legume hays — appropriate for most adult horses.
  • Legume hays (alfalfa, clover) are higher in protein, calcium, and calories — valuable for growing horses, lactating mares, and hard keepers.
  • Hay quality is assessed by smell (fresh, slightly sweet), color (green), texture (fine-stemmed and leafy), and absence of dust, mold, or weeds.
