Equine Nutrition

Feed-Related Health Problems

Colic, laminitis, choke, gastric ulcers, and other conditions with direct nutritional causes or contributors.

Most common horse emergencies are tied to feeding. Colic, laminitis, choke, and stomach ulcers are not bad luck. They often come from feeding mistakes. When you know what causes each one, you can help prevent it.

This lesson is not a substitute for a vet. All of these problems need a vet to check and treat them. This lesson helps you prevent them, and helps you talk clearly with your vet if one happens.

💡 Did You Know

Colic is the top cause of early death in horses. It is behind about 28 to 33% of horse deaths. Even so, most colic cases are mild and get better with a vet's help. Most are also preventable. Keeping hay in front of the horse, changing feed slowly, and skipping big grain meals lowers the risk of the most common kinds.

Colic means belly pain. It has many causes. Feeding problems cause a lot of cases, and most of those can be prevented with steady care.

Colic is belly pain. It is a sign of a problem, not the problem itself. Feeding causes include: big grain meals that flood the gut with starch, sudden diet changes that upset the gut microbes, too little hay that lets the gut slow down, and not enough water that makes the gut contents thick and slow. Signs include pawing, looking at the side, turning away from feed, rolling, sweating, and a fast heartbeat.

Call the vet for any colic. Some signs mean call right now: rolling that will not stop, not being able to stand, sweating for no clear reason, a heartbeat over 60 beats a minute, or pale or white gums. You can walk a horse with mild colic while you wait for the vet. But walking does not replace the vet, and it should not slow down the call.

You cannot tell mild colic from the kind that needs surgery just by looking. Gas colic may pass with walking. A twisted gut needs surgery within hours. Any colic that does not pass soon, that keeps coming back, or that brings serious signs needs a vet, not more time at home.

Laminitis is an emergency. Too much sugar and starch, from extra grain or rich grass, is the main feeding cause. Catching it early can stop the lasting damage that ends many horses' working lives.

Inside the hoof, soft tissue called the laminae holds the coffin bone (the bone inside the hoof) to the hoof wall. Laminitis is when this tissue swells and gets sore and hurt. When it weakens, the coffin bone can drop or tip inside the hoof. This causes lasting, severe damage. Feeding causes include too much grain at once, rich high-sugar grass in horses that are prone to it, and fast diet changes that flood the gut with sugar before the microbes can adjust.

Early signs include: a strong pulse you can feel at the back of the ankle, heat in the hooves, a "rocked back" stance with the weight off the front toes, and not wanting to move or walk on hard ground. Horses that gain weight easily, have Cushing's disease, or have had laminitis before are at much higher risk. They need careful feeding to keep it from coming back. The coffin bone can drop or tip within 24 to 48 hours, so spotting it early and calling the vet right away is key.

Laminitis is a race against time. Help in the first 24 hours can be the difference between getting better and lasting harm. A horse with early signs (heat, a strong hoof pulse, a rocked-back stance) should come off grass right away and go into a stall with deep bedding while you call the vet. Do not wait overnight to see if it gets better.

Choke in a horse is food stuck in the food pipe, not the airway. The horse can still breathe. It needs a vet, and you can usually prevent it with good feeding habits and regular teeth care.

In a horse, choke means food is stuck in the food pipe, not the windpipe like in people. The food pipe is separate from the airway, so the horse can still breathe, but it cannot swallow. Signs include food and spit dripping from both nostrils, swallowing over and over, stretching the neck out, coughing, and looking upset. The horse usually stops eating and may seem sore in the throat and neck.

Choke most often happens when a horse eats pellets too fast, eats dry beet pulp that was not soaked, eats hay cubes, or has bad teeth that keep it from chewing well. Many cases clear on their own as spit softens the blockage. When they do not, the vet must clear it. The main danger from choke that does not clear is a lung infection, caused by food and spit getting into the airway during all the swallowing.

You prevent choke mostly through feeding habits and teeth care. For horses that gulp their food, put large smooth rocks in the bucket so they eat slower, split meals into smaller ones, or feed hay before grain so they are less hungry. Horses with bad teeth need soaked feed or complete feed instead of hay cubes or dry pellets.

Stomach ulcers come mostly from how a horse is managed. A horse's stomach makes acid all the time, even when it is empty. Keeping hay in front of the horse nearly all the time is the best way to prevent and manage them.

Stomach ulcers are sores in the stomach lining. The med term is EGUS (Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome). They are very common in hard-working and stressed horses. Studies found ulcers in 60 to 90% of racehorses and 37 to 50% of pleasure horses. The main cause is going too long with no hay. The stomach makes acid all the time. With no hay to soak it up, the acid burns the upper stomach lining. Signs include a poor appetite, a dull coat, weight loss, being touchy at the girth, mild colic signs, and doing poorly under saddle. But many horses with bad ulcers show no clear signs at all.

Prevention is simple: keep hay in front of the horse nearly all the time. This removes the empty-stomach periods that let acid burn the lining. Splitting grain into smaller meals lowers the acid spike after eating. Cutting down on empty-stomach time during travel or shows lowers the risk too. There is a medicine (omeprazole) that works well and is often needed once ulcers are found. But preventing them with good care costs less and lasts longer than treating them again and again.

The best way to prevent ulcers is to keep hay in front of the horse nearly all the time. A hay net that runs out at midnight and is not refilled until morning leaves five or more hours of empty stomach and acid. Small-hole hay nets, slow feeders, or hay around the clock work better than any supplement.

In the Barn

The feeding mistakes behind these emergencies are simple: too much grain at once, too little hay, and feed changes made too fast. Horses that get colic or laminitis often had a feed change or a big meal in the day or two before. The safest plan: keep hay in front of the horse, make every feed change slowly over at least 10 to 14 days, and weigh feed instead of guessing. Every change, from one hay to another, from hay to grass, or from winter feeding to spring grass, should go slowly. The faster the change, the higher the risk of gut trouble and what comes after it.

Things to Remember

  • Colic is belly pain with many causes. Any colic that does not pass quickly, or that brings serious signs like a fast heartbeat or pale gums, needs a vet.
  • Laminitis is a race against time. Help within 24 hours can stop the coffin bone from tipping, which is what makes laminitis so serious.
  • Choke is food stuck in the food pipe, not the airway. The horse can still breathe, but it needs a vet, and it can lead to a lung infection if it does not clear.
  • Stomach ulcers come from acid burning the stomach lining. The best way to prevent them is to keep hay in front of the horse so the acid is soaked up.
