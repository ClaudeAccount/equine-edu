Equine Nutrition

Concentrates & Supplements

When concentrates are needed, how to evaluate types, and how to assess supplement claims against evidence.

Concentrates are grains and bagged feeds. They help support the diet, but they are not the base of it. Before you pick a concentrate, ask one thing first: does the horse even need it?

Many horses stay at a healthy weight on hay and grass alone. For them, adding grain just adds calories, gut risk, and cost for no reason. A horse needs concentrate only when there is a clear reason, such as:

  • Hard work that needs extra energy.
  • Trouble keeping weight on hay alone.
  • A missing nutrient that a test has shown.

💡 Did You Know

Oats have been fed to horses for hundreds of years because they are safer than many grains. Their outer hull slows digestion and lowers the risk of too much starch at once.

Corn has nearly twice as much starch as oats, pound for pound. Even with oats, watch the total starch in each meal to protect the gut and lower colic risk.

Concentrates & Supplements
Type 1

Whole and Processed Grains

Common horse grains include:

  • Oats — the classic horse grain. Medium starch, with a hull that slows digestion.
  • Corn — very high in starch and energy. Easy to overfeed, which can upset the gut.
  • Barley — sits between oats and corn for energy and starch.

Some grains are crimped, flaked, or cooked. This makes them easier to digest.

No matter the grain, watch the starch. A good rule is no more than 0.5 kg of starch per 100 kg of body weight in one meal. This keeps the gut safe.

Type 2

Commercial Balancers and Complete Feeds

Ration balancers pack a lot of protein, vitamins, and minerals into a small, low-calorie serving. They are often used for:

  • Easy keepers (horses that gain weight fast).
  • Horses fed only hay and grass.
  • Horses with little grass time.

They fill in missing nutrients without adding much weight.

Complete feeds mix forage and grain into one product. They are used when:

  • Bad teeth make chewing hard.
  • Older horses need softer feed.
  • Travel or other reasons make hay hard to get.
Type 3

Evaluating Supplement Claims

The supplement business has few rules, so check the claims with care. Ask:

  • What exact problem is this supposed to fix?
  • Is there real research showing it works in horses?
  • Has an outside group checked its quality (like NASC or USP)?
  • Does the horse already get this nutrient in its food?

Most horses on good hay and a balanced feed do not need extra supplements.

Type 4

Joint and Coat Supplements

Joint supplements often have ingredients like glucosamine and chondroitin. The proof that they work in horses is mixed. Some horses seem to do better, but the results are not steady.

Omega-3 oils (from flaxseed or fish oil) have stronger proof for:

  • Calming swelling and soreness.
  • A healthier coat.

Before adding a supplement, look at the whole diet first. A hay test and expert advice help.

Feeding Balance and Common Errors

One of the most common feeding mistakes is too much grain and not enough hay. This is linked to:

  • Stomach ulcers.
  • Colic.
  • Weight and sugar problems.
  • Jumpy or hard-to-handle behavior.

Fixing the hay amount solves more problems than any supplement.

Horses fed a bagged feed at the right amount usually already get the vitamins and minerals they need. Adding more can throw things off, especially with:

  • Vitamins A, D, E, and K.
  • Minerals like selenium, which is unsafe in large amounts.

In the Barn

Before you add any grain or supplement, first decide if the horse really needs it.

Many horses in light work stay in good shape on hay alone. Let the horse's body condition guide your choices, not habit.

When a horse does need grain, add it slowly over 2 to 4 weeks so the gut microbes can adjust.

Split the daily grain into a few small meals instead of one big one. This is easier on the gut.

Things to Remember

  • Use grain only when hay alone is not enough.
  • Ration balancers add nutrients without much weight.
  • Most horses on good hay and balanced feed do not need supplements.
  • Feed grain in careful amounts to protect the gut.
  • Hay and grass are the base of the diet, not grain or supplements.
