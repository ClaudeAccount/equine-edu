Equine Nutrition

Concentrates & Supplements

When concentrates are needed, how to evaluate types, and how to assess supplement claims against evidence.

Concentrates — grains, complete feeds, and processed feeds — play a supporting role in equine nutrition, not a foundational one. The first question about any concentrate is not "which one?" but "is one needed?" Horses that maintain appropriate body condition on forage alone do not require concentrate supplementation. Adding concentrate to a horse that does not need it increases caloric intake, gastrointestinal upset risk, and feed costs without benefit.

When concentrates are appropriate — for performance horses with high energy demands, horses that cannot maintain weight on forage alone, or horses with specific nutritional deficiencies — the choice of concentrate type and amount requires systematic evaluation.

💡 Did You Know

Oats have been used as an equine grain for centuries partly because they are safer than corn: the fibrous hull slows starch digestion, reducing hindgut carbohydrate overload risk. Corn contains nearly twice the starch density of oats by weight. Even with oats, total starch per meal should not exceed 0.5 kg per 100 kg of body weight to protect hindgut health.

Concentrates & Supplements
Type 1

Whole and Processed Grains

Oats are the most traditional equine grain — lower in starch than corn, with a fibrous hull that slows digestion somewhat. Corn is very high in starch and energy density and carries significant laminitis and hindgut upset risk if fed in excess. Barley falls between the two. Processed grains (crimped, steam-flaked, or extruded) have improved digestibility. Regardless of grain type, total starch intake per meal should not exceed 0.5 kg per 100 kg of body weight.

Type 2

Commercial Balancers and Complete Feeds

Ration balancers are low-calorie, high-protein, vitamin, and mineral supplements designed to correct the nutritional gaps in an all-forage diet without adding significant calories. They are appropriate for easy keepers, horses on hay-only diets, and horses with limited access to fresh pasture. Complete feeds combine forage and concentrate in a single product and are used when dental problems, age, or travel make hay feeding impractical.

Type 3

Evaluating Supplement Claims

The supplement industry for horses is largely unregulated. Evaluating a supplement requires asking: (1) What specific deficiency does this product address? (2) Is there published peer-reviewed research supporting its efficacy in horses? (3) Does the product have a third-party quality certification (NASC seal, USP verification)? (4) Is the nutrient already present at sufficient levels in the current forage and feed program? Most horses on good forage and a commercial feed do not have nutritional gaps that supplements address.

Type 4

Joint and Coat Supplements

Joint supplements containing glucosamine, chondroitin, and hyaluronic acid are among the most commonly purchased equine supplements. Evidence for their efficacy in horses is mixed — some horses show clinical response; many do not. Omega-3 fatty acids (from flaxseed or fish oil) have stronger evidence for anti-inflammatory effects and coat quality improvement. Any supplement decision should begin with a forage analysis and veterinary or nutritionist consultation.

The most common nutritional error in domestic horse management is overfeeding concentrate and underfeeding forage. Behavior problems, gastric ulcers, colic, and metabolic disorders are all associated with high-concentrate, low-forage diets. Correcting the forage base resolves more nutritional management problems than any supplement.

Horses on commercial complete feeds or fortified concentrates at the manufacturer's recommended feeding rate are typically receiving adequate vitamins and minerals. Adding supplements to horses already receiving fortified feeds can create nutrient imbalances — particularly with fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and minerals like selenium, which has a narrow therapeutic window and can cause toxicity if oversupplemented.

In the Barn

Before adding any concentrate or supplement, the first question is whether one is actually needed. Most horses in light work on good-quality forage maintain condition without concentrate at all. Evaluate body condition score before reaching for the feed bucket. When concentrate is added, introduce it gradually over two to four weeks — the hindgut's microbial population needs time to adjust to increased starch and sugar. Divide daily concentrate into a minimum of two meals to avoid overloading the small intestine's starch-processing capacity in a single feeding.

Things to Remember

  • Concentrates (grain, commercial feeds) are used to close the energy or protein gap when forage alone does not meet a horse's needs — they are not a default.
  • A ration balancer provides vitamins, minerals, and protein without significant calories — the correct choice when forage quality is adequate but nutritionally incomplete.
  • Supplement claims must be evaluated against evidence. Most horses on quality forage with balanced concentrate do not need additional supplements.
  • Whole grains fed in large single meals can cause hindgut acidosis — small, frequent meals and proper balancing are critical when concentrates are fed.
