Equine Nutrition

Why Feeding Matters

Feeding a horse is more than providing food. It is about giving the right feed, in the right amount, at the right time, every day.

Feeding Is Daily Care

Horses depend on people for their food. What you feed a horse affects its digestion, growth, energy, mood, and health over time.

Good feeding starts with knowing how a horse's body is built to eat. Once you know that, most feeding rules make sense.

Later lessons cover the gut, types of hay, and how to plan meals.

💡 Did You Know

A horse's stomach is much smaller than most people expect. It holds only about 2 to 4 gallons at a time, and food moves through it quickly. This is one reason horses are built to eat small amounts throughout the day instead of large meals.

Built to Graze All Day

Horses are designed to eat small amounts of forage such as grass and hay for most of the day.

In the wild, horses spend about 16 to 18 hours a day eating grass. They move slowly and take in a little at a time.

Horses kept in stalls or pens still have this same kind of gut.

Long gaps with no hay can upset a horse's gut. Many cases of colic (belly pain) and stomach ulcers (sores in the stomach) come from feeding that does not match how a horse is built to eat.

Four Rules of Safe Feeding

Safe feeding follows four simple rules. Each one matches how a horse's body works.

1

Feed on a Steady Schedule

Horses do best when meals come at the same times each day. A steady routine keeps the gut healthy and helps the horse stay calm.

2

Change Feed Slowly

The gut needs time to get used to new feed. A sudden change in hay, grain, or grass can upset the helpful bacteria in the gut. Make most feed changes slowly, over 7 to 10 days.

3

Keep Forage Available

Hay and grass should make up most of what a horse eats. When a horse goes too long with no hay, stomach acid builds up. This can lead to ulcers and an upset gut.

4

Feed Small Amounts Often

Big grain meals are hard for a horse to digest. If a horse needs grain, split it into smaller meals during the day.

Many feeding problems start with a sudden change, like new hay, new grain, or a missed meal. Slow changes help protect the gut.

Water Comes First

Water is the most important nutrient in a horse's diet. No feeding plan works without it.

A horse usually drinks 5 to 15 gallons of water a day. On hot days or after hard work, it can drink more than 20 gallons.

Water helps food move through the gut. When a horse drinks less, the risk of colic and other problems goes up.

Drinking less than usual is often one of the first signs that something is wrong.

In the Barn

A change in how a horse eats can be an early sign of illness.

A horse with a sore gut may turn away from hay or grain, paw the ground, look at its side, act restless, or stand very still and quiet.

Watching how a horse eats helps you catch problems early, when they are easiest to fix.

Things to Remember

  • Good feeding means the right amount, at the right time, every day.
  • Horses are built to eat hay and grass for most of the day.
  • Make feed changes slowly, over 7 to 10 days.
  • Try to keep hay in front of a horse as much as you can.
  • Feed grain in small meals, not one big one.
  • Water matters most. Keep clean water available at all times.
  • Eating or drinking less can be an early sign of illness.
