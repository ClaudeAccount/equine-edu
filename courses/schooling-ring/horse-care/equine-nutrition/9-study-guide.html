Key Concepts

What are the four rules of safe feeding?

Feed on a steady schedule, change feed slowly over 7 to 10 days, keep forage available so the stomach is rarely empty, and feed grain in small amounts more often. These rules match the way a horse's body is built to eat.

Why is water called the most important nutrient, and how much does a horse need?

Water keeps food moving through the gut and supports the whole body. A horse usually drinks about 5 to 15 gallons a day, and more in heat or hard work. Drinking less than usual can be an early sign of illness, so clean, fresh water should always be available.

Why is the horse called a hindgut fermenter, and what does that mean for feeding?

Most of a horse's fiber is broken down in the back of the gut, called the hindgut. Tiny microbes live there and turn fiber into energy. This means hay is the horse's main fuel. When those microbes get upset, from a sudden feed change, certain medicines, or too much starch, it can cause colic and other gut problems.

Why is the horse's stomach size significant for feeding management?

A horse's stomach is small for such a big animal. It holds about 8 to 15 liters (2 to 4 gallons) and empties fast. It is built for eating small amounts almost all day, not big meals far apart. A big grain meal is too much for it. The extra starch slips into the hindgut and makes acid and gas. A horse needs at least 1.5% of its body weight in hay each day.

What is the difference between grass hay and legume hay in terms of appropriate use?

Grass hays (timothy, orchardgrass, bermudagrass) have less protein, fewer calories, and less sugar. They suit most grown horses at rest or in light work, and horses that gain weight easily (when the hay is tested for sugar and starch). Legume hays (alfalfa, clover) have much more protein, calcium, and energy. They suit horses that work hard, mares that are pregnant or nursing, growing horses, and horses that need to gain weight. Legume hay is usually too rich for easy keepers.

When are concentrates (grains, commercial feeds) needed in an equine diet?

Grain is needed when hay alone cannot give enough energy or protein. That is often horses in medium to hard work, growing horses, mares that are pregnant or nursing, or horses that cannot keep weight on hay alone. Horses at rest or in light work on good hay often do not need grain. The first question is not which grain to buy, but whether the horse needs any at all.

What is the Henneke Body Condition Score system?

The Henneke Body Condition Score is a 1 to 9 scale that checks how much fat a horse carries. It was made at Texas A&M University in 1983. It looks at six spots: the neck, withers, shoulder, ribs, loin, and tailhead. A score of 5 to 6 is right for most grown horses. A 4 or lower means feed more. A 7 or higher means feed less.

What is laminitis and what are the primary feed-related triggers?

Laminitis is when the soft tissue inside the hoof (the laminae) swells and gets hurt. This tissue holds the coffin bone to the hoof wall. When it weakens, the coffin bone can tip or drop, causing lasting damage. Feeding triggers include too much grain, rich high-sugar grass in horses prone to it, and fast feed changes that flood the gut with sugar. Horses that gain weight easily, have Cushing's disease, or have had laminitis before are at higher risk.

What causes equine gastric ulcer syndrome (EGUS), and what is the primary prevention?

EGUS means stomach ulcers, which are sores in the stomach lining. The stomach makes acid all the time. With no hay to soak it up, the acid burns the upper lining. The main cause is going too long with no hay — more than four to six hours raises the risk. To prevent it, keep hay available nearly all the time, split grain into small meals, and limit empty-stomach time during travel or shows. There is a medicine (omeprazole) that works, but good care prevents ulcers better over the long run.

What is the four-step framework for designing a feeding program?

Step 1: Look at the horse — weight, body condition (1 to 9), teeth, workload, and stage of life. Step 2: Check the hay — send a sample to a lab for protein, energy, sugar and starch, and minerals. Step 3: Fill the gaps — fix the hay first, then add a ration balancer for missing vitamins and minerals, then add grain only if the horse needs more energy. Step 4: Keep checking — weigh the horse each month, score its condition every 2 to 4 weeks, and change the feed as the horse changes.

What is choke in horses, and how does it differ from human choking?

Choke in a horse is food stuck in the food pipe. Unlike choking in people, which blocks the airway, a horse with choke can still breathe. Signs include food and spit dripping from the nostrils, swallowing over and over, and stretching the neck out. If it does not clear on its own, the horse needs a vet. The main danger is a lung infection. It is most common with dry pellets, dry beet pulp, hay cubes, or horses with bad teeth that cannot chew well.

What does 'feeding by weight, not by flake' mean, and why does it matter?

A flake is one slab of hay from a bale. Flakes are not all the same weight. They change with the kind of hay, the farm, and the bale. A flake of alfalfa can weigh more than twice as much as a flake of timothy. If you count flakes without weighing them, you do not really know how much the horse is getting. A hay scale tells you the true amount. Most horses get too much or too little when flakes are just counted.

Key Vocabulary

Hindgut Fermentation

How tiny microbes in the back of the gut break down fiber from hay and turn it into energy, the horse's main fuel. These microbes need to stay healthy. Sudden feed changes, some medicines, and too much starch can upset them.

Non-Structural Carbohydrates (NSC)

The sugars and starches in hay and feed. They digest fast. NSC above 10 to 12% in hay is risky for horses that gain weight easily, have Cushing's disease, or have had laminitis. You can only know the NSC from a lab test, not by looking.

Body Condition Score (BCS)

A 1 to 9 score (the Henneke scale) for how much fat a horse carries. You check six spots: neck, withers, shoulder, ribs, loin, and tailhead. A 5 to 6 is right for most grown horses. It helps you see if the feeding plan is working.

Ration Balancer

A small, low-calorie feed that adds the vitamins, minerals, and protein that hay alone may miss, without adding much weight. Good for horses at rest or in light work that need those nutrients but not extra calories.

Forage Analysis

A lab test of hay or grass. It measures protein, energy, fiber, sugar and starch, and key minerals. It is the only sure way to know what a hay really has, so you can balance the diet.

Laminitis

Swelling and damage to the soft tissue inside the hoof that holds the coffin bone to the hoof wall. When bad, the coffin bone can tip or drop. This is an emergency that needs a vet right away. Feeding triggers include too much grain or rich high-sugar grass.

EGUS (Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome)

Sores in the stomach lining. They form when acid burns the lining during long gaps with no hay. Very common in hard-working horses. Best prevented by keeping hay available; the main medicine is omeprazole. Many horses with ulcers show no clear signs.

Hindgut Acidosis

When too much starch or sugar reaches the back of the gut, the microbes there make too much acid. This kills helpful microbes and throws the gut off balance. It is linked to colic, loose manure, and laminitis. Prevent it by keeping starch low per meal and feeding enough hay.

Common Mistakes

Using grain as the dietary foundation rather than forage

The most common feeding mistake. Grain should add to hay when a horse needs more energy, not replace it. Horses fed mostly grain with little hay get stomach ulcers, upset gut microbes (colic and gut acid problems), and weight and sugar trouble. Fixing the hay base solves more problems than any grain change.

Making dietary changes too quickly

The gut microbes need at least 10 to 14 days to adjust to new feed. Switching hay, moving from hay to grass, or adding grain too fast upsets them. This raises the risk of gas colic, gut acid problems, and laminitis. Make every change slowly, even if the new feed looks a lot like the old one.

Assuming fresh green grass is always safe for all horses

Rich spring grass, or fast-growing grass after rain, can be very high in sugar and starch. For horses that gain weight easily, have Cushing's disease, or have had laminitis, all-day spring grass is a top cause of laminitis. These horses need their grass time managed — fewer hours, a grazing muzzle, or tested grass — no matter how healthy the grass looks.

Adding supplements to horses already on fortified feed

Bagged feeds are made to give the vitamins and minerals a horse needs when fed at the right amount. Adding more supplements on top can throw things off, especially with vitamins A, D, E, and K, and with selenium, which is unsafe in large amounts. Before adding a supplement, make sure the diet is actually missing something.

Delaying veterinary contact for colic or laminitis

Both colic and laminitis can go from manageable to serious in hours. A twisted gut that surgery could fix at hour two may not be fixable at hour eight. Laminitis caught and treated in the first 24 hours does far better than one left for 48 to 72 hours. Calling the vet early costs much less than waiting too long.

Horse Bowl Focus

What percentage of body weight should a horse receive in forage dry matter daily?

At least 1.5% of body weight a day. For an 1,100-pound horse, that is about 16 pounds of hay. Many horses do well with 2% or more. This is a common quiz number, so know the percent and how to do the math for a given weight.

What is the Henneke Body Condition Score scale and what are the endpoints?

The Henneke scale runs from 1 to 9. A 1 means very thin, with bones easy to see and no fat. A 9 means very fat, with thick fat pads and a hard, cresty neck. A 5 is ideal for most grown horses, and 5 to 6 is the target range. Horse Bowl often asks these numbers.

What is the maximum safe starch intake per meal for a horse?

No more than 0.5 kg of starch per 100 kg of body weight in one meal. For a 500 kg horse, that is no more than 2.5 kg of starch per meal. More than that sends extra starch to the back of the gut and upsets it. This is a common Horse Bowl number.

What are the signs of colic? (commonly tested list)

Pawing the ground, looking at or biting the side, turning away from feed, lying down and getting up over and over, rolling, sweating with no exercise, a fast heartbeat (over 44 beats a minute at rest), quiet or missing gut sounds, and pale or off-color gums. Horse Bowl may ask for the list or which signs mean it is serious.

What are the early signs of laminitis? (commonly tested list)

A strong pulse at the back of the ankle, heat in the hooves, a stance with weight shifted back off the toes, not wanting to move or walk on hard ground, and short steps. In long-term laminitis: rings on the hoof wall and a flat or bulging sole. Horse Bowl often asks you to spot the early signs.

What is EGUS and what percentage of performance horses does it affect?

Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome, or stomach ulcers. Studies found it in 60 to 90% of racehorses and endurance horses, and even more in horses under hard training and show stress. Because it is so common, Horse Bowl asks about it often. Know what it is, the cause (acid burning the lining when there is no hay), and how to prevent it (keep hay available).

What is a ration balancer and when is it used?

A ration balancer is a small, low-calorie feed that adds the vitamins, minerals, and protein hay alone may miss, without adding much weight. It is for horses at rest or in light work that need those nutrients but not extra calories. Horse Bowl may give a case (a horse on grass hay that holds its weight but shows signs of a missing nutrient) and ask which feed to use.

What is choke in horses, and how does it differ from choking in humans?

In horses, choke is food stuck in the food pipe. Unlike choking in people, which blocks the airway, a horse with choke can still breathe. Signs include food and spit dripping from the nostrils, swallowing over and over, and stretching the neck out. The main danger is a lung infection from food getting into the airway. Horse Bowl may ask for this meaning, the key sign (food from the nostrils), or the main danger.
