Key Concepts

Why is the horse called a hindgut fermenter?

The majority of microbial fermentation and fiber digestion occurs in the hindgut — the cecum and large colon — not in the stomach or small intestine as in ruminants. This makes forage the foundation of the equine diet.

What is the horse's stomach capacity and why does it matter?

The horse's stomach is small relative to body size — approximately 8–15 liters. It empties quickly, which is why horses need access to forage throughout the day rather than two large meals.

What is the role of forage in the equine diet?

Forage — hay and pasture — should comprise at least 50–100% of the diet by dry weight. It provides fiber that drives hindgut fermentation, keeps the digestive tract moving, and supports the microbiome that produces energy and nutrients.

What is hay quality assessed by?

Visual quality (color, smell, leafiness, minimal weeds), maturity at harvest (earlier cut = higher protein, more digestible), and forage analysis — a lab test that measures actual nutrient content including protein, digestible energy, and non-structural carbohydrates.

When are concentrates (grain) needed?

When forage alone cannot meet the energy demands of the horse's workload, growth stage, or body condition — typically for horses in moderate to heavy work, growing horses, or mares in late gestation or lactation. They are not required for most horses at rest or in light work.

What is body condition score (BCS) and what scale is used?

BCS is a numerical assessment of a horse's fat coverage at key body regions (neck, withers, ribs, loin, tailhead, shoulder). The Henneke scale runs from 1 (emaciated) to 9 (obese). A target of 4–6 is considered healthy for most horses.

What is laminitis and how is it connected to nutrition?

Laminitis is inflammation of the laminae inside the hoof, causing severe pain and potential rotation of the coffin bone. Diets high in non-structural carbohydrates (sugars and starch) from grain or rich pasture can trigger the condition in susceptible horses.

What is colic and what feeding practices are linked to it?

Colic is abdominal pain that can range from mild gas pain to a life-threatening impaction or displacement. Sudden feed changes, too little water, large grain meals, and insufficient forage all increase colic risk.

Common Points of Confusion

Should horses be fed primarily grain with hay as a supplement?

No. Hay or pasture is the foundation of the equine diet, not grain. Grain is a supplement added when energy needs exceed what forage can provide — feeding large amounts of grain without adequate forage dramatically increases colic and laminitis risk.

Is fresh green grass always safe for horses?

Not always. Lush spring pasture or rapidly growing grass after rain can be extremely high in non-structural carbohydrates, triggering laminitis in horses with metabolic conditions such as Equine Metabolic Syndrome or PPID (Cushing's disease). Grazing management is critical for these horses.
