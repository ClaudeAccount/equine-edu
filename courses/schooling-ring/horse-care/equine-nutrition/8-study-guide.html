Key Concepts

Why is the horse called a hindgut fermenter, and what does that mean for feeding?

The majority of microbial fermentation and fiber digestion in horses occurs in the hindgut — the cecum and large colon — rather than the stomach or small intestine (as in ruminants). The hindgut microbiome produces volatile fatty acids that supply much of the horse's energy. This architecture means forage fiber is the foundational fuel — and disruptions to the hindgut microbiome (from sudden diet changes, antibiotic use, or excess starch) produce digestive problems including colic and hindgut acidosis.

Why is the horse's stomach size significant for feeding management?

The horse's stomach holds approximately 8–15 liters — relatively small for the animal's size. It empties continuously rather than holding feed for extended periods. This design evolved for near-constant grazing of small amounts, not for large, infrequent meals. Overfeeding concentrate at a single meal overwhelms the stomach's capacity and pushes undigested starch into the hindgut, where fermentation produces acid and gas. Minimum forage requirement is 1.5% of body weight per day.

What is the difference between grass hay and legume hay in terms of appropriate use?

Grass hays (timothy, orchardgrass, bermudagrass) are lower in protein, calories, and sugar — appropriate for most adult horses at maintenance or in light work, and for horses at metabolic risk (when tested for NSC). Legume hays (alfalfa, clover) are significantly higher in protein, calcium, and caloric density — appropriate for horses with high energy demands, broodmares in late gestation and lactation, growing horses, and horses needing to gain condition. Legume hay is typically too energy-dense for easy keepers or horses with metabolic disorders.

When are concentrates (grains, commercial feeds) needed in an equine diet?

Concentrates are needed when forage alone cannot meet the horse's energy or protein requirements — typically for horses in moderate to heavy work, growing horses, mares in late gestation or lactation, or horses that cannot maintain appropriate body condition on forage alone. Horses at maintenance or in light work on good-quality forage often do not require concentrate. The first question about any concentrate is not which product to use but whether one is needed at all.

What is the Henneke Body Condition Score system?

The Henneke Body Condition Score (BCS) is a standardized 1–9 scale for assessing a horse's body fat reserves. Developed at Texas A&M University in 1983, it evaluates fat deposits at six locations: the neck, withers, shoulder, ribs, loin, and tailhead. A score of 5 (moderate) to 6 (moderately fleshy) is appropriate for most adult horses. A score of 4 or below warrants dietary increase; a score of 7 or above warrants dietary reduction.

What is laminitis and what are the primary feed-related triggers?

Laminitis is inflammation of the sensitive laminae — the tissue connecting the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. When the laminae become compromised, the coffin bone loses its support within the hoof capsule and can rotate or sink, causing permanent structural damage. Feed-related triggers include: carbohydrate overload from excess grain (grain founder), high-sugar pasture in susceptible horses, and rapid diet changes that flood the hindgut with fermentable carbohydrate. Horses with EMS, PPID, or prior laminitis are at elevated risk.

What causes equine gastric ulcer syndrome (EGUS), and what is the primary prevention?

EGUS is caused by acid damage to the squamous mucosa of the upper stomach when it is exposed to the stomach's continuously produced acid without forage to buffer it. The primary cause is extended periods without forage access — more than four to six hours increases ulcer risk. Prevention is through continuous or near-continuous forage access, divided concentrate meals (no large grain feeds), and minimizing fasting periods during transport or competition. Medical treatment (omeprazole) is effective but prevention through management is more sustainable.

What is the four-step framework for designing a feeding program?

Step 1: Assess the horse — body weight, BCS (Henneke 1–9), dentition, workload, and life stage. Step 2: Analyze the forage — submit a hay sample for laboratory analysis to determine protein, digestible energy, NSC, and mineral content. Step 3: Close the gaps — use forage adjustments first, then ration balancer for vitamin/mineral deficits, then concentrate for energy deficits. Step 4: Monitor and adjust — weigh the horse monthly, score condition every 2–4 weeks, and adjust ration based on observed changes.

What is choke in horses, and how does it differ from human choking?

Choke in horses is esophageal obstruction — material stuck in the esophagus. Unlike human choking (airway obstruction), a horse in choke can still breathe. Signs include feed and saliva draining from the nostrils, repeated swallowing attempts, and neck stretching. Choke requires veterinary treatment if it does not resolve spontaneously. The primary risk is aspiration pneumonia. It is most common with dry pellets, sugar beet pulp fed dry, hay cubes, or horses with dental problems that impair adequate chewing.

What does 'feeding by weight, not by flake' mean, and why does it matter?

Flake weight — the amount of hay in each flake cut from a bale — varies significantly between species, farms, and individual bales. A flake of alfalfa can weigh more than twice what a flake of timothy weighs. Feeding by flake count without knowing flake weight means the actual amount of hay the horse receives is unknown. A hay scale ensures the horse receives the correct dry matter intake. Most horses are either under-supplied or over-supplied when flake count is used as the primary measure.

Key Vocabulary

Hindgut Fermentation

The process by which microorganisms in the cecum and large colon break down fiber (cellulose and hemicellulose) into volatile fatty acids — the primary energy source for the horse. The integrity of the hindgut microbial population is essential to this process and is disrupted by sudden diet changes, antibiotics, and excess starch reaching the hindgut.

Non-Structural Carbohydrates (NSC)

Sugars and starches in forage and feed — the carbohydrate fraction that is rapidly digested and absorbed. NSC values above 10–12% in hay are considered elevated risk for horses with metabolic syndrome, Cushing's disease, or prior laminitis. NSC is measured through laboratory forage analysis and cannot be estimated visually.

Body Condition Score (BCS)

A standardized numerical assessment of a horse's body fat reserves, using the Henneke 1–9 scale. Evaluated at six anatomical locations: neck, withers, shoulder, ribs, loin, and tailhead. A score of 5–6 is appropriate for most adult horses. BCS is used to monitor dietary adequacy and guide feeding program adjustments.

Ration Balancer

A low-calorie, high-nutrient-density concentrate designed to supply the vitamins, minerals, and protein absent or deficient in an all-forage diet without adding significant caloric load. Appropriate for horses in light work or at maintenance that need nutritional completion without added energy.

Forage Analysis

A laboratory test of hay or pasture that measures crude protein, digestible energy, acid detergent fiber, neutral detergent fiber, non-structural carbohydrates, and key minerals. Provides the quantitative data needed for accurate ration balancing and is the only reliable way to know what a specific forage source contains.

Laminitis

Inflammation of the sensitive laminae — the tissue bonding the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall inside the hoof capsule. When acute and severe, leads to rotation or sinking (founder) of the coffin bone. A medical emergency requiring immediate veterinary intervention. Feed-related triggers include carbohydrate overload from grain or high-sugar pasture.

EGUS (Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome)

Ulceration of the stomach lining caused by prolonged exposure of the squamous mucosa to stomach acid in the absence of forage buffering. Highly prevalent in performance horses. Primary prevention is continuous forage access; primary treatment is omeprazole. Many affected horses show no obvious clinical signs.

Hindgut Acidosis

A disruption of normal hindgut fermentation caused by excess starch or sugar reaching the cecum and large colon, where it is rapidly fermented and produces lactic acid. Lowers hindgut pH, killing beneficial microorganisms and disrupting the fermentation balance. Associated with colic, diarrhea, and laminitis. Prevented by limiting starch per meal and maintaining adequate forage intake.

Common Mistakes

Using grain as the dietary foundation rather than forage

The most widespread nutritional error in domestic horse management. Grain should supplement forage when energy needs exceed what forage can provide — not replace it. Horses fed primarily concentrate with minimal hay experience gastric acid buildup (ulcers), hindgut microbial disruption (colic, hindgut acidosis), and metabolic stress. Correcting the forage base resolves a wider range of nutritional management problems than any concentrate change.

Making dietary changes too quickly

The hindgut microbiome requires 10–14 days minimum to adjust to a new feed substrate. Changing from one hay source to another, switching from hay to pasture, or introducing concentrate rapidly disrupts microbial populations and increases risk of gas colic, hindgut acidosis, and laminitis. All dietary transitions should be made gradually, regardless of how similar the new feed appears to the old one.

Assuming fresh green grass is always safe for all horses

Lush spring pasture or rapidly growing grass after rain can be extremely high in non-structural carbohydrates. For horses with metabolic syndrome (EMS), Cushing's disease (PPID), or a history of laminitis, unrestricted spring pasture access is a primary laminitis trigger. Grazing management — restricting hours of access, using a grazing muzzle, or testing pasture NSC — is required for these horses regardless of how healthy the grass looks.

Adding supplements to horses already on fortified feed

Commercial concentrates and complete feeds are formulated to supply the vitamins and minerals horses need at the manufacturer's recommended feeding rate. Adding individual supplements to horses already receiving fortified feed can produce nutrient imbalances — particularly with fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and selenium, which has a narrow therapeutic window. Any supplement addition should begin with verification that the current program is nutritionally incomplete.

Delaying veterinary contact for colic or laminitis

Both colic and laminitis can progress from manageable to critical within hours. A strangulating colic that might be surgically correctable at hour two may not be at hour eight. Early signs of laminitis (heat, digital pulse, altered stance) that receive veterinary intervention within the first 24 hours carry a significantly better prognosis than cases that go 48–72 hours without treatment. The cost of an early veterinary call is far lower than the cost of late intervention in either condition.

Horse Bowl Focus

What percentage of body weight should a horse receive in forage dry matter daily?

A minimum of 1.5% of body weight per day. For a 1,100-pound horse, that is approximately 16 pounds of dry matter from hay. Many horses benefit from 2% or more. This is a frequently tested number in equine nutrition quiz contexts — know the percentage and the calculation for a given body weight.

What is the Henneke Body Condition Score scale and what are the endpoints?

The Henneke BCS scale runs from 1 to 9. A score of 1 is emaciated (no fat cover, skeleton visually prominent). A score of 9 is extremely obese (heavy fat deposits, cresty neck, obvious fat pads). A score of 5 (moderate) is considered ideal for most adult horses; 5–6 is the appropriate target range. These values are commonly asked in Horse Bowl nutrition questions.

What is the maximum safe starch intake per meal for a horse?

No more than 0.5 kg of starch per 100 kg of body weight per meal. For a 500 kg horse, that is no more than 2.5 kg of starch per meal. Exceeding this limit causes undigested starch to reach the hindgut, where it disrupts fermentation. This is a specific, testable number in Horse Bowl nutrition questions.

What are the signs of colic? (commonly tested list)

Pawing at the ground, looking at or biting at the flank, refusing feed, repeatedly lying down and getting up, rolling, sweating without exertion, elevated heart rate (above 44 bpm at rest), absent or reduced gut sounds on auscultation, and pale or discolored mucous membranes. Horse Bowl questions may ask for a specific list or ask which signs indicate severity.

What are the early signs of laminitis? (commonly tested list)

Increased digital pulse (palpable at the back of the fetlock), heat in the hooves, a characteristic stance where weight is shifted back to unload the toes, reluctance to move or walk on hard ground, and short-striding gait. In chronic laminitis: rings on the hoof wall (growth rings wider at the heel), white line disease, and flat or convex sole. Horse Bowl laminitis questions often involve recognizing early signs.

What is EGUS and what percentage of performance horses does it affect?

Equine Gastric Ulcer Syndrome. Research has documented prevalence of 60–90% in racehorses and endurance horses, with some studies reporting even higher rates in horses under competition and training stress. The high prevalence makes it a frequent Horse Bowl topic — know the definition, the cause (stomach acid damage in the absence of forage buffering), and the primary prevention (continuous forage access).

What is a ration balancer and when is it used?

A ration balancer is a low-calorie, high-nutrient-density feed supplement designed to supply the vitamins, minerals, and protein that forage alone does not provide — without adding significant caloric load. It is used for horses in light work or at maintenance that need nutritional completion without weight gain. Horse Bowl questions may present a scenario (a horse on grass hay that is maintaining condition but showing vitamin/mineral deficiency signs) and ask for the appropriate supplement type.

What is choke in horses, and how does it differ from choking in humans?

In horses, choke is esophageal obstruction — material lodged in the esophagus. Unlike human choking (which blocks the airway), a horse with choke can still breathe. Signs include draining of feed and saliva from the nostrils, repeated swallowing, and neck stretching. The primary danger is aspiration pneumonia from material entering the airway during swallowing attempts. Horse Bowl questions may ask for this definition, the key clinical sign (feed draining from nostrils), or the primary complication.
