Equine Nutrition

Designing a Feeding Program Feeding Program

Building a complete, balanced feeding program using body condition scoring, life stage guidelines, and forage-first principles.

A feeding program is not a list of what to feed — it is a systematic assessment of what the horse needs, what the current diet provides, and what changes close the gap between the two. Building a feeding program begins not with the feed room but with the horse: its body condition, its life stage, its workload, and its individual metabolism.

The Henneke Body Condition Score (BCS) system is the standard tool for assessing whether a horse's current diet is meeting its energy needs. BCS is assessed on a 1–9 scale, where 1 is extremely thin and 9 is extremely obese. A score of 5 (moderate) to 6 (moderately fleshy) is appropriate for most adult horses. A BCS of 4 or below warrants a diet increase; a BCS of 7 or above warrants a diet reduction.

Feeding Program Framework
Step 1

Assess the Horse

Before making any feed changes, establish baseline: weigh or estimate the horse's body weight (weight tape ± 50–75 lbs), score body condition (Henneke BCS 1–9), assess dentition (poor teeth = poor hay utilization), evaluate workload (maintenance, light, moderate, heavy, very heavy), and note life stage (maintenance, growth, pregnancy, lactation, senior).

Step 2

Analyze Current Forage

Submit a hay sample to a forage testing laboratory. Results will show crude protein, digestible energy, non-structural carbohydrates, calcium, phosphorus, and other key minerals. Compare these values to the National Research Council (NRC) requirements for the horse's life stage and workload. Most good-quality grass hay meets energy and protein needs for horses at maintenance but is deficient in vitamins A and E and possibly selenium.

Step 3

Close the Gaps

Address nutritional gaps identified in the forage analysis. If energy is deficient, add calorie-dense forage (alfalfa blend) before adding concentrate. If vitamins and minerals are deficient, add a ration balancer at the appropriate feeding rate. If the horse is in hard work with high energy demands that forage cannot meet, add concentrate in divided meals, introducing it gradually over two to four weeks.

Step 4

Monitor and Adjust

A feeding program is not set-and-forget. Weigh the horse monthly. Score body condition every two to four weeks. Note changes in coat quality, energy level, and behavior that may indicate nutritional adequacy or deficiency. Adjust forage and concentrate amounts based on observed changes, not on fixed amounts. A horse that gains weight in spring on the same winter hay diet needs its ration reduced, not continued on the same schedule.

Life stage drives nutritional requirements more than any other variable. A lactating mare at peak milk production requires nearly double the digestible energy of the same mare at maintenance. A senior horse with declining dental function may need the same nutritional content as a younger horse but in a form that requires less chewing — wet feeds, soaked hay cubes, or complete feeds.

For horse management, demonstrating the ability to design a feeding program based on BCS, forage analysis, and life stage is a distinguishing skill that reflects the integration of multiple course topics. Horse Bowl questions on nutrition frequently present scenarios — a horse losing weight on a given ration — that require applying this systematic framework to identify the likely cause and correction.

Things to Remember

  • Building a feeding program starts with assessing the horse: body condition score, age, breed, workload, and life stage all affect requirements.
  • The Henneke Body Condition Score (1–9) is the standard tool for assessing fat reserves. A score of 4–6 is ideal for most horses.
  • Feed changes must happen gradually over 10–14 days minimum to allow hindgut microbes to adjust without disruption.
  • Monitoring and adjusting is ongoing — a feeding program is never 'set and forget.' Seasonal changes, workload changes, and condition changes all require reassessment.
