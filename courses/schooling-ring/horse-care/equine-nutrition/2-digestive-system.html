Understanding equine digestive anatomy — from the small stomach to the hindgut fermentation chamber — and why it drives every feeding guideline.

Why Digestive Anatomy Drives Every Feeding Decision

The horse's digestive system is the most important factor in feed management decisions. Unlike cattle and other ruminants, horses are hindgut fermenters — their primary digestive fermentation chamber comes after the stomach, not before it. This arrangement has profound implications for how horses should be fed, how often, and what happens when those principles are violated.

Most feeding errors — from colic risk to gastric ulcers to behavioral issues — can be traced directly to management decisions that conflict with equine digestive anatomy. Understanding how the system works is the starting point for understanding why feeding guidelines exist.

Digestive Anatomy

Key Structures of the Equine Digestive System

Structure 1

A Small Stomach

A horse's stomach holds approximately 8 to 15 liters — relatively small for the horse's body size. The stomach empties continuously rather than holding feed for extended digestion. This design evolved for near-constant grazing on small amounts of forage, not for large, infrequent grain or hay meals. Overfeeding concentrate at a single feeding overwhelms the stomach's capacity and pushes undigested starch into the hindgut.

Structure 2

The Hindgut: Fermentation Chamber

The cecum and large intestine make up the hindgut, where microbial fermentation breaks down fibrous plant material. This process produces volatile fatty acids that supply a significant portion of the horse's daily energy. The microbial population performing this fermentation is sensitive to sudden dietary changes — particularly sudden increases in starch or sugar — which can disrupt the microbial balance and trigger colic or laminitis.

Structure 3

Why Forage Comes First

The entire equine digestive system evolved around processing grass. Forage — hay and pasture — moves through the system in a way that supports healthy microbial populations, appropriate gut motility, and normal hydration of digestive contents. Reducing forage below minimum thresholds (approximately 1.5% of body weight per day in dry matter) disrupts all three functions simultaneously.

Structure 4

Difference from Ruminants

Cattle and sheep ferment feed in a multi-chambered forestomach before it reaches the small intestine. Horses process carbohydrates in the small intestine first, then ferment fiber in the hindgut. This means feeds appropriate for ruminants are not automatically appropriate for horses — and vice versa. Urea-based nitrogen supplements safe for cattle, for example, are toxic to horses.

A horse that goes more than four to six hours without forage begins to experience gastric acid pooling in the stomach. The stomach produces acid continuously regardless of whether food is present — making prolonged fasting a structural risk factor for gastric ulcers, not merely a preference issue.

Management Implications

Understanding digestive anatomy translates directly into feeding management decisions. Horses should have access to forage — hay or pasture — as close to continuously as practical. Grain and concentrate meals should be small and divided across multiple feedings. Dietary changes should be made gradually over a minimum of 10 to 14 days to allow the hindgut microbial population to adjust.

In horse management, demonstrating knowledge of equine digestive anatomy is foundational to scoring well on management questions, veterinary science sections, and Horse Bowl categories related to nutrition and health.

Things to Remember

  • Horses are hindgut fermenters: primary fiber digestion happens in the cecum and large intestine, after the stomach — not before it like in cattle.
  • The horse's stomach holds only 8–15 liters and empties continuously — it was designed for near-constant small meals of grass, not large grain feedings.
  • Going 4–6 hours without forage allows gastric acid to pool, significantly increasing ulcer risk.
  • Forage (hay and grass) must come first: minimum 1.5% of body weight in dry matter per day to maintain healthy gut function.
