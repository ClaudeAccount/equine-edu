Equine Nutrition

The Equine Digestive System

Understanding how the horse's digestive system works explains almost every feeding decision, from meal size and forage access to feed changes and health prevention.

Feeding Starts with the Body

Good feeding choices come from knowing how a horse's body handles food.

The same feeding habit can help one horse and hurt another. It depends on how well it fits the horse's gut. Big grain meals, too few meals, or sudden diet changes each affect the gut in their own way.

Most feeding-related health problems happen when feeding does not match how the gut is built to work.

Horses are hindgut fermenters. This means most of their fiber is broken down in the large intestine, near the back of the gut, not in the stomach. This one fact explains many feeding rules.

💡 Did You Know

A horse's stomach is small for such a big animal. It holds only about 2 to 4 gallons. In the wild, horses eat small amounts almost all day, so food keeps moving through. Big meals that are far apart can overload this system and raise the risk of ulcers, choke, and colic.

Digestive Anatomy

Digestive Anatomy

Segment 1

Front of the Gut: Mouth to Small Intestine

Digestion starts in the mouth. Horses grind their food with a side-to-side chew. Chewing makes saliva, or spit, which helps protect the stomach and start digestion.

The food pipe only works one way. Horses cannot throw up. That is why a blocked food pipe or an overfull stomach is a real emergency.

The stomach is small and empties fast. The small intestine is about 70 feet long. This is where most protein, fat, and sugar are taken in.

Segment 2

Hindgut: Cecum and Large Intestine

The back part of the gut is called the hindgut. Tiny helpers called microbes live here. They break down the fiber from hay and turn it into energy for the horse.

These microbes do not like sudden change. A quick change in food, meal size, or meal times can upset them. When they are upset, it can cause colic and other gut problems.

Segment 3

How Long Food Takes

Food takes about 36 to 72 hours to move all the way through a horse.

The large colon has bends and tight spots where food can slow down or get stuck. That is why water, hay, and steady meals matter so much.

The small colon shapes the manure and pulls out water before it leaves the body.

Segment 4

Why Starch Must Be Watched

Horses break down starch and sugar in the small intestine first. When a horse eats too much starch at once, the extra slips past into the hindgut.

The microbes back there are not made to handle starch. They make acid, fall out of balance, and this can lead to colic or laminitis, a painful hoof problem.

This is why grain and other starchy feeds must be fed with care.

Where the Feeding Rules Come From

Every feeding rule comes from how the gut is built:

  • Feed small meals often, because the stomach is small.
  • Make hay the base of the diet, because fiber is broken down in the hindgut.
  • Change feed slowly, because the microbes need time to adjust.
  • Keep water available, because the large colon needs water to move food safely.

These rules are not made up. The horse's body needs them.

Important: Horses Cannot Throw Up

One of the most important facts about horses is that they cannot throw up.

The food pipe only works one way, so food cannot come back up.

This makes choke and an overfull stomach much more dangerous for a horse than for animals that can throw up.

In the Barn

Early signs of gut trouble often show up in how a horse acts. Watch for pawing, looking at its side, eating less, restlessness, or rolling.

These signs can point to different parts of the gut, like gas in the stomach, a blockage in the large colon, or upset in the hindgut.

Knowing how the gut works helps you spot trouble sooner and explain it clearly to a vet.

Things to Remember

  • Horses are hindgut fermenters. Fiber is broken down in the back of the gut.
  • The stomach is small, about 2 to 4 gallons. It is built for small meals all day.
  • Horses cannot throw up, so a blocked gut is a serious emergency.
  • Sudden changes or too much starch can upset the gut microbes and cause colic or laminitis.
  • Feeding rules come from how the horse's body works, not from habit.
