Equine Nutrition

Designing a Feeding Program

Building a complete, balanced feeding program using body condition scoring, life stage guidelines, and forage-first principles.

A feeding program is not just a list of feeds. It is a plan built around the horse's needs, the hay you have, and how well the diet is working. Start with the horse, not the feed room.

A horse's needs depend on its weight, its age and stage of life, how hard it works, and how its body burns energy. The feed you pick is just how you meet those needs.

💡 Did You Know

The Henneke Body Condition Score was made at Texas A&M University in 1983. It is now the main way to check how much fat a horse carries. It looks at six spots on the body:

  • Neck.
  • Withers.
  • Shoulder.
  • Ribs.
  • Loin.
  • Tailhead.

These spots are used because horses gain and lose fat in the same pattern.

Feeding Program Framework
Step 1

Assess the Horse

Every feeding plan starts by looking closely at the horse. Check:

  • Body weight (use a weight tape or scale).
  • Body Condition Score (the 1 to 9 Henneke scale).
  • Teeth, which affect chewing and digestion.
  • How hard the horse works (from rest to very hard).
  • Stage of life (growing, grown, pregnant, nursing, or older).

Get the full picture of the horse before you choose any feed.

Step 2

Analyze Current Forage

Hay is the base of the diet, so check it first. When you can, send a hay sample to a lab. The test shows:

  • Protein.
  • Energy.
  • Sugar and starch (NSC).
  • Calcium and phosphorus.
  • Key minerals.

Then compare these numbers to what the horse needs for its work and stage of life.

Most grass hays give enough energy for a resting horse, but may be low in:

  • Vitamin A.
  • Vitamin E.
  • Selenium (in some areas).
Step 3

Close the Nutritional Gaps

Once you know what the hay has, fill the gaps in order:

  • Fix the forage first.
    • Add more hay, or mix in alfalfa, if the horse needs more energy.
  • Add a ration balancer if needed.
    • It adds vitamins, minerals, and protein without much weight.
  • Add grain only for high energy needs.
    • Use it only when hay cannot give enough energy.
    • Add it slowly over 2 to 4 weeks.
    • Split it into a few meals a day.
Step 4

Monitor and Adjust

A feeding plan is never finished. Keep checking with:

  • A weight check each month.
  • A body condition check every 2 to 4 weeks.
  • A look at the coat, the mood, and the energy level.

Change the feed based on changes in the horse, not on a set amount. The seasons and the horse's workload will keep changing what it needs.

Life Stage and Nutritional Demand

A horse's stage of life has one of the biggest effects on what it needs. For example:

  • A nursing mare may need nearly twice the energy of a resting horse.
  • Growing horses need a good balance of protein, energy, and minerals.
  • Older horses may need softer, easier-to-chew feed because of worn teeth.

Often it is the form of the feed that changes more than the nutrients themselves.

In the Barn

Good feeding depends on measuring, not guessing. Three key tools:

  • A hay scale, to see how much the horse really eats.
  • A weight tape, to track weight changes.
  • The Body Condition Score, to check fat.

Counting flakes or eyeballing it often leads to too much or too little.

Check the feeding plan often. Weight, hay amount, and body condition give you what you need to make changes before problems start.

Things to Remember

  • Feeding plans are built around the horse, not the feed room.
  • The Body Condition Score (1 to 9) is the main way to check a horse's condition.
  • Hay and grass are the base of the diet.
  • Fill gaps in order: hay first, then balancer, then grain.
  • Make feed changes slowly, over 10 to 14 days.
  • Keep checking and adjusting the plan over time.
