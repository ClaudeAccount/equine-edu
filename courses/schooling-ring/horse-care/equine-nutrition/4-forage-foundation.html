Equine Nutrition

Forage: The Foundation

Forage, hay and pasture, is the base of every equine feeding program. Every other part of the diet is built around it.

Forage means hay and grass. It is the base of every horse's diet. Everything else is built around it.

A horse's gut is made to chew on plants for many hours a day. Hay and grass give this fiber. The fiber feeds the gut microbes, keeps food moving, and soaks up stomach acid.

No grain or supplement can fix a diet that is short on hay.

The Base of the Diet

The biggest forage question is not which hay to pick. It is whether the horse gets enough hay.

A horse should get at least 1.5% of its body weight in hay each day.

For a 1,100-pound horse, that is about 16 pounds of hay a day.

💡 Did You Know

A horse's stomach is small and is made for eating small amounts often. When a horse goes hours with no hay, stomach acid builds up on the empty lining. Long gaps with no hay are a top cause of stomach ulcers.

Grass hay is the most common hay for grown horses that are not working hard.

Common grass hays include:

  • Timothy.
  • Orchardgrass.
  • Bromegrass.
  • Bermudagrass.

Grass hays have less protein and energy than legume hays. That makes them a good fit for most grown horses and horses that do light work.

Grass hay is also used for horses that gain weight easily or have had hoof problems. For these horses, the sugar and starch level in the hay matters. This is called NSC (non-structural carbohydrates). They do best on hay with NSC under 10%.

Grass hays are not all the same:

  • Timothy is common, and horses like it.
  • Orchardgrass has a bit more protein and energy.
  • Bermudagrass grows well in warm places.
  • Coastal bermudagrass is coarser and can raise the risk of a gut blockage in some horses.

Grass hay works for most horses at rest or in light work, as long as the hay is good quality.

Legume hays have more protein and energy than grass hays.

Common legume hays include:

  • Alfalfa, also called lucerne.
  • Clover.

These hays fit horses that need more from their food, like:

  • Horses in hard work.
  • Mares that are pregnant or nursing.
  • Growing horses.
  • Horses that need to gain weight.

Alfalfa usually has 14 to 18% protein. Most grass hays have only 8 to 12%. Alfalfa also has more energy and more calcium.

Because it is so rich, legume hay is often too much for easy keepers (horses that gain weight fast) and horses that have had hoof problems.

A common trick is to mix alfalfa with grass hay to balance the energy.

You cannot fully judge hay just by looking. But a quick look still tells you a lot.

Things to check:

  • Color: bright green often means good hay. Yellow or brown means it lost value to weather.
  • Leaves: more leaves usually mean better food value.
  • Smell: fresh and grassy is good. Do not feed musty or dusty hay.
  • Cutting time: hay cut earlier is easier to digest and has more protein.

A hay test at a lab gives the most exact numbers, like:

  • Protein.
  • Energy.
  • Sugar and starch (NSC).
  • Minerals.

For horses that gain weight easily or have had hoof problems, the NSC number matters a lot. You cannot tell it just by looking.

Feed hay by weight, not by the number of flakes.

Flakes are not all the same. Their size and weight change with the bale, the kind of hay, and how it was cut.

Use a hay scale to weigh the hay. Counting flakes can lead to feeding too little or too much, even when it looks right.

In the Barn

Picking and storing hay is part of daily barn work.

For best results:

  • Buy hay from sellers you trust.
  • Ask for a hay test when you can.
  • Keep hay off the ground and away from water and sun.
  • Do not feed moldy, dusty, or weather-damaged hay.

Grass changes with the seasons. Spring grass can be high in sugar. This can raise the risk of hoof problems in some horses. For horses that gain weight easily, watch their grass time and follow your vet's advice.

Things to Remember

  • Hay and grass are the base of every horse's diet.
  • A horse needs at least 1.5% of its body weight in hay each day.
  • Grass hays have less energy. Legume hays have more energy and protein.
  • Hay can vary a lot. Test it when the exact numbers matter.
  • You cannot fully judge hay just by looking at it.
  • A lab test gives the most exact food values.
  • Feed hay by weight, not by flake.
