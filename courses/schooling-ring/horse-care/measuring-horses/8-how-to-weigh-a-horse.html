Measuring Horses

Weighing Horses

Few barns own a livestock scale, so a horse's weight is usually estimated — with a weight tape, with a simple measuring formula, and alongside a body condition score that shows whether that weight is healthy.

Why Weight Is Worth Estimating

Knowing a horse's weight is not just a number for the record book. Dewormer and many medications are dosed by body weight, feed and hay are measured against it, and a steady record of weight is one of the clearest early signs of a health problem. Because most owners cannot put a horse on a scale, they rely on practical methods that give a close, repeatable estimate.

The tabs below cover three tools that work together: a weight tape for a quick field estimate, a measuring formula for a closer figure, and the body condition score, which judges whether the weight a horse carries is too little, too much, or about right.

💡 Did You Know

Dosing by guesswork is risky. Many wormers are sold in a single tube measured for an average-sized horse, and giving too little is a leading cause of medicines losing their effect over time. A weight estimate that is even roughly correct helps a horse get the right amount — enough to work, without waste.

A weight tape is the fastest way to estimate weight in the barn. It is a soft tape marked in pounds or kilograms instead of inches.

Using a Weight Tape

Reading Weight From the Heart Girth

A weight tape estimates body weight from the size of the heart girth — the distance around the horse's barrel just behind the front legs. With the horse standing square on level ground, the tape is passed around the body so it crosses the highest point of the withers and meets underneath, just behind the elbows. The tape should lie flat and be drawn snug but not tight, and the reading is taken as the horse breathes out.

The number where the tape meets is the estimated weight. A weight tape is quick and needs no math, which makes it ideal for routine checks. It is less exact than a scale and can read high or low on horses with an unusual build, so its real value is in tracking change: taping the same horse the same way each month shows whether weight is steady, rising, or dropping.

A weight tape gives an estimate, not an exact weight. Used the same way each time, it is most useful for spotting a trend — steady, gaining, or losing — rather than for a single perfect number. Always tape at the heart girth, behind the elbows, as the horse exhales.

A cloth tape and a little arithmetic give a closer estimate than a weight tape alone, using two measurements and a fixed number.

The Weight Formula

Heart Girth, Body Length, and the Constant

The standard estimate for an adult horse uses this formula, with both measurements taken in inches:

Weight in pounds = (heart girth × heart girth × body length) ÷ 330

The heart girth is measured around the barrel just behind the front legs and over the withers, exactly as for a weight tape. The body length is measured along the side of the horse from the point of the shoulder back to the point of the buttock, with the tape kept level. Multiplying the heart girth by itself, then by the body length, and dividing by 330 gives the estimated weight.

For example, a horse with a heart girth of 78 inches and a body length of 65 inches works out to 78 × 78 × 65 ÷ 330, which is about 1,200 pounds. The number 330 is the value used for a mature horse. Growing horses carry their weight differently, so a smaller constant is used for them.

Measurement 1

Heart Girth

Around the barrel just behind the front legs and over the withers. Measured in inches, drawn snug as the horse exhales.

Measurement 2

Body Length

From the point of the shoulder to the point of the buttock, tape kept level and parallel to the ground.

The Constant

330, 301, or 280

Divide by 330 for a mature horse. Use 301 for a yearling and 280 for a weanling, because young horses are built differently.

Why It Helps

Closer Than a Tape

Because it uses two measurements instead of one, the formula often gives a closer estimate than a weight tape, with only simple math.

Weight tells how much horse there is; body condition tells whether that weight is healthy. The two are read together.

Body Condition Scoring

The 1-to-9 Condition Scale

Body condition scoring rates how much fat a horse carries on a scale from 1 to 9, where 1 is emaciated and 9 is extremely fat. For most horses the healthy range is about 4 to 6, with 5 considered ideal. This scale — commonly called the Henneke system after the researcher who developed it — is widely used because it needs no equipment and works on any breed.

A score is found by both looking at the horse and feeling it with the hands, because a winter coat or heavy muscling can hide or fake fat. Fat is checked at six areas: along the neck, over the withers, behind the shoulder, across the ribs, along the loin and back, and around the tailhead. Combining what the eye sees with what the hand feels at these points gives a score that reflects fat cover rather than size.

Low Range

Scores 1–3

Too thin. Ribs, backbone, and hip bones are easy to see, and the horse needs more feed and a health check.

Healthy Range

Scores 4–6

About right for most horses. Ribs cannot be easily seen but can be felt, and the body looks smooth and even.

High Range

Scores 7–9

Too heavy. Fat builds along the neck, over the ribs, and around the tailhead, raising the risk of health problems.

How to Check

Look and Feel

Assess fat by sight and by hand at six points: neck, withers, behind the shoulder, ribs, loin, and tailhead.

In the Barn

The most useful picture of a horse's weight comes from using these tools together. A weight tape or the measuring formula gives a number to track and to dose medicine by, while a body condition score every few weeks shows whether the horse is the right weight for its frame. A horse can be a "good weight" on the tape and still be too fat or too thin in condition — checking both is what turns a measurement into a feeding decision.

Things to Remember

  • Most horses are not weighed on a scale, so weight is estimated with a tape, a formula, and a condition score.
  • A weight tape estimates weight from the heart girth and is best for tracking change over time.
  • The weight formula is (heart girth × heart girth × body length) ÷ 330 for a mature horse, with measurements in inches.
  • Use 301 for a yearling and 280 for a weanling in place of 330.
  • Body condition scoring rates fat from 1 to 9, with 4 to 6 healthy, judged by sight and feel at six points on the body.
