Measuring Horses

Weighing Horses

Most barns do not have a large livestock scale, so a horse's weight is usually estimated instead. Learning how to estimate weight helps with feeding, medication, and keeping horses healthy.

Why Weight Matters

Knowing a horse's weight is an important part of everyday horse care. Veterinarians, owners, and trainers all use weight to make decisions about feeding, medications, and overall health.

Since most horses are not weighed on a scale, horse owners use several simple methods to estimate weight. While these methods are not exact, they provide reliable estimates when used correctly.

💡 Did You Know

Giving too little dewormer or medication may not work as well as it should. Estimating a horse's weight helps make sure the correct amount is given.

A weight tape is one of the easiest ways to estimate a horse's weight. It is a soft tape marked in pounds or kilograms instead of inches.

Using a Weight Tape

A Quick Weight Estimate

A weight tape is one of the easiest ways to estimate a horse's weight.

The tape is wrapped around the horse's heart girth, just behind the front legs and over the highest part of the withers. Instead of inches, the tape is marked in pounds or kilograms.

To get the best estimate:

  • Stand the horse square on level ground.
  • Wrap the tape around the heart girth.
  • Keep the tape snug but not tight.
  • Read the number as the horse relaxes and breathes out.

A weight tape gives an estimate, not an exact weight. Its greatest value is measuring the same horse over time to see whether it is gaining, losing, or maintaining weight.

Key Point: A weight tape is quick, easy to use, and best for tracking weight changes over time.

Using two measurements instead of one can give a closer estimate than a weight tape alone.

The Weight Formula

A More Accurate Estimate

Using two measurements instead of one can provide a closer estimate of a horse's weight. The formula for a mature horse is:

Weight (lb) = (Heart Girth × Heart Girth × Body Length) ÷ 330

To use the formula, measure:

Measurement 1

Heart Girth

Measure around the horse's barrel just behind the front legs and over the withers.

Measurement 2

Body Length

Measure from the point of the shoulder to the point of the buttock while keeping the tape level.

Because the formula uses two measurements, it is often more accurate than a weight tape alone.

Key Point: The weight formula combines heart girth and body length to estimate body weight.

Weight tells how much horse there is; body condition tells whether that weight is healthy. The two are read together.

Body Condition Scoring

Looking Beyond the Number

Weight tells how heavy a horse is. Body condition tells whether that weight is healthy.

The Body Condition Score (BCS) uses a scale from 1 to 9.

  • 1–3: Too thin
  • 4–6: Healthy for most horses
  • 7–9: Overweight

Instead of using a scale, body condition scoring looks at how much fat the horse carries. Check these six areas:

  • Neck
  • Withers
  • Behind the shoulder
  • Ribs
  • Loin
  • Tailhead

Both looking and feeling are important because thick hair coats and heavy muscles can hide body fat.

Key Point: Body condition scoring measures body fat, not body weight.

In the Barn

The best picture of a horse's health comes from using more than one tool. A weight tape or weight formula estimates how much the horse weighs, while a body condition score shows whether that weight is healthy. Tracking both over time makes it easier to notice changes before they become bigger problems.

Things to Remember

  • Most horses are weighed by estimating their weight rather than using a scale.
  • A weight tape measures around the heart girth and provides a quick estimate.
  • The weight formula uses both heart girth and body length for a closer estimate.
  • Body Condition Scores range from 1 to 9, with 4 to 6 being healthy for most horses.
  • Weight and body condition work together to help monitor a horse's health.
