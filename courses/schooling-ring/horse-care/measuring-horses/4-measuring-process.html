Measuring Horses

The Measuring Process

Accurate measurement depends on three things: finding the correct anatomical point on the horse, using the right equipment, and ensuring the horse is positioned and standing correctly before the measurement is taken.

Technique Determines Accuracy

Two people measuring the same horse on the same day can arrive at different results if one follows correct measuring technique and the other does not. The most common sources of error are measuring at the wrong point on the horse's body, allowing the horse to stand on unlevel ground, or reading the measuring stick at the wrong scale. Correct technique eliminates these variables and produces a measurement that can be relied upon.

The following content covers the three elements that must be correct for an accurate measurement: identifying the withers, setting up the horse correctly, and using measuring equipment properly.

💡 Did You Know

In some countries and formal competitions, official height measurements must be performed by a licensed measurer, with the horse standing on a certified level measuring pad. The horse may also be required to be barefoot (unshod) at the time of official measurement, as horseshoes add height to a measurement. In informal settings, shoes are typically left on — but it is worth noting that a shod horse will measure slightly taller than the same horse barefoot.

The Measuring Point

The Withers — Where Every Height Measurement Is Taken

Horse height is always measured at the withers — the highest point of the horse's back, located at the base of the neck where the neck meets the shoulder. The withers are formed by the dorsal spinous processes of the vertebrae in this region and create a visible ridge or prominence between the shoulder blades. This is not the top of the horse's head, the top of the neck, or the croup — it is specifically the bony prominence where the mane ends and the back begins.

The withers are used as the standard measuring point because they represent a stable, bony landmark that does not change significantly with the horse's head position. Measuring to the top of the head would produce inconsistent results because a horse can raise or lower its head by many inches — potentially changing the reading dramatically. The withers stay in a consistent position regardless of where the horse is looking.

Height is always measured to the withers — never to the top of the head or the neck. The withers are the bony ridge at the base of the neck, where the mane ends and the back begins. Using any other point produces a measurement that does not correspond to the established standard and cannot be compared to other horses' recorded heights.

Equipment and Setup

Measuring Tools and the Correct Setup for an Accurate Reading

The standard tool for measuring a horse is a measuring stick — a rigid stick calibrated in hands and inches, with a horizontal arm that extends at a right angle to contact the top of the withers. The arm is leveled using a built-in level bubble. When the level is centered and the stick is vertical, the reading at the ground gives the horse's height. A fabric tape measure can produce an approximate reading but is less reliable than a rigid stick because it conforms to the horse's contours and can introduce error.

Before the measurement is taken, the horse must be standing square on a level, hard surface. "Standing square" means all four hooves are planted directly below the horse's legs, with weight distributed evenly — not camped out in front, stretched back behind, or resting a hind leg. Unlevel ground will raise or lower one side of the horse, and a resting leg creates an asymmetrical stance that distorts the measurement. The handler should ask the horse to stand calmly and wait until the horse settles into a square position before placing the measuring stick.

Equipment

Measuring Stick

A rigid, calibrated stick with a level arm. Preferred for formal measurement because it provides a level reading at the withers without conforming to the horse's body. The level bubble must be centered for an accurate reading.

Equipment

Weight Tape

A fabric tape designed to wrap around the horse's girth and estimate weight from the girth circumference. Not a height-measuring tool, though some tapes include a height scale. Less reliable than a rigid stick for height measurement.

Setup

Level, Hard Surface

The horse must stand on a flat, level, hard surface. Pasture, grass, or soft footing introduces error because hooves sink unevenly. A concrete or asphalt surface is ideal for an accurate reading.

Setup

Square Stance

All four legs should be directly under the horse, weight distributed evenly. A resting leg, camped-out stance, or stretched position will change the height reading. Wait for a balanced, relaxed standing position before measuring.

In the Barn

Most informal height measurements — the kind taken at a barn to update records or select equipment — are done without a measuring pad and without a certified measurer. That does not make them less important to perform correctly. Using a hard, level area such as a barn aisle or concrete pad, waiting for the horse to stand square, placing the stick vertically alongside the horse, and ensuring the level is centered will produce a reliable result. The most common mistake is rushing: a horse that is not standing square, or is standing on uneven footing, can read up to half a hand differently than when properly positioned.

Things to Remember

  • Horse height is always measured at the withers — the bony ridge at the base of the neck where the mane ends.
  • The withers is used as the measuring point because it is a stable bony landmark that does not move with the horse's head position.
  • A rigid measuring stick with a level arm is the standard and most reliable measuring tool.
  • The horse must stand square on a level, hard surface — unlevel ground or an asymmetric stance creates measurement error.
  • Shod horses measure slightly taller than the same horse barefoot; formal measurements may require the horse to be unshod.
