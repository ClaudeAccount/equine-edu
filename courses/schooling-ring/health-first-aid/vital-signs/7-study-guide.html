Normal Ranges

What is the normal temperature range for an adult horse?

99.5 to 101.5 degrees Fahrenheit. Above 102°F warrants veterinary attention. Temperature is taken rectally.

What is the normal heart rate (pulse) range for an adult horse?

28 to 44 beats per minute. Values above 60 bpm at rest, or a consistently elevated rate that does not settle, are reasons to call a veterinarian.

What is the normal respiratory rate for an adult horse?

8 to 16 breaths per minute. Count one rise and fall of the flank as one breath. Rates above 20 breaths per minute at rest may indicate pain, fever, or respiratory compromise.

What does CRT stand for, and what is the normal range?

Capillary refill time. Press on the gum above the upper teeth, release, and count how long it takes for color to return. Normal is under 2 seconds. Longer than 2 seconds indicates poor circulation and requires immediate veterinary contact.

Gut Sounds and Hydration

How many quadrants should have gut sounds, and what does silence indicate?

All four quadrants should have audible gut sounds when checked with a stethoscope. Silence in any quadrant is always a reason to call a veterinarian, as it may indicate reduced intestinal motility or colic.

How is the skin pinch (tenting) test performed, and what is a normal result?

Pinch a fold of skin on the neck and release it. In a well-hydrated horse, the skin snaps back flat within 1 to 2 seconds. If it stays tented (holds its shape for more than 2 seconds), the horse may be dehydrated.

What does normal gum color and texture look like?

Healthy gums are pink, moist, and slightly slippery. Pale, white, gray, or tacky gums indicate a problem and require immediate veterinary attention.

Scenario Spot-Checks

A horse at rest has a respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute and appears restless. What does this suggest, and what should a handler do?

An elevated resting respiratory rate combined with restlessness can indicate pain, colic, fever, or respiratory distress. The handler should take the full set of vitals (temperature, heart rate, gut sounds, CRT) and call a veterinarian.

You press on a horse’s gum and the color takes four seconds to return. What does this mean?

CRT of four seconds is significantly above normal (normal is under 2 seconds) and indicates poor circulation — a veterinary emergency. The horse may be in shock or have cardiovascular compromise.
