Equine Health & Disease Prevention

Parasite Control Control

Strategic deworming programs, fecal egg count testing, and managing anthelmintic resistance.

Internal parasites — primarily cyathostomins (small strongyles), large strongyles, tapeworms, and bots — cause significant damage to the gastrointestinal tract, blood vessels, and liver of infected horses. Parasite management is one of the most important preventive health practices in horse care and one of the most misunderstood.

The practice of rotating deworming drugs on a fixed calendar schedule — the standard approach for several decades — has produced widespread anthelmintic resistance in small strongyle populations. The current evidence-based approach is strategic deworming based on fecal egg count (FEC) monitoring rather than calendar-based rotation.

Strategic Parasite Management
Concept 1

Fecal Egg Count (FEC)

A fecal egg count measures the number of parasite eggs per gram of fresh feces. Results classify horses as low shedders (<200 eggs/gram), moderate shedders (200–500 eggs/gram), or high shedders (>500 eggs/gram). A small proportion of horses — approximately 20–30% — shed the majority of eggs in a herd. Targeting treatment to high shedders reduces drug use, slows resistance development, and focuses resources where they provide the most benefit.

Concept 2

Fecal Egg Count Reduction Test (FECRT)

The FECRT measures anthelmintic efficacy on a specific farm. A fecal sample is collected before treatment; another is collected 14 days after treatment. A reduction in egg count below 95% indicates resistance to the drug class used. The FECRT is the most reliable way to determine whether a chosen dewormer is actually working on the parasites present on a specific property.

Concept 3

Major Parasite Targets

Ivermectin and moxidectin (macrocyclic lactones) remain effective against large strongyles, bots, and many small strongyle stages — but resistance in small strongyles to this class is increasing. Pyrimidine deworming (pyrantel) addresses tapeworms at double dosing. Benzimidazoles (fenbendazole, oxibendazole) have high small strongyle resistance on many farms and should not be used without FECRT confirmation. Praziquantel is the most reliable tapeworm treatment.

Concept 4

Environmental Management

Deworming alone does not control parasite burden — environmental management is equally important. Removing manure from pastures at least twice weekly significantly reduces the number of infective larvae on grazing areas. Composting manure kills most larvae. Avoiding overcrowding (more than one horse per two acres) reduces exposure. Rotating pastures and resting them periodically allows the larval population to decline between grazing periods.

Resistance to commonly used anthelmintics is a farm-specific problem. Dewormer resistance on one farm does not predict the resistance pattern on another. The only reliable way to know which drugs are effective for a specific horse population is to conduct an FECRT. Using a drug that the local parasite population is resistant to provides no protection while contributing to ongoing resistance selection.

Tapeworms require a specific diagnostic test — the ELISA-based EquiSal Tapeworm Saliva Test — for accurate detection, as they shed very few eggs and are not reliably detected by standard FEC. Tapeworm treatment (praziquantel or double-dose pyrantel) is typically recommended once to twice yearly in endemic areas, often timed around the fall when seasonal transmission is highest.

Things to Remember

  • FEC classifies horses as low/moderate/high shedders — treat high shedders; monitor low shedders
  • FECRT (before and 14-day post-treatment egg counts) is the only reliable way to confirm drug efficacy
  • Benzimidazole resistance is widespread — never use without FECRT confirmation first
  • Environmental management (manure removal 2x/week) is as important as drug treatment
