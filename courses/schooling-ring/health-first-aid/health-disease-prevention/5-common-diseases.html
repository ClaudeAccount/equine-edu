Equine Health & Disease Prevention

Common Equine Diseases

Recognition, transmission, and prevention of the diseases most commonly encountered in domestic horse management.

Recognizing common equine diseases — their signs, causes, transmission routes, and appropriate initial responses — is a core competency for anyone responsible for horse care. Early recognition and prompt veterinary contact can make the difference between uncomplicated recovery and permanent damage or death in several common equine conditions.

This module covers both infectious diseases (caused by pathogens and capable of spreading between horses) and non-infectious conditions (caused by management, nutrition, or environment). Both categories require systematic understanding for effective prevention and appropriate response.

Strangles is one of the most contagious bacterial diseases in horses and one of the most commonly reported equine infectious diseases worldwide. Understanding its transmission and biosecurity requirements is critical for any horse facility.

Strangles is caused by Streptococcus equi subsp. equi, a highly contagious bacterium that infects the upper respiratory tract. The name reflects the disease's most dramatic sign: abscesses in the lymph nodes under the jaw and in the throatlatch area that can become so enlarged they compress the airway. Classic signs progress from fever (typically the first sign), then nasal discharge, followed by lymph node enlargement, and ultimately abscess rupture and drainage.

Transmission occurs through direct horse-to-horse contact and through indirect contact with contaminated surfaces, shared water troughs, buckets, tack, grooming tools, and human clothing and hands. Horses can become long-term, asymptomatic carriers — shedding the bacteria intermittently from the guttural pouches for months to years after recovery. The carrier state is detected by culture or PCR testing of guttural pouch lavage samples.

Biosecurity during an outbreak requires immediate and strict isolation of affected horses, dedicated equipment for each affected horse, no horse movement into or out of the facility until two consecutive negative cultures (14 days apart) confirm resolution, and disinfection of all shared surfaces. A strangles outbreak can shut down a facility for weeks to months.

Strangles spreads through contaminated equipment and indirect contact as readily as through direct horse-to-horse contact. Shared buckets, water troughs, bits, and grooming tools are all transmission vectors. No equipment should be shared between horses during an outbreak — or as a general biosecurity practice.

Equine influenza is the most common respiratory disease of horses. It spreads explosively at shows and events and can move through a barn within hours of introduction by a single infected horse.

Equine influenza is caused by influenza A virus strains (primarily H3N8). It spreads through aerosolized respiratory droplets from coughing horses — the virus is so efficiently transmitted that a single infected horse at a show can infect dozens of others within 24–48 hours. Classic signs include sudden-onset high fever (up to 106°F), a dry, harsh, hacking cough, clear to mucopurulent nasal discharge, and profound depression and weakness.

Rest is the cornerstone of treatment. The widely cited guideline is to rest the horse for a minimum of one week (some guidelines recommend one week per day of fever) after the fever resolves before returning to work. Horses returned to exercise too early are at significantly elevated risk for developing secondary bacterial bronchopneumonia — a serious complication that can cause permanent lung damage. The virus itself is not treatable with antibiotics, which are used only for secondary bacterial infections.

The influenza virus mutates rapidly, which is why vaccination requires more frequent updates and more frequent boosters than core vaccines. For horses that travel regularly to events, boosters every 6 months are recommended. Vaccination does not prevent infection entirely but significantly reduces severity, duration of clinical signs, and virus shedding — which reduces spread within a facility.

Premature return to work is the most common management error in equine influenza. The respiratory tract needs full recovery before training stress is reapplied. Rest until 5–7 days after the last sign of fever, then a gradual return to work — not immediate resumption of full training.

Equine herpesvirus exists in several strains with distinct and serious consequences. EHV-1 in particular can cause neurological disease, abortion, and neonatal illness — and requires mandatory reporting when suspected.

Equine herpesvirus (EHV) encompasses several distinct strains with different primary manifestations. EHV-1 and EHV-4 both cause respiratory disease; EHV-1 is additionally capable of causing late-term abortion in mares, equine herpesvirus myeloencephalopathy (EHM, the neurological form), and illness in newborn foals. EHV-2 and EHV-5 cause ocular and pulmonary conditions. EHV-3 causes equine coital exanthema, a venereal disease.

EHM deserves particular attention. It results from EHV-1 replication within blood vessels supplying the spinal cord and brain, causing vasculitis and subsequent neurological signs including progressive hindlimb weakness, ataxia, urinary incontinence, and paralysis in severe cases. Affected horses may be unable to rise in severe cases. Fever typically precedes neurological signs by 6–8 days, making daily temperature monitoring during high-exposure periods a potentially life-saving practice — early detection and isolation can limit spread.

EHV is transmitted through direct contact, contaminated surfaces, and through inhalation of aerosols from nasal discharge. The virus can persist in the environment for weeks under certain conditions. Most horses carry latent EHV infections from prior exposure; stress, illness, or immunosuppression can trigger reactivation. EHM outbreaks have occurred at shows and sales in sufficient numbers that most states require mandatory reporting of suspected cases.

Daily temperature monitoring during high-exposure events is the earliest detection tool for EHV. A temperature above 101.5°F in a horse that has been at a show or exposed to new horses triggers immediate isolation and veterinary contact — before neurological signs develop, when biosecurity measures are most effective.

Laminitis and colic are the two non-infectious conditions most frequently responsible for equine emergency calls. Neither is transmitted between horses, but both cause serious, sometimes fatal damage — and both reward early recognition.

Colic is the term for abdominal pain in horses, encompassing dozens of distinct conditions ranging from mild gas distension to life-threatening intestinal displacement or strangulation. Signs include pawing, looking at the flank, getting up and down repeatedly, rolling, elevated heart rate, lack of gut sounds on auscultation, and refusing feed. Any horse exhibiting more than mild, transient signs requires veterinary evaluation — the horse handler cannot distinguish mild from surgical colic at the farm level.

Laminitis is inflammation and compromised blood flow to the sensitive laminae — the tissue that bonds the coffin bone to the inner hoof wall. Common causes include carbohydrate overload (horses accessing lush grass or grain), systemic illness (sepsis, retained placenta), Cushing's disease (PPID), and equine metabolic syndrome (EMS). Early signs include heat in the hooves, a bounding digital pulse, and a characteristic weight-shifted stance where the horse leans backward to take weight off the toes. Rotation or sinking of the coffin bone within the hoof capsule is the severe sequela and may require months of intensive treatment.

Both conditions become significantly harder to manage with delayed treatment. With colic, a strangulation that might be correctable with surgery becomes inoperable after several hours of compromise. With laminitis, coffin bone rotation that might be stabilized with early veterinary and farriery intervention becomes a chronic, career-limiting condition with delayed response. Early contact with a veterinarian is the most important action in both emergencies.

Colic and laminitis are the leading causes of death and retirement in domestic horses. Both respond better to early intervention than to delayed treatment — the difference between walking a colicky horse until vet arrival and waiting hours for symptoms to worsen can determine whether surgery is still an option.

In the Barn

Knowing a horse's normal vital signs makes abnormal signs recognizable. Temperature (99–101°F), heart rate (28–44 bpm), respiratory rate (12–20 breaths per minute), and gut sounds (present in all four quadrants) should be familiar baselines for every horse in the barn. Recording these during routine daily handling means that a heart rate of 60 bpm or absent gut sounds stands out immediately as abnormal — rather than requiring the handler to guess what is normal for that horse in that moment. Equine education project record books require documentation of routine vital sign checks. Non-infectious diseases — heaves (equine asthma), Cushing's (PPID), and equine metabolic syndrome — are increasingly common in middle-aged and senior horses and warrant annual management review with a veterinarian as horses age.

Things to Remember

  • Temperature >101.5°F at rest = abnormal; daily monitoring during outbreaks enables early isolation
  • Strangles: highly contagious bacterial; strict isolation + no horse movement until two clear cultures
  • EHM (neurological herpesvirus): mandatory reporting in most states; fever monitoring is key
  • Colic and laminitis are not infectious but are leading causes of equine death — both respond best to early intervention
