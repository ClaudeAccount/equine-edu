Key Concepts

What is the difference between preventive care and reactive care in equine health management?

Preventive care intercepts health problems before they develop: vaccination before disease exposure, deworming to control parasite burdens, dental care before pain causes weight loss, and annual wellness exams to establish baselines. Reactive care responds to problems that have already appeared. Preventive care is almost always less expensive, less disruptive, and more effective than treating conditions that have progressed to clinical illness.

What are the four AAEP core vaccines, and why are they classified as "core"?

Tetanus, Eastern/Western Equine Encephalomyelitis (EEE/WEE), West Nile Virus, and Rabies. These are classified as core because the diseases they prevent are either highly lethal, geographically widespread in North America, or zoonotic (transmissible to humans). Every horse in North America should receive all four annually, regardless of geography or use level.

How does strategic deworming differ from calendar-based deworming, and why does it matter?

Calendar-based deworming treats all horses on a fixed schedule regardless of actual parasite burden. Strategic deworming uses fecal egg counts (FECs) to identify which horses carry significant parasite loads — then treats only those horses. Strategic deworming reduces anthelmintic drug use overall, significantly slows the development of drug resistance, and focuses treatment where it has the most benefit (high shedders carrying 80% of the herd's egg burden).

What are the signs of colic, and why is early veterinary involvement important?

Colic signs include pawing, looking at the flank, rolling, elevated heart rate, lack of gut sounds, refusal of feed, and depressed attitude. The handler at the farm level cannot distinguish mild gas colic from a surgical emergency — and the difference in outcome between a strangulation caught early (correctable) and one caught late (not correctable) is often hours. Any colic that does not resolve within 20–30 minutes of initial signs warrants a veterinary call.

What is the purpose and protocol of biosecurity when a new horse arrives at a facility?

Biosecurity prevents the introduction of disease from an incoming horse (which may carry an incubating illness or be an asymptomatic carrier) to the resident herd. Protocol: isolate the new arrival from all resident horses for a minimum of 14–21 days; use dedicated equipment (buckets, brushes, lead ropes) for the isolated horse; monitor daily for fever, nasal discharge, or coughing; do not share airspace if possible. The isolation period must cover the incubation period of the diseases most likely to be introduced.

What is strangles, and what biosecurity response does an outbreak require?

Strangles is a highly contagious bacterial upper respiratory infection (Streptococcus equi subsp. equi) characterized by fever, nasal discharge, and abscessation of the submandibular and throatlatch lymph nodes. Outbreak biosecurity: immediate isolation of affected horses, dedicated equipment for each horse, cessation of all horse movement in or out of the facility, and testing by two consecutive negative cultures (typically guttural pouch lavage) 14 days apart before the outbreak is declared resolved. Carriers can shed bacteria for months to years after apparent recovery.

What is equine herpesvirus myeloencephalopathy (EHM), and why is temperature monitoring critical?

EHM is the neurological form of EHV-1 infection, caused by vasculitis within spinal cord and brain blood vessels. It produces progressive ataxia, hindlimb weakness, urinary incontinence, and potentially inability to rise. EHM outbreaks at horse shows and events are reportable events in most states. Temperature elevation typically precedes neurological signs by 6–8 days — identifying a fever and isolating the horse before neurological signs develop is the most effective intervention, because once EHM progresses to severe stages, the prognosis deteriorates significantly.

What is dental floating, why is it needed annually, and what signs indicate dental work is overdue?

Floating is the process of filing down the sharp enamel points that form on the outer edges of upper cheek teeth and inner edges of lower cheek teeth as horses chew. Without floating, these points lacerate the cheeks and tongue, causing pain that affects chewing efficiency, willingness to accept the bit, and weight maintenance. Annual floating is the standard. Signs that dental work is overdue include quidding (dropping partially chewed feed), tilting the head while chewing, unexplained weight loss, head tossing under saddle, difficulty on the bit, and foul-smelling breath.

What are the early signs of laminitis, and why is early intervention important?

Early laminitis signs: heat in the hooves (especially the toe region), a bounding digital pulse at the back of the pastern, and a characteristic weight-shifted stance where the horse leans backward to take weight off the affected feet. With prompt veterinary and farriery intervention, coffin bone rotation may be prevented or minimized. Delayed intervention allows the laminar connection between the coffin bone and hoof wall to deteriorate to the point where rotation or sinking (founder) becomes irreversible, causing permanent lameness or requiring euthanasia in severe cases.

What are the normal equine vital signs, and why does knowing baselines matter?

Temperature: 99–101°F. Pulse (heart rate): 28–44 beats per minute. Respiration: 12–20 breaths per minute. Gut sounds: present in all four quadrants. Capillary refill time: under 2 seconds. Knowing a specific horse's normal values — established during routine healthy observations — makes deviations recognizable immediately. A temperature of 102°F may be alarming for a horse normally running at 99.5°F, while a normally slightly elevated horse at 101.5°F tells a different story.

Key Vocabulary

Anthelmintic

A drug that kills internal parasites (worms). Common classes include macrocyclic lactones (ivermectin, moxidectin), benzimidazoles (fenbendazole, oxibendazole), and pyrimidines (pyrantel). Resistance to these drugs is increasing in small strongyle populations, which is why strategic deworming guided by fecal egg counts is the current standard of care.

Fecal egg count (FEC)

A laboratory test measuring the number of parasite eggs per gram of fresh feces. Results are used to classify horses as low shedders (<200 eggs/gram), moderate shedders (200–500 eggs/gram), or high shedders (>500 eggs/gram), guiding treatment decisions in a strategic deworming program.

FECRT (Fecal Egg Count Reduction Test)

A test of anthelmintic efficacy: fecal egg counts are compared before and 14 days after treatment. A reduction below 95% of the original count indicates resistance to the drug class used in that horse population on that specific farm. It is the only reliable method for confirming that a chosen dewormer is actually effective.

Coggins test

A blood test that detects antibodies to Equine Infectious Anemia (EIA) virus. EIA is a reportable disease with no vaccine or treatment. A negative (clean) Coggins test result is required for interstate transport and for entry to most events and boarding facilities. Positive horses must be permanently quarantined or euthanized.

Quidding

Dropping partially chewed feed from the mouth. A primary sign of dental pain — the horse forms a feed bolus but cannot apply enough pain-free pressure to chew it fully before releasing it. Horses that quid frequently are at risk for weight loss, nutritional deficiency, and choke (esophageal obstruction from inadequately chewed feed).

Hypsodont

A tooth type with an extended crown that erupts continuously throughout life as the grinding surfaces wear down. Horses have hypsodont cheek teeth — this is what makes equine dentistry a lifelong management requirement. The continuous eruption means horses can grind forage efficiently across a long lifespan, but also means they depend on regular dental care to maintain even wear patterns.

Capillary refill time (CRT)

The time it takes for the gum color to return after firm pressure is applied and released. Normal CRT is under 2 seconds (return to pink within 2 seconds). Prolonged CRT (>2 seconds) indicates compromised peripheral circulation — seen in shock, severe dehydration, and cardiovascular compromise. CRT is checked as part of colic assessment and emergency evaluations.

Laminitis

Inflammation and compromise of the sensitive laminae — the tissue that bonds the coffin bone (P3) to the inner hoof wall. Severe or chronic laminitis can result in rotation or vertical displacement (sinking) of the coffin bone within the hoof capsule. Causes include carbohydrate overload, systemic illness, Cushing's disease (PPID), and equine metabolic syndrome (EMS).

Common Mistakes

Deworming all horses on a fixed calendar schedule regardless of individual parasite burden

Calendar-based deworming treats horses that may not need treatment (low shedders) and misses the opportunity to concentrate resources on high shedders who are driving the majority of environmental contamination. More critically, treating all horses repeatedly with the same drug class selects strongly for drug-resistant parasite populations. Fecal egg count-guided strategic deworming has replaced calendar rotation as the evidence-based standard.

Adding more padding to fix a poorly fitting saddle is wrong — but a parallel mistake in health: returning a horse to work after influenza too soon

Returning a horse with equine influenza to work before full recovery — specifically before 5–7 days after the last sign of fever — significantly increases the risk of secondary bacterial bronchopneumonia. The respiratory epithelium damaged by the virus is vulnerable to bacterial superinfection during this recovery period. Many chronic respiratory problems in horses can be traced to inadequate rest during an influenza episode.

Assuming a horse eating and moving normally does not need a dental exam

Horses are prey animals and suppress signs of weakness, including chronic pain. Significant dental disease — sharp enamel points, hooks, uneven wear, infected teeth — can exist for months before the behavioral and physical signs become apparent. Annual dental exams find and address developing problems before they reach the stage of visible weight loss, quidding, or bit resistance. Waiting for signs means the problem has already been present for some time.

Treating colic at home without veterinary consultation

Walking a colicky horse to prevent rolling and self-injury while waiting for a veterinarian is appropriate management. Administering banamine (flunixin meglumine) to mask pain without veterinary guidance is not — pain relief delays recognition of a worsening surgical condition and may allow an owner to be falsely reassured that the horse is improving. Any colic that does not resolve within 20–30 minutes, involves repeated rolling, elevated heart rate above 50 bpm, or absence of gut sounds requires immediate veterinary contact.

Treating white hairs or coat changes in older horses as cosmetic without considering Cushing's disease

A long, wavy, curly coat that fails to shed normally in spring and summer is the hallmark sign of Cushing's disease (PPID) in horses. This is not a cosmetic issue — PPID horses are at significantly elevated risk for laminitis, increased susceptibility to infection, muscle wasting, and abnormal fat distribution. Early diagnosis and management with pergolide significantly reduces the risk of laminitis and improves quality of life. PPID should be suspected in any horse over 15 with abnormal coat shedding.

Horse Bowl Focus

What are the four AAEP core vaccines and their primary target diseases?

Tetanus (Clostridium tetani toxin; enters through wounds), EEE/WEE (mosquito-transmitted encephalomyelitis; EEE fatality rate ≈ 75–90%), West Nile Virus (mosquito-transmitted neurological disease; introduced to North America 1999), and Rabies (fatal zoonotic viral disease; annual, invariably fatal without vaccination). All four are recommended annually for every horse in North America.

What are the normal equine vital signs that should be memorized?

Temperature: 99–101°F (37.2–38.3°C). Pulse: 28–44 bpm. Respiration: 12–20 breaths per minute. Gut sounds: present in all four quadrants. Capillary refill time: under 2 seconds (pink gums). These are standard Horse Bowl questions in the veterinary science and health management categories.

What does a Coggins test detect, and what is the consequence of a positive result?

The Coggins test detects antibodies to Equine Infectious Anemia (EIA) virus. EIA is a reportable, incurable viral disease. Positive horses must be either humanely euthanized or permanently quarantined under federal law. A negative (clean) Coggins test is required for interstate transport, entry to most shows, sales, and boarding facilities.

What is floating equine teeth, and how often should it be performed?

Floating is the process of filing (rasping) sharp enamel points from the cheek teeth using a dental float (manual or motorized rasp). It is recommended annually for horses over three years old, and more frequently for horses under five or over fifteen. The procedure requires a full-mouth speculum and typically sedation for thorough examination of the entire dental arcade.

What is the transmission route and early sign of Equine Herpesvirus Myeloencephalopathy (EHM)?

EHM is the neurological form of EHV-1 infection. Transmission occurs through direct contact, respiratory secretions, and contaminated surfaces. Fever typically precedes neurological signs (ataxia, hindlimb weakness, urinary incontinence) by 6–8 days. EHM is mandatory reportable in most states. Daily temperature monitoring during high-exposure periods is the most effective early detection tool.

What are the FEC classifications, and what do they determine in a strategic deworming program?

Low shedders: <200 eggs per gram — monitored, dewormed seasonally. Moderate shedders: 200–500 epg — treated based on season and clinical judgment. High shedders: >500 epg — treated promptly. Approximately 20% of horses produce 80% of the eggs on pasture. Strategic programs target high shedders and reduce overall anthelmintic use, slowing resistance development.

What is the biosecurity isolation period for new horse arrivals, and what should be monitored?

Minimum 14–21 days of isolation from resident horses. During this period: monitor daily temperature (flag any elevation above 101°F), observe for nasal discharge, coughing, and diarrhea. Use dedicated equipment for the isolated horse. Do not share airspace with resident horses if possible. Resume contact with the herd only after the isolation period is completed without signs of illness.

What is strangles caused by, and what two cultures are required before ending facility lockdown?

Strangles is caused by Streptococcus equi subsp. equi. During an outbreak, no horses may enter or leave the facility until two consecutive negative cultures (typically guttural pouch lavage PCR or culture, taken 14 days apart) confirm resolution. Long-term carriers — horses that appear recovered but shed bacteria from their guttural pouches — are a common source of recurrence if not properly tested before reintegrating with the herd.
