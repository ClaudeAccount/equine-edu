Equine Health & Disease Prevention

Vaccination Programs Programs

Core and risk-based equine vaccines: what they protect against, recommended schedules, and record-keeping.

Vaccination is the most cost-effective preventive care measure in equine medicine. Vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce disease-specific antibodies, providing protection against infections that can be fatal or cause permanent damage. Understanding the difference between core and risk-based vaccines, the diseases they prevent, and the schedule that provides optimal protection is foundational to building a preventive care program.

Equine vaccination recommendations are developed by the American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) and updated regularly based on current disease prevalence and vaccine efficacy data. The AAEP divides equine vaccines into core vaccines — recommended for all horses regardless of geography or use — and risk-based vaccines — recommended based on geographic location, travel frequency, and exposure risk.

Equine Vaccination Categories

Eastern & Western Equine Encephalomyelitis (EEE/WEE)

EEE and WEE are mosquito-transmitted viral diseases of the central nervous system. EEE carries a fatality rate of approximately 75–90% in affected horses. WEE is somewhat less lethal but still serious. Annual vaccination before mosquito season is recommended universally. In areas with year-round mosquito activity, semi-annual vaccination may be appropriate.

West Nile Virus

West Nile Virus is another mosquito-transmitted neurological disease. Since its introduction to North America in 1999, it has caused thousands of equine fatalities. Annual vaccination is recommended; in high-mosquito regions, a spring booster followed by a late summer booster provides stronger seasonal coverage.

Tetanus

Tetanus is caused by the toxin of Clostridium tetani, a soil bacterium that enters through wounds. Horses are particularly susceptible — more so than most other domestic species. The fatality rate in unvaccinated horses with clinical tetanus approaches 80%. Annual vaccination is the standard; additional boosters are recommended after wounds, surgeries, or foaling.

Influenza & Rhinopneumonitis

Equine influenza (EIV) and equine herpesvirus (EHV, rhinopneumonitis) are highly contagious respiratory and neurological diseases. Vaccination is recommended every 6 months for horses that travel to competitions, shows, or clinics. These vaccines provide shorter-lived immunity than core vaccines and require more frequent administration in horses with regular exposure risk.

Vaccination records are a management tool, not just documentation. Knowing when each vaccine was last administered, which vaccine brand was used, and whether a booster is due prevents both under-vaccination (disease risk) and over-vaccination (unnecessary cost and immune stimulation). Vaccination history is also required for entry to most horse shows, sales, and boarding facilities.

Rabies vaccination is classified as a core vaccine by the AAEP. Though horses do not frequently contract rabies, the disease is invariably fatal and has zoonotic implications — it can be transmitted from horse to human through handling an affected animal. Rabies vaccination requires only annual administration and provides protection against a condition that is neither treatable nor survivable once clinical signs appear.

Things to Remember

  • Core vaccines: EEE, WEE, West Nile, Tetanus, and Rabies — recommended annually for all horses
  • EEE fatality rate ≈ 75–90% in affected horses — one of the most lethal preventable equine diseases
  • Influenza and EHV: risk-based, every 6 months for horses competing or traveling
  • Rabies: core vaccine, annual, fatal and zoonotic — not frequently seen but not treatable if contracted
