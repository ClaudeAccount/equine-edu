Equine Health & Disease Prevention

Vaccination Programs

Core and risk-based equine vaccines: what they protect against, recommended schedules, and record-keeping.

Vaccination is the most cost-effective preventive care measure in equine medicine. Vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce disease-specific antibodies, providing protection against infections that can be fatal or cause permanent damage. Understanding the difference between core and risk-based vaccines, the diseases they prevent, and the schedule that provides optimal protection is foundational to building a preventive care program.

Equine vaccination recommendations are developed by the American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) and updated regularly based on current disease prevalence and vaccine efficacy data. The AAEP divides equine vaccines into core vaccines — recommended for all horses regardless of geography or use — and risk-based vaccines — recommended based on geographic location, travel frequency, and exposure risk.

Eastern and Western Equine Encephalomyelitis are the most lethal vaccine-preventable diseases in North American horses. Both are transmitted by mosquitoes and cause devastating neurological disease.

EEE and WEE are caused by alphaviruses transmitted by mosquitoes that have fed on infected birds. Both diseases target the central nervous system, producing progressive neurological signs including stumbling, incoordination, depression, and inability to swallow. EEE carries a case fatality rate of approximately 75–90% in affected horses — most horses that develop clinical signs do not survive. WEE is somewhat less lethal but still causes high morbidity.

Annual vaccination before mosquito season is the universal standard. Because no treatment reverses neurological damage once clinical signs appear, prevention is the only meaningful intervention. In warm climates where mosquitoes are active year-round, semi-annual vaccination may be recommended. The vaccines are typically combined in a single product (EWT — encephalomyelitis, West Nile, tetanus), making administration efficient.

Horses that have never been vaccinated require a two-dose primary series 3–4 weeks apart before annual boosters are sufficient. Annual boosters given in late spring, before peak mosquito season, provide maximum seasonal protection. Vaccination certificates may be required for horses entering equine events in affected regions.

EEE carries a 75–90% fatality rate — vaccination is not optional for horses in mosquito-active environments. Annual spring vaccination before mosquito season is the single most effective step toward preventing this disease.

West Nile Virus arrived in North America in 1999 and rapidly became one of the most important mosquito-borne neurological diseases of horses. Vaccination is a core requirement for all horses.

West Nile Virus (WNV) is an arbovirus transmitted by Culex mosquitoes that have fed on infected birds. It was introduced to North America through New York in 1999 and spread across the continent within four years, causing thousands of equine deaths annually before effective vaccines became widely available. Horses are considered a dead-end host — they do not transmit the virus to other horses or to humans directly.

Clinical signs include muscle weakness, stumbling, incoordination, facial tremors, and difficulty swallowing. The mortality rate in unvaccinated horses with clinical WNV is approximately 30–40%, and survivors often retain residual neurological deficits. Early veterinary intervention — supportive care including anti-inflammatories, fluids, and nutritional support — improves outcomes but cannot reverse established neurological damage.

Annual vaccination is recommended, timed before peak mosquito season. In regions with extended warm seasons and year-round mosquito activity (Texas, Florida, Southern California), a spring prime and late-summer booster provides stronger seasonal coverage. The vaccine is typically combined with EEE/WEE and tetanus in a single product for convenience.

West Nile Virus is not transmitted between horses — but it spreads rapidly through a mosquito population once established in local birds. Annual vaccination is the primary defense because environmental control of mosquitoes is not sufficient to prevent horse exposure.

Tetanus is ubiquitous in soil and manure — and horses are uniquely susceptible to it. Annual vaccination is non-negotiable, and additional boosters are required after wounds, surgeries, or foaling.

Tetanus is caused by the potent neurotoxin produced by Clostridium tetani, an anaerobic bacterium found in soil, manure, and the intestinal tracts of animals worldwide. The bacteria enter through wounds — including punctures, lacerations, surgical incisions, and the umbilical stump of newborn foals. Deep, narrow puncture wounds are particularly dangerous because the anaerobic environment within the wound allows bacterial proliferation and toxin production.

Horses are more susceptible to tetanus than most other domestic species. The neurotoxin (tetanospasmin) binds to the nervous system and cannot be neutralized once bound. Clinical signs progress from muscle stiffness, a characteristic stiff-legged gait ("sawhorse stance"), and difficulty opening the jaw ("lockjaw") to hypersensitivity to noise and light, and eventually respiratory failure. The fatality rate in unvaccinated horses with clinical tetanus approaches 80%.

Annual vaccination maintains protective immunity. Additional boosters are recommended within 24 hours of any significant wound or surgical procedure, and at foaling in mares (to provide passive immunity to the foal through colostrum). The tetanus toxoid is highly effective and has one of the best safety profiles of all equine vaccines.

Tetanus toxoid is the most important single vaccine in equine medicine. Annual vaccination is standard; any wound, surgery, or foaling event triggers a booster recommendation. A horse that has not been vaccinated within the year of a wound event should receive both tetanus toxoid and tetanus antitoxin.

Equine influenza and equine herpesvirus are the most common infectious respiratory diseases at horse shows, sales, and training facilities. Both are risk-based vaccines that require more frequent administration than core vaccines.

Equine influenza (EIV) is caused by influenza A virus strains and spreads rapidly through aerosolized respiratory droplets from coughing horses. The virus mutates frequently — requiring vaccine manufacturers to update formulations regularly — and can cause explosive outbreaks at events where many horses from different facilities are assembled. Signs include sudden-onset fever (up to 106°F), dry harsh cough, nasal discharge, and profound lethargy. Rest for a minimum of 7 days for every day of fever is the standard treatment guideline.

Equine herpesvirus (EHV), also called rhinopneumonitis, exists in multiple strains with distinct disease manifestations. EHV-1 and EHV-4 cause respiratory disease; EHV-1 additionally causes late-term abortion in mares, neurological disease (equine herpesvirus myeloencephalopathy, EHM), and neonatal foal illness. EHM outbreaks have occurred with sufficient frequency at shows and events that most states now require mandatory reporting of suspected cases.

Both vaccines are classified as risk-based because horses with no exposure to other horses may not need the vaccination frequency required for horses that travel regularly to events. For horses that compete or travel, the AAEP recommends vaccination every 6 months. The vaccines do not prevent infection entirely but reduce severity and duration of illness.

Influenza and EHV vaccines provide shorter-lived immunity than core vaccines — every 6 months for horses in regular contact with horses from other facilities. Vaccination timing should be coordinated with the show calendar: boostering 2–4 weeks before a high-exposure event provides the strongest immune response at peak risk.

In the Barn

Rabies vaccination, though less commonly discussed, is also classified as a core vaccine by the AAEP. Though horses do not frequently contract rabies, the disease is invariably fatal and carries zoonotic risk — it can be transmitted from horse to human through handling an affected animal before the diagnosis is confirmed. Annual rabies vaccination is a low-cost, high-protection measure that every horse should receive as part of the core vaccination series. Vaccination certificates — records of which vaccines were given, when, and by whom — are required for entry to most boarding facilities, events, and sales. Keeping accurate vaccination records in the health file is as much a practical management requirement as it is documentation.

Things to Remember

  • Core vaccines: EEE, WEE, West Nile, Tetanus, and Rabies — recommended annually for all horses
  • EEE fatality rate ≈ 75–90% in affected horses — one of the most lethal preventable equine diseases
  • Influenza and EHV: risk-based, every 6 months for horses competing or traveling
  • Tetanus booster is required after any wound, surgery, or foaling — not just annually
