Equine Health & Disease Prevention

Dental Care & Oral Health Oral Health

Why annual dental care is essential, equine dental anatomy, and how dental problems affect behavior and nutrition.

Dental care is the most frequently neglected component of equine preventive care — and one of the most consequential. Horses with untreated dental problems experience chronic pain that affects behavior, resistance to the bit, weight loss, and reduced feed utilization. Because horses are prey animals that conceal signs of weakness, dental pain often goes unrecognized until it is well-advanced.

Horses have hypsodont teeth — high-crowned teeth that erupt continuously throughout the horse's life as the grinding surfaces wear down. This eruption mechanism makes horses uniquely dependent on a chewing motion that produces even, symmetrical wear. Abnormalities in feed, chewing pattern, or tooth alignment produce sharp points, hooks, and uneven surfaces that cause pain and reduce feed utilization.

Equine Dental Anatomy & Management
Anatomy 1

Dental Formula and Eruption

Adult horses have 36 to 44 permanent teeth depending on sex (mares typically have fewer). Incisors cut forage; premolars and molars grind it. Wolf teeth (first premolars) are vestigial teeth that can interfere with bit contact and are routinely removed. Canine teeth are present in most male horses and some mares. Deciduous (baby) teeth begin shedding between ages 2.5 and 4.5 as permanent teeth erupt — this process can cause temporary discomfort and training challenges.

Anatomy 2

Sharp Points and Their Effects

As horses chew, uneven wear produces sharp enamel points on the outer edges of the upper cheek teeth and the inner edges of the lower cheek teeth. These points lacerate the cheeks and tongue, causing pain that affects chewing efficiency, behavioral resistance to the bit, head tossing, and difficulty with collection. Annual dental floating (filing) removes these points and restores correct chewing surfaces.

Anatomy 3

Signs of Dental Problems

Behavioral signs include: dropping partially chewed feed (quidding), tilting the head to one side while chewing, preferring one side of the bit, head tossing under saddle, reluctance to accept contact, difficulty with lateral flexion, and unexplained weight loss despite adequate feed. Physical signs include: foul-smelling breath, undigested grain in the manure, nasal discharge on one side (indicating a dental sinus fistula), and facial swelling.

Anatomy 4

Senior Horse Dental Needs

As horses age past 20, teeth may become worn to the root, resulting in loss of grinding efficiency and ultimately tooth loss. Senior horses may require hay soaked to soften it, hay cubes soaked in water, or transition to complete feeds that provide nutrition without requiring extensive chewing. Annual dental exams are especially important in senior horses — the condition of their dentition directly determines what feeds they can utilize.

Annual dental examination by a veterinarian or veterinary dental specialist is recommended for all horses over age three. Horses under three should be examined as permanent teeth erupt, as this period involves significant dental change. Waiting for behavioral or weight-loss signs before scheduling a dental exam means the problem has been present — and causing pain — for some time before it was detected.

Equine dentistry requires specialized equipment — a full-mouth speculum, adequate lighting, motorized or manual dental floats — and the ability to sedate the horse for a thorough oral examination. Hand floating (filing) without sedation and without a full-mouth speculum does not allow adequate visualization of the entire arcade and cannot address problems in the caudal (back) cheek teeth. A complete dental examination includes the entire arcade, all surfaces.

In the Barn

Most horses require dental floating once yearly, though horses under five and over fifteen may need twice-yearly attention. Signs that dental work is overdue include difficulty chewing, dropping grain, weight loss without appetite changes, head tossing under saddle, and quidding (dropping partially chewed hay). A horse that has not had dental work in over a year should be assumed to have sharp enamel points and should be scheduled accordingly.

Things to Remember

  • Annual dental exam recommended for all horses >3 years — earlier if behavioral or weight issues appear
  • Sharp enamel points cause cheek and tongue lacerations → pain → bit resistance, head tossing, weight loss
  • Quidding (dropping partially chewed feed) is the most visible sign of active dental pain
  • Senior horses: tooth loss changes what they can eat — soaked hay/cubes or complete feeds may be required
